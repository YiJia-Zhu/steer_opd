Abstract
On-policy distillation (OPD) is a standard tool for transferring teacher behavior to a smaller student, but it implicitly assumes that teacher and student predictions are comparable token by token, an assumption that fails whenever the two models tokenize the same text differently.
Under heterogeneous tokenizers, exact shared-token matching silently discards a large fraction of the teacher signal at precisely the positions where vocabularies disagree.
We propose Simple Cross-Tokenizer OPD (SimCT), which restores this signal by enlarging the supervision space: alongside shared tokens, SimCT compares teacher and student over short multi-token continuations that both tokenizers can realize, leaving the OPD loss form itself unchanged.
We show that these units form the finest interface consistent with the two fixed tokenizations, and that coarser alternatives remove teacher-student distinctions that are useful for on-policy learning.
Across three heterogeneous teacher-student pairs on mathematical reasoning and code-generation benchmarks, SimCT shows consistent gains over shared-vocabulary OPD and representative cross-tokenizer baselines, with ablations supporting the benefit of recovering supervision discarded by exact shared-token matching.
Code is available at https://github.com/sunjie279/SimCT-.

1 Introduction
Knowledge distillation transfers the behavior of a strong teacher to a smaller student and remains central to efficient LLM deployment [1, 2, 3, 4].
For autoregressive LLMs, however, conventional offline knowledge distillation trains the student on fixed contexts rather than on states induced by its own generations [5, 6], so early generation errors can compound and move the student into contexts that were rarely supervised during training [7, 8, 5, 9].
On-policy distillation (OPD) addresses this train–test mismatch by querying the teacher on prefixes sampled from the student’s policy, following the broader principle of on-policy imitation learning [10, 11, 12, 13, 14].
This makes OPD a natural paradigm for LLM distillation [15, 16, 17, 18, 19], but it also exposes a hidden requirement for cross-model supervision: at each student-generated prefix, teacher and student predictions must be defined over comparable prediction units.
This requirement is non-trivial in realistic LLM distillation, where teacher and student models come from different families and use different tokenizers [20].
The mismatch arises at two levels.
First, at the vocabulary level: as shown in Figure 1(A), modern LLM tokenizers share only a limited portion of their vocabularies across model families, so restricting supervision to tokens that appear in both vocabularies excludes teacher predictions outside this intersection.
Second, at the sequence level: the same text may be segmented into different token sequences, so the teacher’s next-token distribution at a student-generated prefix may not supervise the same text unit as the student’s next-token distribution [21, 22, 23, 24, 25].
As illustrated in Figure 1(B), this sequence mismatch breaks a basic assumption of standard OPD, namely that teacher and student predict over the same text unit at every supervised position.

(A) LLM tokenizers often share only partial vocabularies across model families, making shared-token distillation restrictive.
(B) The same text can induce different token boundaries and prediction spaces, so standard token-level OPD is ill-defined.

Existing cross-tokenizer distillation methods use several kinds of supervision interfaces.
Distribution-alignment methods compare heterogeneous logits through optimal transport [21, 26], while GOLD adapts distribution alignment to on-policy training through probability merging and a hybrid vocabulary loss [27].
Representation-transfer methods use learned cross-model projections [22, 28], introducing additional trainable parameters.
Other interfaces use chunk likelihoods [29], BPE structure [25], dynamic vocabulary mapping [30], or byte-level decoders [24].
These approaches differ in granularity, cost, and training setting.
Our focus is a parameter-free interface that recovers supervision on student-generated prefixes using the existing tokenizer boundaries.
This raises a natural question: how simple can the supervision interface be while still recovering most of the teacher signal that tokenizer mismatch would otherwise discard?
We propose Simple Cross-Tokenizer OPD (SimCT), a cross-tokenizer OPD method that changes the supervision space rather than the OPD objective.
Instead of forcing teacher and student logits to match only on identical tokens, SimCT induces a normalized preference distribution over a common supervision space and applies the standard reverse-KL OPD loss there [12, 31].
The space contains shared tokens and minimal aligned units recovered from tokenizer-mismatched text.
A minimal aligned unit is the shortest non-empty span bounded by synchronized boundaries of the two fixed tokenizations, even when their token sequences differ.
SimCT requires no additional parameters, no learned projections, and no transport computation, since the supervision space is constructed directly from the two existing tokenizers. It recovers teacher signal that shared-vocabulary OPD discards. SimCT and SimpleOPD share the reverse-KL loss form; SimCT additionally uses a normalization safeguard specified in Appendix C.2. The ablations examine the benefit of the recovered supervision within this training design.
As previewed in Figure 1(C), this supervision-space expansion substantially improves over existing cross-tokenizer OPD baselines.
We evaluate SimCT across three model families on mathematical reasoning and code-generation benchmarks.
SimCT consistently improves over SFT and representative cross-tokenizer OPD baselines, achieving an average relative gain of 6.0% over SFT with modest additional computational cost relative to shared-vocabulary OPD.
Ablations further explain where the gains come from: increasing recovered tokenizer-mismatched text supervision improves performance, whereas coarsening minimal aligned units removes KL supervision signal and reduces downstream gains.
These trends support our theoretical analysis, which characterizes SimCT as the finest boundary-consistent supervision interface jointly expressible by the teacher and student tokenizers, and shows that coarser interfaces can erase useful within-text teacher–student distinctions.
Overall, our results identify supervision-space construction as a key design choice in cross-tokenizer OPD, with minimal aligned units offering a simple way to recover teacher supervision otherwise lost under exact token overlap.
2 Related Work
Knowledge distillation and on-policy distillation.
Knowledge distillation transfers teacher behavior to a smaller student and has become a standard tool for efficient LLM deployment [1, 3], with early work treating it as offline compression on fixed corpora [32, 33, 6].
Because fixed training contexts can differ from the prefixes the student visits at inference [7, 5], on-policy distillation (OPD) instead queries the teacher on student-generated prefixes [34, 11], while related distillation methods refine the objective and data use [12, 13, 14, 15, 35, 36, 37].
A complementary line analyzes when and where OPD provides signal: distributional compatibility governs success or failure at the sequence level [18], while token-level studies show that supervision quality varies sharply across positions [38, 39].
Recent OPD methods also refine distinct components of the supervision pipeline.
TrustMOPD allocates token-level supervision among specialist teachers using label-free reliability estimates [40]; influence-directed distillation controls the effect of updates on sampled-token probabilities [41], and SOD adapts supervision at the reasoning-step level [42].
Guided-OPD changes the rollout distribution through curriculum-scheduled teacher guidance in multi-turn interactions [43], whereas Data-Free OPD generates its own training questions to reduce dependence on external prompt corpora [44].
EasyOPD provides modular infrastructure for studying supervision forms, tokenizer compatibility, and optimization granularity [45].
These directions address teacher selection, signal weighting, rollout construction, data sourcing, and implementation. SimCT instead addresses the comparison units needed when tokenizers differ. These complementary design choices suggest extensions of cross-tokenizer OPD.
Cross-tokenizer knowledge distillation.
When teacher and student tokenize the same text differently, prior work bridges the vocabulary gap at one of several interfaces.
Optimal-transport methods align heterogeneous logits directly, either between token distributions [21], jointly at token and sequence levels [26], or layer-wise with chain-of-thought augmentation [46].
Dual-space and representation-matching methods instead unify the output spaces themselves through cross-model attention and its variants [22, 47].
Other methods construct comparison interfaces through approximate chunk likelihoods [29], doing context-aware sequence matching with dynamic vocabulary mapping [30], or using bytes as a tokenizer-agnostic interface [24], while related directions reuse the teacher loss as supervision [48], extend cross-tokenizer transfer to preference alignment [23], or bridge tokenizer gaps in embedding-model representation space [49].
3 Methodology
3.1 Problem Setup
Let 𝒯\mathcal{T} and 𝒮\mathcal{S} denote the teacher and student models, with vocabularies 𝒱T\mathcal{V}_{T} and 𝒱S\mathcal{V}_{S}. In on-policy distillation, supervision is provided on prefixes sampled from the student policy rather than a fixed offline dataset. At step tt, let x<tx_{<t} denote the student-generated prefix. Both models are conditioned on the same text-level context x<tx_{<t}, but they predict the next token over their own vocabularies:
pT(⋅∣x<t)∈Δ(𝒱T),pS(⋅∣x<t)∈Δ(𝒱S).p_{T}(\cdot\mid x_{<t})\in\Delta(\mathcal{V}_{T}),\qquad p_{S}(\cdot\mid x_{<t})\in\Delta(\mathcal{V}_{S}).
(1)
Here, pT​(v∣x<t)p_{T}(v\mid x_{<t}) is the probability the teacher assigns to token v∈𝒱Tv\in\mathcal{V}_{T}, and pS​(u∣x<t)p_{S}(u\mid x_{<t}) is defined analogously for student token u∈𝒱Su\in\mathcal{V}_{S}. We write Δ⁡(𝒱)\Delta(\mathcal{V}) for the probability simplex over 𝒱\mathcal{V}.
When teacher and student share a tokenizer, the two distributions in Eq. (1) are defined over a common prediction space, and standard OPD compares them directly, e.g., by minimizing a token-level KL:
ℒOPD(x<t)=KL(pS(⋅∣x<t)∥pT(⋅∣x<t)).\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPD}}(x_{<t})=\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_{S}(\cdot\mid x_{<t})\,\|\,p_{T}(\cdot\mid x_{<t})\right).
(2)
This objective is well defined only because each student token corresponds to the same prediction unit as the corresponding teacher token. Thus, standard OPD implicitly assumes that teacher and student probabilities live on the same simplex and can be compared token by token.
Cross-tokenizer OPD breaks this assumption in two coupled ways. First, the vocabularies differ, 𝒱T≠𝒱S\mathcal{V}_{T}\neq\mathcal{V}_{S}, so the teacher and student distributions in Eq. (1) are supported on different probability spaces. Second, the same text may be segmented into different token sequences, so textually equivalent texts do not necessarily admit a one-to-one token alignment. We refer to these obstacles as vocabulary mismatch and sequence mismatch, respectively. The goal of cross-tokenizer OPD is to construct a common supervision interface in which teacher feedback remains comparable to the student’s next-token prediction on student-generated prefixes.
3.2 Generalized OPD over a Common Supervision Space
Cross-tokenizer OPD requires comparing two next-token distributions that are defined over different vocabularies and may correspond to different text segmentations.
We handle this by replacing the shared-vocabulary assumption with a shared set of prediction units. Figure 2(A) sketches the next-token form of this interface; the formulation below extends scoring to multi-token candidates.
Specifically, we introduce a common supervision space 𝒰\mathcal{U} and scoring maps. Let 𝒫M(h)={pM(⋅∣h,z):z∈𝒱M∗}\mathcal{P}_{M}(h)=\{p_{M}(\cdot\mid h,z):z\in\mathcal{V}_{M}^{*}\} denote model MM’s autoregressive conditional family after prefix hh, including the empty continuation zz. The maps act on this family:
ΠT:𝒫T(h)↦qT(⋅∣h)∈Δ(𝒰),ΠS:𝒫S(h)↦qS(⋅∣h)∈Δ(𝒰),\Pi_{T}:\mathcal{P}_{T}(h)\mapsto q_{T}(\cdot\mid h)\in\Delta(\mathcal{U}),\qquad\Pi_{S}:\mathcal{P}_{S}(h)\mapsto q_{S}(\cdot\mid h)\in\Delta(\mathcal{U}),
(3)
with the candidate set held fixed when scoring. For multi-token units, access to conditional probabilities inside each candidate is required; a single next-token probability vector is insufficient.
Given a student-generated prefix x<tx_{<t}, these maps induce comparable distributions
qT(⋅∣x<t)=ΠT(𝒫T(x<t)),qS(⋅∣x<t)=ΠS(𝒫S(x<t)).q_{T}(\cdot\mid x_{<t})=\Pi_{T}\!\left(\mathcal{P}_{T}(x_{<t})\right),\qquad q_{S}(\cdot\mid x_{<t})=\Pi_{S}\!\left(\mathcal{P}_{S}(x_{<t})\right).
(4)
Distillation can then be performed directly in 𝒰\mathcal{U}:
ℒgOPD(x<t)=𝒟(qS(⋅∣x<t),qT(⋅∣x<t)),\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{gOPD}}(x_{<t})=\mathcal{D}\!\left(q_{S}(\cdot\mid x_{<t}),q_{T}(\cdot\mid x_{<t})\right),
(5)
where 𝒟\mathcal{D} denotes a distillation divergence over the common supervision space.
This formulation recovers standard OPD as a special case.
When teacher and student share a tokenizer, we can set 𝒰=𝒱T=𝒱S\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{V}_{T}=\mathcal{V}_{S} and ΠM(𝒫M(h))=pM(⋅∣h)\Pi_{M}(\mathcal{P}_{M}(h))=p_{M}(\cdot\mid h), reducing Eq. (5) to the token-level objective in Eq. (2).
In the cross-tokenizer setting, however, the central design choice is how to construct 𝒰\mathcal{U} so that it preserves useful teacher supervision while remaining compatible with student predictions.
A direct baseline is to use only the shared vocabulary
𝒰shared=𝒱T∩𝒱S\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{shared}}=\mathcal{V}_{T}\cap\mathcal{V}_{S},
and restrict both distributions to this subspace.
We refer to this baseline as SimpleOPD.
SimpleOPD is a natural but conservative instantiation of Eq. (5): it avoids comparing tokens that are not shared by both vocabularies,
but it discards supervision signal from texts that are semantically comparable yet represented by different token segments.
Thus, SimpleOPD fails not because generalized OPD is inadequate, but because exact token overlap defines an overly narrow supervision space.
Figure 2: 
Framework of SimCT.
(A) Teacher and student distributions are mapped to a common supervision space 𝒰\mathcal{U} under the same student-generated prefix. The panel shows the next-token form of the interface; for multi-token candidates, Eqs. (3)–(4) define scoring through the autoregressive conditional families.
(B) SimCT builds 𝒰SimCT=(𝒱T∩𝒱S)∪𝒜\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{SimCT}}=(\mathcal{V}_{T}\cap\mathcal{V}_{S})\cup\mathcal{A} by adding minimal aligned units 𝒜\mathcal{A}, then applies the original OPD loss on the induced supervision distributions.
3.3 SimCT: Recovering Supervision via Minimal Aligned Units
To expand the common supervision space that goes beyond exact token overlap, we propose Simple Cross-Tokenizer OPD (SimCT).
Its central principle is to recover teacher supervision whenever teacher and student predictions refer to the same underlying text, even if the two tokenizers realize that text with different token boundaries.
Figure 2(B) gives a simple example: the teacher tokenizes “happy” as “hap”, “py”, while the student tokenizes the same text as “ha”, “pp”, “y”.
Although not comparable as individual tokens, these refer to the same text, so SimCT treats it as a recoverable supervision unit.
To construct these units, SimCT considers texts where the two tokenizations disagree and forms the finest boundary-consistent text units realizable by both tokenizers.
We call these units minimal aligned units (MAU), and denote the set of all such units by 𝒜\mathcal{A}.
SimCT then defines its supervision space as
𝒰SimCT=(𝒱T∩𝒱S)∪𝒜.\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{SimCT}}=(\mathcal{V}_{T}\cap\mathcal{V}_{S})\cup\mathcal{A}.
(6)
The shared-vocabulary term keeps supervision that is already comparable on 1:1-aligned shared tokens, while 𝒜\mathcal{A} recovers supervision from texts that tokenize differently but are comparable after mapping to common aligned units.
We now define the SimCT scoring maps ΠMSimCT\Pi_{M}^{\mathrm{SimCT}} for M∈{𝒯,𝒮}M\in\{\mathcal{T},\mathcal{S}\}.
SimCT first assigns each unit u∈𝒰SimCTu\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{SimCT}} a continuation score and then normalizes over the candidate supervision set.
For a 1:1-aligned shared token u∈𝒱T∩𝒱Su\in\mathcal{V}_{T}\cap\mathcal{V}_{S}, we use the original next-token log-probability,
sM​(u∣x<t)=log⁡pM​(u∣x<t)s_{M}(u\mid x_{<t})=\log p_{M}(u\mid x_{<t}).
For a minimal aligned unit u∈𝒜u\in\mathcal{A}, let τM​(u)=(v1,…,vk)\tau_{M}(u)=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}) be its tokenizer-MM realization, and define
sM​(u∣x<t)=1k​log⁡pM​(u∣x<t)=1k​∑j=1klog⁡pM​(vj∣x<t,v<j).s_{M}(u\mid x_{<t})=\frac{1}{k}\log p_{M}(u\mid x_{<t})=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\log p_{M}(v_{j}\mid x_{<t},v_{<j}).
(7)
The factor 1/k1/k uses the average log-likelihood rather than the raw sequence log-likelihood, avoiding systematically penalizing units that require more tokens under a given tokenizer [50]. This normalization is a standard practical choice, not a requirement of the theoretical construction in §3.4.
The normalized supervision distribution over the candidate set is then
qMSimCT​(u∣x<t)=exp⁡(sM​(u∣x<t))∑u′∈𝒰SimCTexp⁡(sM​(u′∣x<t)),q_{M}^{\mathrm{SimCT}}(u\mid x_{<t})=\frac{\exp(s_{M}(u\mid x_{<t}))}{\sum_{u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{SimCT}}}\exp(s_{M}(u^{\prime}\mid x_{<t}))},
(8)
which we denote as qMSimCT=ΠMSimCT​(𝒫M)q_{M}^{\mathrm{SimCT}}=\Pi_{M}^{\mathrm{SimCT}}(\mathcal{P}_{M}).
Equation (8) defines a distribution over the finite candidate supervision set. Its probabilities are obtained by normalizing continuation scores: shared tokens retain their next-token log-probabilities, while multi-token MAUs use model-specific length-normalized continuation likelihoods. This candidate-score normalization and the original model’s next-token probability measure operate on distinct spaces.
Among retained shared tokens, the common normalizer preserves probability ratios. Multi-token MAU scores depend on the conditional factors and token counts of each model’s realization; Appendix details these scoring properties.
The SimCT loss is the generalized OPD loss instantiated on this recovered supervision space:
ℒSimCT(x<t)=𝒟(qSSimCT(⋅∣x<t),qTSimCT(⋅∣x<t)).\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SimCT}}(x_{<t})=\mathcal{D}\!\left(q_{S}^{\mathrm{SimCT}}(\cdot\mid x_{<t}),q_{T}^{\mathrm{SimCT}}(\cdot\mid x_{<t})\right).
(9)
SimCT performs OPD over the next continuation units rather than tokenizer-specific next tokens.
For a multi-token unit, Eq. (7) scores the candidate continuation under the current prefix x<tx_{<t} through its autoregressive factors pM​(vj∣x<t,v<j)p_{M}(v_{j}\mid x_{<t},v_{<j}), using only the prefix and the candidate’s preceding tokens as conditioning. Candidate construction can use the already sampled student rollout, as in Algorithm 1; it does not require an external ground-truth continuation.
Thus, SimCT preserves the on-policy training form while changing the supervision interface from exact token overlap to aligned continuation units.
For all main SimCT experiments, we mask positions where the reciprocal of the teacher score normalizer in Eq. (8) exceeds 22; implementation details are in Appendix C.2. The procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1.
3.4 Properties of the SimCT Supervision Space
We present two theoretical results that clarify the design of the SimCT supervision space.
First, minimal aligned units provide the finest comparable text units consistent with the two fixed tokenizations.
Second, merging these units into coarser supervision units can remove teacher–student distinctions that are visible at the minimal-unit level.
Formal definitions, assumptions, and proofs are provided in Appendix E.
Theorem 1 (Minimal aligned units).
For a text region with fixed teacher and student tokenizations, SimCT constructs a partition into minimal aligned units.
Each unit is a union of consecutive complete tokens under both fixed tokenizations.
No unit admits a strictly finer non-empty decomposition with this property.
Theorem 1 shows that SimCT constructs the finest boundary-consistent interface on which teacher and student predictions can be compared, making the supervision space more expressive than exact shared-token matching while avoiding unconstrained character-level comparison.
Proposition 1 (Signal loss under coarser supervision spaces).
For a fixed student-generated prefix, let qTminq_{T}^{\min} and qSminq_{S}^{\min} be teacher and student distributions over SimCT minimal aligned units, with qTmin​(u)>0q_{T}^{\min}(u)>0 whenever qSmin​(u)>0q_{S}^{\min}(u)>0.
Let qT𝒞q_{T}^{\mathcal{C}} and qS𝒞q_{S}^{\mathcal{C}} be the distributions obtained by a non-trivial coarsening 𝒞\mathcal{C}, which sums probabilities inside each coarse unit.
Then
KL(qSmin∥qTmin)≥KL(qS𝒞∥qT𝒞).\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q_{S}^{\min}\,\|\,q_{T}^{\min}\right)\geq\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q_{S}^{\mathcal{C}}\,\|\,q_{T}^{\mathcal{C}}\right).
(10)
The gap is exactly the within-unit teacher–student discrepancy removed by the coarsening.
Proposition 1 motivates SimCT to preserve minimal aligned units rather than coarser units. In its idealized distributional setting, coarsening removes the within-unit teacher–student discrepancy that provides a fine-grained distillation signal; Section 4.4 measures this erased signal by the KL gap over student-generated prefixes. Together with Theorem 1, this supports using the finest tokenizer-compatible supervision units. The practical SimCT objective in Eq. (8) implements this principle with normalized continuation scores over a finite candidate set. The KL result applies to distributions coarsened by summing probabilities within fixed groups. Concatenating strings and recomputing continuation scores defines a separate transformation.
4 Experiments
4.1 Experimental Setup
Models and pairs.
We construct three teacher–student pairs that span all three model families and tokenizers: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct [51] →\to Phi-4-mini-Instruct [52], Qwen →\to Gemma-2-2B-IT [53], and Phi →\to Gemma.
All pairs follow the realistic large-to-small distillation direction. Two distinct models (Qwen and Phi) serve as teachers and two distinct models (Phi and Gemma) serve as students across pairs, so our results do not hinge on any single teacher or student choice.
Data and warm-start.
We construct teacher-specific corpora to warm-start the students before OPD. Prompts are drawn from GSM8K [54], Orca-Math [55], OpenMathInstruct-1 [56], and MATH excluding MATH-500 [57] for math, and OpenCodeInstruct [58], KodCode [59], TACO [60], and CodeContests [61] for code. For each prompt, the teacher samples 8 responses (temperature 0.60.6, top-p=0.95p=0.95); we keep one per prompt via task-specific correctness checks where available and quality filters otherwise, yielding a 10K-example SFT corpus for each teacher. Within each teacher–student pair and training seed, all OPD methods start from the same corresponding SFT checkpoint. Full composition, filtering, and license details are in Appendix B.
On-policy distillation.
Starting from the warm-start checkpoint, all methods follow a common OPD loop with method-specific supervision and loss safeguards: at each step the student samples a prefix under its current policy, the teacher is queried on this prefix to produce next-token supervision, and the student is updated under each method’s loss. Training prompts, prefix-generation settings, optimizer, schedule, training epochs, and hardware are held identical across methods. Hyperparameters are in Appendix C.
Baselines.
We compare SimCT against four baselines that span the main families of cross-tokenizer distillation. SimpleOPD performs exact shared-vocabulary OPD, supervising only the tokens that appear in both vocabularies. DSKD [22] unifies teacher and student output spaces through a learned cross-model projection, requiring additional trainable parameters. ALM [29] matches approximate likelihoods over tokenizer-induced chunks, providing supervision at the chunk level rather than the token level. GOLD [27] extends universal logit distillation [21] to on-policy training with text-aligned probability merging across tokenizer boundaries.
Benchmarks.
We evaluate mathematical reasoning on GSM8K [54] and MATH-500 [62], and code generation on MBPP [63] and LiveCodeBench-v6 [64]. For each SFT or OPD configuration, we train independently with 5 seeds and evaluate each resulting checkpoint once under zero-shot prompting, reporting mean pass@1 and standard deviation across the 5 runs; full templates, decoding settings, and scoring details are in Appendix D.

Within each pair and training seed, all distillation methods start from the same SFT-warm-started student checkpoint and follow the same common training protocol.
As reported in Appendix F, SimCT only slightly increases the wall-clock cost over SimpleOPD and is faster than the evaluated ALM, GOLD, and DSKD implementations.
The headline 6.0%6.0\% relative gain is the unweighted mean of the three pair-level improvements over SFT: 6.0%6.0\%, 7.3%7.3\%, and 4.7%4.7\% after rounding. Each pair-level score first averages the four benchmark pass@1 values, so this summary gives equal weight to benchmarks and to model pairs.
From these results, we draw the following observations:
• 
Obs 1: SimCT improves cross-tokenizer OPD consistently across teachers, students, and task domains.
SimCT achieves the best average performance for all three teacher–student pairs and obtains the strongest result on every evaluated benchmark.
The gains hold when Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct distills to two different student families, and also when Phi-4-mini-instruct serves as the teacher for Gemma-2-2B-it.
They also appear on both mathematical reasoning and code-generation benchmarks.
This consistency suggests that SimCT is not tied to a particular teacher, student, or task type, but provides a robust supervision interface for cross-tokenizer OPD.
• 
Obs 2: Exact shared-token supervision is too conservative under tokenizer mismatch.
SimpleOPD uses the most direct common supervision space: the exact shared vocabulary.
However, it improves only marginally over the SFT warm start across the three distillation settings.
This indicates that shared tokens preserve only a limited portion of useful teacher feedback when teacher and student tokenizers differ.
By adding minimal aligned units, SimCT recovers supervision from text continuations that are comparable but not represented as identical tokens, leading to consistently stronger performance than SimpleOPD.
• 
Obs 3: Prior cross-tokenizer interfaces are useful but less reliable across settings.
ALM, GOLD, and DSKD often improve over SimpleOPD, confirming that cross-tokenizer supervision beyond exact token overlap is beneficial.
However, their relative rankings vary across teacher–student pairs and benchmarks: no existing baseline gives a uniformly strongest interface.
This suggests that making heterogeneous predictions comparable is necessary but not sufficient for cross-tokenizer OPD.
The supervision interface must also preserve local teacher–student distinctions needed for on-policy next-token feedback, which SimCT addresses through minimal aligned units.
Overall, these observations support the central design choice of SimCT: keep the OPD training paradigm unchanged, but improve the supervision space in which teacher feedback is expressed.
Minimal aligned units provide a more robust supervision interface than exact shared tokens or prior cross-tokenizer mappings, leading to consistent gains in realistic cross-tokenizer OPD settings.

4.3 Supervision Recovery under Tokenizer Mismatch
We next ask whether SimCT improves cross-tokenizer OPD by recovering teacher supervision that is discarded under tokenizer mismatch.
Figure 3 decomposes the missing supervision, while Figure 4 tests whether recovering this supervision leads to downstream gains.
The downstream results in these figures come from independent ablation runs. Their relative improvements use the corresponding unadapted Base student as the reference; Table 1 reports the main training runs, with gains measured relative to SFT.
From these analyses, we draw the following observations:
• 
Obs 4: Tokenizer mismatch removes useful teacher feedback through both sequence mismatch and vocabulary mismatch.
Figure 3(A) shows that a non-trivial fraction of teacher- and student-side tokens cannot be aligned one-to-one.
This indicates that standard token-level matching fails to use teacher feedback on units that correspond to the same underlying text but are realized with different tokenizer boundaries.
Figure 3(B) reveals a complementary failure mode: even at token-to-token-aligned positions, many high-probability teacher predictions fall outside the shared vocabulary.
Together, these results show that SimpleOPD is conservative in two distinct ways: it discards supervision from non-1:1 aligned texts, and it also removes non-shared vocabulary predictions even when the current positions are aligned.
• 
Obs 5: The discarded signals are beneficial rather than incidental tokenizer artifacts.
Figure 3(C) directly tests whether the missing signals identified above improve learning.
Starting from SimpleOPD, +Out of Shared Vocab adds non-shared vocabulary supervision at token-to-token-aligned positions, while +Unaligned adds supervision from tokenizer-mismatched units that cannot be aligned one-to-one.
Both variants improve over SimpleOPD for both student models, showing that the discarded supervision signal contains useful distillation information.
Full SimCT achieves the largest improvement because it recovers both sources through a unified aligned-unit supervision space.
• 
Obs 6: Recovering more mismatch unit supervision consistently improves downstream performance.
Figure 4 (Left) further varies the fraction of tokenizer-mismatched unit supervision incorporated into training.
For both Gemma-2-2B and Phi-4-mini, performance improves as more recovered supervision is used.
This trend strengthens the mechanism suggested by Figure 3: mismatch unit supervision is not merely recoverable, but provides a stable learning signal when mapped into a common supervision space.
SimCT exploits this signal by aligning tokenizer-specific continuations into minimal aligned units, rather than discarding them under exact token overlap.
These findings explain why SimpleOPD yields only limited gains in Table 1.
Shared-vocabulary distillation preserves only the subset of teacher feedback that is already comparable in the original token spaces.
SimCT expands the supervision space to reincorporate supervision signal excluded by shared-vocabulary matching, thereby recovering useful teacher supervision while keeping the original OPD objective unchanged.

4.4 Ablation on Supervision-Space Construction
The previous analysis shows that tokenizer-mismatched units contain useful teacher supervision. We now ask whether the granularity of the recovered supervision also matters.
Starting from the SimCT supervision space, we construct coarsened variants by merging adjacent minimal aligned units into larger units.
Let qTminq_{T}^{\min} and qSminq_{S}^{\min} denote the teacher and student distributions over minimal aligned units.
For a coarsening 𝒞\mathcal{C}, the corresponding coarse distributions aggregate the probability mass of all minimal units inside each coarse unit:
qT𝒞​(c∣h)=∑u∈cqTmin​(u∣h),qS𝒞​(c∣h)=∑u∈cqSmin​(u∣h),q_{T}^{\mathcal{C}}(c\mid h)=\sum_{u\in c}q_{T}^{\min}(u\mid h),\qquad q_{S}^{\mathcal{C}}(c\mid h)=\sum_{u\in c}q_{S}^{\min}(u\mid h),
(11)
where hh is the student-generated prefix.
This aggregation preserves the total probability assigned to each coarse unit, but collapses the relative teacher–student preferences among its constituent minimal units.
We quantify the erased supervision signal as:
Δ𝒞=𝔼h[KL(qSmin(⋅∣h)∥qTmin(⋅∣h))−KL(qS𝒞(⋅∣h)∥qT𝒞(⋅∣h))].\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbb{E}_{h}\left[\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q_{S}^{\min}(\cdot\mid h)\,\|\,q_{T}^{\min}(\cdot\mid h)\right)-\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q_{S}^{\mathcal{C}}(\cdot\mid h)\,\|\,q_{T}^{\mathcal{C}}(\cdot\mid h)\right)\right].
(12)
Thus, Δ𝒞\Delta_{\mathcal{C}} measures the within-unit KL signal removed when minimal aligned units are merged.
• 
Obs 7: Coarsening removes useful local supervision.
Figure 4 (Right) shows a negative relationship between removed KL signal and downstream improvement: coarsenings with larger Δ𝒞\Delta_{\mathcal{C}} tend to yield weaker gains for both Gemma-2-2B-it and Phi-4-mini.
This indicates that coarsening does more than simplify the supervision space; it erases local teacher–student preferences useful for OPD.
The result supports Proposition 1 and explains why SimCT keeps minimal aligned units instead of merging them into coarser units.
Overall, this ablation shows that SimCT benefits not only from recovering supervision under tokenizer mismatch, but also from preserving it at the finest granularity jointly expressible by both tokenizers.
An additional within-unit scoring ablation favors token-length normalization over unnormalized and byte-normalized scores on Qwen2.5 →\to Gemma-2 (Appendix ).
5 Limitations
Our main evaluation centers on math and code reasoning with three teacher–student pairs spanning two teacher families and heterogeneous tokenizers; extending the study to additional domains, scales, and tokenizer families is left to future work. Methodologically, SimCT relies on mapping tokenizer mismatches to minimal aligned units, which is natural for standard BPE-style tokenizers and may require adaptation for tokenizers with substantially different segmentation behavior.
Finally, we target the white-box OPD setting in which teacher next-token distributions are available; extending supervision-space construction to black-box teachers that expose only samples or scalar feedback is a promising direction.
6 Conclusion
We studied cross-tokenizer OPD, where teacher and student policies follow OPD but use different tokenizers.
We argued that cross-tokenizer OPD should be viewed as a supervision-space construction problem: useful teacher feedback is lost when heterogeneous token-level predictions are forced to match only on exact shared tokens.
We introduced SimpleOPD as a conservative shared-vocabulary baseline and proposed SimCT, which expands the common supervision space with minimal aligned units while preserving the standard OPD objective.
Our theoretical analysis characterizes these units as the finest supervision interface consistent with the two fixed tokenizations and shows that coarsening them can erase useful teacher–student distinctions.
Across model families and math/code benchmarks, SimCT consistently improves over SimpleOPD and representative cross-tokenizer distillation baselines.
These results suggest that changing the supervision space, rather than the OPD training paradigm, is an effective path for cross-tokenizer OPD.

Appendix A Broader Impacts
SimCT may improve the accessibility of language-model distillation by enabling more knowledge transfer across models with different tokenizers.
This can reduce the cost of training capable, smaller models and make model development more accessible.
At the same time, stronger distillation can transfer undesirable teacher behaviors, including biases, unsafe responses, and hallucinations, and may lower the cost of producing models that could be misused.
Therefore, SimCT should be paired with appropriate data filtering, safety evaluation, and alignment procedures before deployment.
Appendix B Training Data Construction and Curation
This appendix describes the construction of the teacher-specific cold-start SFT corpora. Within each pair and seed, the corresponding warm-start checkpoint is shared by all OPD methods. We use public math and code datasets as prompt sources, apply filtering and deduplication, generate multiple teacher responses per prompt, and select one response according to correctness, executability, and formatting criteria. The SFT training procedure that consumes this corpus is described in Appendix C.
B.1 Composition
Each teacher-specific corpus contains 10,000 examples: 5,800 mathematical reasoning examples and 4,200 code-generation examples. The math/code split roughly matches the relative emphasis of the two task families in our evaluation suite, and within each family we draw from multiple sources to avoid overfitting to any single dataset’s style.
For mathematical reasoning, we sample 1,800 prompts from the GSM8K [54] training split, 2,200 from Orca-Math 200K [55], 1,000 from OpenMathInstruct-1 [56], and 800 from MATH [57], the last with the MATH-500 evaluation split [62] excluded. The MATH subset is drawn from levels 1–4 (150/150/350/150, respectively); we exclude level 5 because its solutions frequently exceed our SFT context budget and exhibit high teacher-side variance, both of which inject noise into the warm-start signal without proportionate benefit. Excluded difficulty levels are still represented in evaluation through MATH-500.
For code generation, we sample 1,800 prompts from OpenCodeInstruct [58], 900 from KodCode [59], 900 from TACO [60], and 600 from CodeContests [61]. The resulting subset covers function-level Python synthesis and competitive-programming I/O formats, with a preference toward examples that admit reliable execution-based verification.
B.2 Prompt Filtering and Deduplication
Before teacher generation, we filter prompts to remove noisy, duplicated, ill-formed, overly long, or difficult-to-verify instances, and we run overlap checks against held-out evaluation data using normalized text and near-duplicate matching to prevent test contamination.
For math sources, we retain text-only problems with clear statements, well-defined answer formats, and complete textual solutions. We discard examples with malformed questions, inconsistent answer formats, excessive derivation length, embedded code blocks, code-interpreter traces, or dependencies on diagrams, Asymptote figures, or other non-textual reasoning artifacts. We additionally apply problem-level near-duplicate filtering to suppress repeated template families.
For code sources, we keep only Python tasks with a well-defined solution target. When tests are available, we require compatibility with execution-based checking. We discard debugging, iterative-repair, SQL, shell, front-end, multi-file, interactive, project-dependent, and non-executable tasks. For contest-style sources (TACO, CodeContests), we further remove problems whose statements exceed our context budget or cannot be cleanly converted into standard Python generation examples.

B.3 Teacher Response Generation and Selection
For each retained prompt, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Phi-4-mini-instruct separately sample 8 candidate responses with temperature 0.60.6 and top-p=0.95p=0.95, yielding a diverse but focused candidate set. We then select a single response per prompt as the SFT target.
Verification-based selection.
When automatic verification is available, we keep only candidates that pass the corresponding checker. For math, we use math_verify11
 1
 https://github.com/huggingface/math-verify to compare extracted final answers under symbolic equivalence (handling fractions, equivalent algebraic forms, and standard formatting variations). For code, we execute each candidate against the dataset’s provided unit tests in a sandboxed Python environment with a per-test wall-clock timeout of 10 seconds and a memory cap of 128 MB. When multiple candidates pass, we keep the shortest valid response after standardized formatting normalization, which suppresses unnecessary verbosity and limits incidental teacher-specific style.
Quality-filter selection.
When verification is unavailable or partial (e.g., open-ended math without a canonical final-answer field, or code prompts without supplied tests), we apply rule-based quality filters: responses must contain a non-empty final answer in the expected format (boxed final answer for math, a complete fenced Python code block with a defined entry point for code), must not exceed 4096 tokens, and must not contain unterminated reasoning, repeated boilerplate, or empty solution bodies. The full filter rule set is documented in our released curation script. Among candidates passing all filters, we again prefer the shortest.
Role of the public datasets.
Public datasets are used as prompt sources only; the supervised targets are generated and selected through the unified teacher-response pipeline above. This keeps warm-start supervision consistent with the teacher policy used during the subsequent OPD stage and prevents distributional drift between the two training phases. Source attribution and metadata are preserved per example throughout the pipeline.

Appendix C Training Details
For each student model, training proceeds in two stages: a warm-start SFT stage to produce a non-trivial starting policy, followed by on-policy distillation. Within each teacher–student pair and seed, all OPD methods share the same warm-start checkpoint, training prompts, and common OPD hyperparameters. Each SFT and OPD configuration is trained independently with 5 seeds; each resulting checkpoint is evaluated once. Method-specific supervision construction and loss safeguards are specified below. Hyperparameters for both stages are summarized in Table 4; this section describes each stage in detail.
C.1 Warm-Start SFT
We first SFT each student on the corresponding teacher’s 10K-example corpus described in Appendix B to obtain the warm-start checkpoint from which all OPD methods start. Sharing this initialization both gives the student a non-trivial policy before OPD [11] and ensures that differences across OPD methods are not confounded by SFT data or initial checkpoints.
We optimize with AdamW using a peak learning rate of 2×10−62\times 10^{-6}, a linear warmup ratio of 0.050.05 followed by cosine decay, and train for 22 epochs. All students share the same SFT hyperparameters (Table 4). Training is performed in bfloat16 on 8×\timesH20 96GB.
C.2 On-Policy Distillation
Starting from the warm-start checkpoint, we train each OPD method under an identical loop: at every step the student samples a prefix under its current policy, the teacher is queried on this prefix to produce next-token supervision, and the student is updated under the method-specific loss. Training prompts, prefix-generation settings (temperature 0.60.6, top-p=0.95p=0.95), optimizer, learning-rate schedule, batch construction, and training epochs are held identical across methods (Table 4).
For SimCT and SimpleOPD, the OPD loss is reverse KL [11, 12] applied in their respective supervision spaces—SimCT’s space combines shared vocabulary tokens with minimal aligned units, while SimpleOPD restricts supervision to the exact teacher–student vocabulary overlap.

Appendix D Evaluation Benchmarks and Metrics
D.1 Benchmarks
We evaluate on four benchmarks covering complementary task regimes.
GSM8K [54] consists of grade-school word problems requiring multi-step arithmetic from natural-language descriptions; final answers are short numerical values, but solving the problems demands a coherent reasoning trajectory.
MATH-500 [57, 62] draws from competition-style problems spanning algebra, geometry, number theory, and combinatorics; answers may be symbolic forms, fractions, or equations, making the task sensitive to both reasoning quality and output normalization.
MBPP [63] measures function-level Python synthesis from natural-language specifications, evaluated by the dataset’s provided unit tests.
LiveCodeBench-v6 [64] evaluates code generation on contemporary problems with reduced contamination, requiring more robust algorithmic reasoning under hidden tests.
These benchmarks expose tokenizer mismatch in different ways—math evaluation involves symbolic expressions and rationale tokens, while code involves identifiers, indentation, and syntax fragments—so they jointly test whether SimCT recovers useful supervision across heterogeneous output structures rather than overfitting to a single format.
D.2 Decoding
All benchmarks are evaluated zero-shot: each model receives only the task prompt, with no in-context demonstrations. This isolates capability learned during SFT and OPD from prompt-level effects, and avoids tokenizer-dependent confounds introduced by few-shot examples.
For each benchmark, we use a fixed prompt template across all methods and student models. Math prompts ask the model to solve the problem and place the final answer in \boxed{}; code prompts present the specification and ask for a Python solution in the benchmark’s expected format. Full prompt templates are released in our supplementary code.
We decode with nucleus sampling at temperature 0.60.6 and top-p=0.95p=0.95, sampling one completion per instance. For each SFT or OPD configuration, the 5 runs correspond to independently trained checkpoints, each evaluated once. Unadapted Base models have no training stage and use 5 decoding seeds. The maximum generation length is fixed per benchmark and held identical across methods (4096 tokens for GSM8K, MATH-500 and LiveCodeBench-v6, and 2048 tokens for MBPP).
D.3 Scoring
We compute pass@1 [66] separately for each run and report the mean and standard deviation of the 5 run-level scores in Table 1. For trained models, this variability includes independent training and stochastic evaluation; it is not the standard error of the mean.
For math, we extract the final answer (preferring content inside \boxed{} when present) and compare against the reference using math_verify22

Appendix E Theory and Proofs
This appendix provides the formal definitions and proofs for
Theorem 1 and
Proposition 1. The section has two goals. First, we
formalize why SimCT aligned units are the minimal text units that remain
unions of complete tokens under the two fixed tokenizer partitions. Second, we prove the
property used in the supervision-space ablation: merging minimal aligned units
removes exactly the within-span KL signal that distinguishes teacher and
student preferences inside each merged span.
E.1 Tokenizer-Induced Spans and Minimal Aligned Units
Let yy be a finite text span represented over a fixed atomic string
representation, such as characters or bytes. We identify yy with the interval
[0,|y|][0,|y|]. The teacher tokenizer induces a partition
𝒫T(y)={[bi−1T,biT):i=1,…,m},0=b0T<⋯<bmT=|y|,\mathcal{P}_{T}(y)=\{[b^{T}_{i-1},b^{T}_{i}):i=1,\ldots,m\},\qquad 0=b^{T}_{0}<\cdots<b^{T}_{m}=|y|,
and the student tokenizer induces a partition
𝒫S(y)={[bj−1S,bjS):j=1,…,n},0=b0S<⋯<bnS=|y|.\mathcal{P}_{S}(y)=\{[b^{S}_{j-1},b^{S}_{j}):j=1,\ldots,n\},\qquad 0=b^{S}_{0}<\cdots<b^{S}_{n}=|y|.
Each interval in 𝒫T​(y)\mathcal{P}_{T}(y) is a complete teacher-token span, and
each interval in 𝒫S​(y)\mathcal{P}_{S}(y) is a complete student-token span. Throughout this proof, a jointly tokenizable unit means a union of complete consecutive tokens in each of these fixed partitions. The minimality statement is relative to these fixed partitions, with token boundaries inherited from the full text.
We first take the union of teacher and student token boundaries:
ℬ∪​(y)=ℬT​(y)∪ℬS​(y),\mathcal{B}_{\cup}(y)=\mathcal{B}_{T}(y)\cup\mathcal{B}_{S}(y),
where ℬT​(y)={biT}i=0m\mathcal{B}_{T}(y)=\{b^{T}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{m} and
ℬS​(y)={bjS}j=0n\mathcal{B}_{S}(y)=\{b^{S}_{j}\}_{j=0}^{n}. Let
0=c0<c1<⋯<cℓ=|y|0=c_{0}<c_{1}<\cdots<c_{\ell}=|y|
be the sorted elements of ℬ∪​(y)\mathcal{B}_{\cup}(y). This gives atomic
fragments
ℱ(y)={[ck−1,ck):k=1,…,ℓ}.\mathcal{F}(y)=\{[c_{k-1},c_{k}):k=1,\ldots,\ell\}.
These fragments are only used to define the construction. A fragment may lie
inside a teacher token or a student token, and therefore is not necessarily a
valid supervision unit by itself.
To obtain jointly-tokenizable units, we build an undirected graph GyG_{y} whose
vertices are the atomic fragments in ℱ⁡(y)\mathcal{F}(y). Two vertices are
connected if their fragments lie inside the same teacher-token span or inside
the same student-token span. The connected components of GyG_{y} induce a
partition of yy, denoted
𝒫SimCT​(y)={u1,…,ur}.\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SimCT}}(y)=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}\}.
We call each uku_{k} a minimal aligned unit. Intuitively, the connected
component closes all tokenizer overlaps: if splitting a span would cut through
a complete teacher token or a complete student token, the corresponding
fragments must remain in the same aligned unit.
E.2 Proof of Theorem 1
Proof.
We prove joint tokenizability and minimality.
Joint tokenizability.
Consider any unit u∈𝒫SimCT​(y)u\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SimCT}}(y). By construction,
uu is a connected component of GyG_{y}. If a teacher-token span intersects
uu, then all atomic fragments inside that teacher token are connected in
GyG_{y}, so the entire teacher-token span must lie in the same connected
component. Therefore, no boundary of uu cuts through a teacher token, and
uu is a union of consecutive complete teacher tokens.
The same argument applies to the student tokenizer. If a student-token span
intersects uu, all atomic fragments inside that student token are connected,
so the complete student-token span lies inside uu. Hence uu is also a
union of consecutive complete student tokens. Thus uu can be represented as
one or more teacher tokens and also as one or more student tokens.
Minimality.
Assume for contradiction that a unit uu admits a strictly finer non-empty
decomposition
u=u′∘u′′,u=u^{\prime}\circ u^{\prime\prime},
where both u′u^{\prime} and u′′u^{\prime\prime} are jointly tokenizable. Let zz be the boundary
between u′u^{\prime} and u′′u^{\prime\prime}. Since both sides are unions of complete teacher
tokens, zz must be a teacher-token boundary. Since both sides are also
unions of complete student tokens, zz must be a student-token boundary.
Therefore, no teacher token or student token crosses zz.
It follows that no edge in GyG_{y} can connect an atomic fragment in u′u^{\prime} to
an atomic fragment in u′′u^{\prime\prime}, because such an edge would require the two
fragments to lie inside the same teacher-token span or the same student-token
span, contradicting that no token crosses zz. Thus u′u^{\prime} and u′′u^{\prime\prime} would
belong to different connected components of GyG_{y}, contradicting that uu
is one connected component. Therefore, no strictly finer non-empty
jointly-tokenizable decomposition exists.
∎
Implication.
The theorem shows that SimCT does not rely on an arbitrary span length. It
selects the finest units that are valid under both tokenizers. Finer atomic
fragments may cut through complete tokens, while coarser mismatch spans merge
multiple valid aligned units.

E.4 Discussion
The theorem and proposition play complementary roles. The theorem identifies
the finest jointly-tokenizable units available under the two tokenizer
partitions. The proposition shows what is lost when those units are merged:
coarsening preserves the total probability of each merged span, but discards
the conditional teacher–student differences among the minimal units inside it.
These results rely only on tokenizer boundaries and probability aggregation.
They do not assume a learned semantic alignment, an embedding similarity metric,
or an optimal-transport plan. This matches the design of SimCT: the method
changes the supervision space to the finest comparable text units while keeping
the OPD objective itself unchanged.

For the aggregate estimate, we use a conservative rounded runtime of approximately 33 hours per cross-tokenizer OPD run, consistent with the measured runtimes in Table 5.

Second, cross-tokenizer OPD training costs about 9.49.4 days on a single 8×8\times H20 server, covering three teacher–student pairs (Qwen2.5→\rightarrowPhi4, Qwen2.5→\rightarrowGemma-2, and Phi4→\rightarrowGemma-2), each trained with five methods (SimpleOPD, SimCT, ALM, GOLD, and DSKD), and each method-pair configuration repeated over 55 random seeds, yielding 3×5×5=753\times 5\times 5=75 training runs.
Third, evaluation costs about 4.44.4 days on a single 8×8\times H20 server, covering 3×53\times 5 Base-model decoding runs, 3×53\times 5 independently trained warm-start checkpoints, and 15×515\times 5 independently trained OPD checkpoints. Each trained checkpoint is evaluated once, yielding 15+15+75=10515+15+75=105 evaluation runs.
The reported total only accounts for the runs needed to reproduce the main experimental results.
It excludes preliminary studies, hyperparameter exploration, debugging runs, ablation studies in Section 4.3 and Section 4.4, the additional analyses in Appendix , and failed or repeated jobs during development.
The full project compute is therefore larger than the reproducibility cost reported here.
Finally, the end-to-end wall-clock cost of cross-tokenizer OPD is dominated by student rollouts and teacher forward passes.
SimCT operates at the distillation-interface level. It introduces no trainable projection module, requires no global vocabulary-level alignment, and keeps the standard OPD training pipeline unchanged.
As a result, SimCT remains close to the lightweight SimpleOPD baseline and is faster than more complex cross-tokenizer baselines, while recovering substantially more teacher supervision than SimpleOPD.
Appendix G Case Study
We provide qualitative case studies on math and code examples to illustrate how different supervision interfaces can affect cross-tokenizer OPD behavior at the local level.
For each example, we compare SimCT with representative baselines under the same teacher–student setting and report the final answer or execution result.
These cases highlight representative local errors—an incorrect modular computation, a missing symmetry factor, a mismatched input-output convention, or an invalid function signature—that are easy to miss in aggregate scores.
Several baselines recover the high-level solution template but fail on these local distinctions, which is consistent with the supervision loss caused by tokenizer mismatch as measured in Section 4.3.
SimCT avoids these specific errors in the shown examples, providing qualitative intuition for how finer-grained supervision can help preserve local reasoning accuracy.
These examples complement the aggregate evaluation in Table 1.
