Abstract
On-policy distillation provides dense teacher supervision on a language model’s own trajectories.
In self-distillation with privileged context, this supervision comes from the model itself, conditioned on a hint or solution trace hidden from the student.
When the teacher’s preferences hinge on privileged information, it can assign higher probability to continuations the student cannot infer from its own context.
Matching these preferences throughout training can induce predictive drift and degrade out-of-distribution (OOD) reasoning.
We propose GeoSD, a self-distillation method that controls this drift through two complementary geometric terms.
A Hellinger loss weights each teacher preference by the student–teacher overlap, reducing the influence of tokens to which the student assigns low probability.
Because these influences can still accumulate, a Fisher–Rao penalty regulates predictive distance from a copy of the student refreshed periodically during training.
Both terms compare next-token distributions in Fisher–Rao geometry and are jointly optimized with a preconditioner motivated by the natural gradient.
Across three model families, GeoSD retains strong in-distribution gains while improving average mathematical OOD accuracy by 5.75.7–8.68.6 points over the base model.
OOD gains hold across five model scales from 1.71.7B to 3232B and transfer to code generation, where GeoSD improves code accuracy by 1.91.9 points on average despite distilling on mathematics alone.
Our analysis of mathematical reasoning shows that standard matching rapidly concentrates probability mass at high-entropy states and that its samples confidently agree on incorrect answers. In contrast, GeoSD preserves alternative token mass and reduces false consensus.
1 Introduction
On-policy distillation (OPD) offers a practical approach to post-training large language models (LLMs), providing dense teacher supervision on trajectories generated by the student itself (Agarwal et al., 2024; Song and Zheng, 2026).
Dense supervision is especially valuable for reasoning, where final-answer rewards are often too sparse to reveal which intermediate steps were useful (Lightman et al., 2024).
Rather than assigning credit only after a solution is complete, OPD uses the teacher’s predictions at each state the student visits to provide token-level supervision throughout the trajectory.
In on-policy self-distillation (OPSD), the student acts as its own teacher, conditioned on self-generated prefixes. The recipe can scale cheaply, since it requires no separate, stronger teacher.
A common and effective variant gives the teacher privileged context, such as verified reasoning traces (Zhao et al., 2026), textual environment feedback (Hübotter et al., 2026), or other informative context available during training (Penaloza et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026). The teacher’s predictions are then distilled into a student that has no access to this additional context.
Whether dense supervision helps depends on the student’s ability to follow the teacher’s token-level preferences.
Li et al. (2026) show that OPD progresses as student and teacher assign more mass to the same high-probability tokens, while incompatible reasoning patterns can prevent effective distillation.
Privileged context makes such mismatch common: the teacher can commit to continuations that are obvious given the solution but still uncertain from the student’s context (Harne et al., 2026).
At these mismatched states, the teacher’s confident predictions can generate large gradients, even when the student assigns low probability to the same tokens.
Across many updates, the student’s probability mass can concentrate on continuations it was initially uncertain about.
This shift can hurt generalization: Kim et al. (2026) observe that privileged-context OPSD suppresses uncertainty and degrades out-of-distribution (OOD) performance.
Existing approaches mostly address which teacher signals to use or how they are matched. Some filter or down-weight signals judged unreliable (Xing et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026; Stein et al., 2026); others change the direction or weighting of divergences in the Kullback–Leibler (KL) family (Gu et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2024; Ko et al., 2025) or reweight token credit (Yang et al., 2026).
These choices can shape the teacher’s influence at each state but do not directly regulate the predictive drift that accumulates over training.
States that produce large updates can still provide useful supervision. Filtering them may discard that signal, while retaining them may transfer the teacher’s overconfidence.
We introduce GeoSD (Geometric Self-Distillation),
which combines two complementary components to attenuate teacher influence at mismatched states and regulate the student’s predictive drift across updates.
First, we replace the standard KL distillation objective with a Hellinger loss.
Its gradient weights each teacher preference by the geometric mean of the student and teacher probabilities.
Teacher-preferred tokens therefore have little influence when the student assigns them little probability, but receive greater weight as the student’s support increases (Figure 1A).
Second, we penalize accumulated movement from a periodically refreshed checkpoint with the Fisher–Rao distance between predictive distributions (Figure 1B).
Since both terms compare next-token distributions in the same geometry, we optimize them jointly with a curvature preconditioner motivated by the natural gradient, which measures parameter updates by their effect on the student’s next-token predictions.
Empirically, GeoSD preserves the in-distribution (ID) gains of standard OPSD while substantially improving OOD reasoning in mathematics and code generation.
We link this improvement to how each objective reaches agreement: standard matching concentrates mass on the teacher’s top choice by draining the alternatives, whereas GeoSD makes that choice the most probable token while retaining more mass on alternative continuations.
Our contributions are threefold.
(i) We introduce GeoSD, a self-distillation objective that weights each teacher preference by teacher–student overlap and penalizes predictive drift from a recent checkpoint.
(ii) GeoSD improves average OOD accuracy in mathematical reasoning by 5.75.7–8.68.6 points (avg@16) over the base model across three model families, whereas standard KL objectives degrade it (§3).
GeoSD retains ID gains and leads on all tested OOD benchmarks, with mathematical gains across five model scales (1.7B–32B) and average code gains of 1.91.9 points over the base model.
(iii) We characterize how standard matching fails out of distribution, showing that it concentrates probability mass at high-entropy states and its samples agree confidently on incorrect answers (§4).

2 Method
GeoSD regulates the student’s next-token distributions using a geometry that measures changes by their relative effect on probability mass instead of their raw magnitude.
After defining the setup (§2.1), we introduce overlap-aware matching to limit local teacher pressure (§2.2), a checkpoint penalty to control predictive drift (§2.3), and a curvature preconditioner to optimize both (§2.4).
2.1 Setup
Let 𝒱\mathcal{V} denote the vocabulary and Δ𝒱\Delta_{\mathcal{V}} the probability simplex over it. For an input 𝐱\mathbf{x} and an on-policy continuation 𝐲∼πθ(⋅∣𝐱)\mathbf{y}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x}), the student (pp) and teacher (qq) next-token distributions at position tt are

where the teacher reads the same prefix but with privileged context 𝐜\mathbf{c}, an answer-verified solution trace sampled from the base model π0\pi_{0}.
By default, we use the current student as the teacher with privileged context, though the objective also supports a fixed reference, a delayed copy, or an exponential moving average of the student (see Appendix C.7 for evaluations).
Because 𝐜\mathbf{c} reveals the solution, q(t)q^{(t)} provides a more informed target than pθ(t)p_{\theta}^{(t)}, but it can also favor tokens that the student cannot infer from 𝐱\mathbf{x} and 𝐲<t\mathbf{y}_{<t} alone.
We omit the parameter subscript in q(t)q^{(t)} to indicate that it is a stop-gradient target. Gradients flow only through pθ(t)p_{\theta}^{(t)}, moving the student toward the privileged-context distribution while treating that target as fixed.
Since the default teacher shares the student’s parameters, each update also changes the teacher’s predictions (§2.3).

2.2 Local teacher influence
The student often retains plausible alternatives beyond its top prediction, corresponding to continuations it may still sample. Suppressing one such alternative can produce only a small change in raw probability space, while effectively removing that continuation. A suitable distance should therefore measure changes relative to each token’s probability mass: the same absolute change can be minor for the top prediction but significant for a lower-probability alternative.
Information geometry provides a natural way to measure these changes (Amari, 2016; Amari and Nagaoka, 2000). By taking the square root of each probability, we represent next-token distributions as points on a unit hypersphere, where geometric distances correspond to statistical distances between distributions (Figure 1B; Appendix A.2).
On the sphere, two natural distances are the straight-line chord and the shortest arc along the surface.
The chord yields the squared Hellinger divergence used for per-state matching:

2.3 Global drift control
Overlap weighting limits teacher influence at each state, but small updates can still accumulate into substantial predictive drift over training.
Because the student and teacher share parameters, self-distillation can compound this effect: shifts in the student’s predictions carry over to the teacher’s later targets.
Penalizing displacement from a reference model is an established practice in post-training (§5). We adopt this approach using the squared Fisher–Rao distance from a recent checkpoint θckpt\theta_{\mathrm{ckpt}}, refreshed every KckptK_{\mathrm{ckpt}} steps:1
ℒprox(θ;θckpt)=𝔼𝐱,𝐲∼πθ[1T∑t=1TdF​R2(πθ(⋅∣𝐱,𝐲<t),πθckpt(⋅∣𝐱,𝐲<t))].\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prox}}(\theta;\theta_{\mathrm{ckpt}})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\,\mathbf{y}\sim\pi_{\theta}}\Big[\tfrac{1}{T}\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{FR}^{2}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{<t}),\,\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{ckpt}}}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{<t})\big)\Big].
(5)
Refreshing the checkpoint θckpt\theta_{\mathrm{ckpt}} regularly allows continued adaptation that a fixed anchor at π0\pi_{0} could restrict.
In our experiments, overlap weighting alone slows cumulative drift but does not stop it, and adding the proximal term keeps it controlled (Figure 2B).
2.4 Full objective and preconditioned update
The objective combines the two terms,
ℒ⁡(θ)=ℒdistil​(θ)+λ​ℒprox​(θ,θckpt),\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{distil}}(\theta)+\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prox}}(\theta;\theta_{\mathrm{ckpt}}),
(6)
with λ\lambda setting the strength of the drift penalty.
Both terms measure differences between next-token distributions in the same geometry. We use this geometry to guide the parameter update as well.
A natural gradient gives the direction of greatest local decrease in the objective for a fixed, small change in the student’s predictive distribution, measured by the Fisher metric.
This amounts to preconditioning the gradient with the inverse Fisher information matrix Fk−1F_{k}^{-1} of the current policy (Amari, 1998; Schulman et al., 2015):
θk+1=θk−η​Fk−1​∇θℒ​(θk).\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}-\eta\,F_{k}^{-1}\,\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{k}).
(7)
Under a local approximation, the proximal term contributes a pull toward the checkpoint, weighted by the checkpoint’s Fisher information and preconditioned by the current policy’s inverse Fisher (Appendix A.6).
At LLM scale the full Fisher is infeasible to form or invert, so we instead precondition with a damped Kronecker-factored curvature estimate (K-FAC; Martens and Grosse, 2015). This layerwise approximation avoids materializing the full matrix and reuses the existing backward pass (Appendix B.6).
It keeps the update curvature-aware while adding a modest cost in memory (2121% total, 88% on training workers at 8B) and runtime (1.1×1.1\times wall-clock) over a standard distillation step (Appendix D).
3 Experiments
3.1 Experimental setup
Models.
We evaluate along two independent axes. For scale, we use the Qwen3 dense family at five sizes: 1.7B, 4B, 8B, 14B, and 32B (Yang et al., 2025).
For cross-family generalization, we compare three models from different families: Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025), Olmo-3-7B-Think (Olmo et al., 2025), and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B (DS-R1-Llama-8B; Guo et al., 2025). At 14B and 32B, we use a more memory-efficient K-FAC configuration (Appendix B.6).
Training and evaluation.
On-policy trajectories are sampled from the student on problems from DAPO-Math-17k (Yu et al., 2025), a competition-mathematics dataset with verifiable integer answers but no reference solutions. In-distribution performance is measured on a held-out split.
We first evaluate generalization on related mathematics benchmarks, then test transfer to code generation.
For mathematics, we use AIME 2024, AIME 2025, AMC 2023, and MATH-500 (Lightman et al., 2024), where prior work reports degradation from privileged-context OPSD (Kim et al., 2026). These benchmarks all cover competition mathematics, so they mainly test shifts in problem source and difficulty rather than domain.
We report avg@kk (average accuracy over kk samples) and pass@kk (the fraction of problems with at least one correct answer among kk samples).
By default, the privileged context is a full answer-verified solution, following the OPSD convention (Zhao et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026). We construct solutions offline by sampling up to 3232 rollouts per problem from π0\pi_{0} and retaining those with verified answers. Problems with no verified solution are excluded from training. At each training step, we draw one retained solution as the teacher’s privileged context.
To assess cross-domain transfer, we evaluate Qwen3-8B trained only on DAPO-Math-17k on HumanEval+ (Chen et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2023), MBPP+ (Austin et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2023), and LiveCodeBench (Jain et al., 2025).
For both domains, aggregate OOD accuracy is the unweighted mean across the corresponding benchmarks.
For the main comparisons, we report avg@16 averaged over ten training and evaluation runs with different random seeds.
Full data, rollout, and evaluation settings are in Appendix B.
Baselines.
We compare GeoSD against three baseline families.
Reference baselines include Base (π0\pi_{0}; initial model checkpoint), SFT (supervised fine-tuning) on privileged teacher traces, and GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) with verifiable rewards on the same training prompts. These respectively represent the pre-trained reference point, off-policy imitation of privileged solutions, and a privilege-free sparse-reward post-training baseline.
Divergence controls use the same data and rollout pipeline with alternative per-position losses: forward KL (FwdKL; Hinton et al., 2015; Zhao et al., 2026), reverse KL (RevKL; Gu et al., 2024), Jensen–Shannon (JSD, β=0.5\beta{=}0.5; Agarwal et al., 2024), and skewed KL (SkewKL, α=0.1\alpha{=}0.1; Ko et al., 2024; Ko et al., 2025).
Filtering baselines control which teacher signals to use: TrOPD (Xing et al., 2026) separates teacher-reliable regions from outlier regions using teacher–student agreement, while TIP (Xu et al., 2026) selects informative tokens using student uncertainty and teacher–student mismatch.
We provide implementation details for baselines in Appendix B.5.
Compute overhead.
GeoSD requires only 1.10×1.10\times the wall-clock time of standard OPSD, as checkpoint scoring overlaps with rollout generation.
The checkpoint forward pass and K-FAC accumulation raise optimizer FLOPs to 1.26×1.26\times, and the checkpoint weights and K-FAC state raise total memory to 1.21×1.21\times the baseline.
Since the checkpoint resides on inference workers by default, training-worker memory grows by only ≈8%{\approx}8\%. A detailed breakdown is in Appendix D.

Geometry improves the ID–OOD reasoning frontier.
Table 1 compares post-training methods on ID and OOD performance, showing that large in-distribution gains do not necessarily transfer under distribution shift.
FwdKL achieves some of the largest ID gains of any method, yet its OOD accuracy degrades relative to π0\pi_{0} in all three families by 4.64.6–8.18.1 points avg@1616.
This combination of ID improvement and OOD degradation is consistent with prior reports on imitation-style post-training.
Softer objectives (SkewKL, JSD) reduce the degradation but yield little or no improvement over the base model, and the filtering baselines (TrOPD, TIP) yield only modest OOD gains (+0.4+0.4 to +3.2+3.2).
In contrast, GeoSD improves average OOD accuracy by 5.75.7–8.68.6 points avg@1616 over π0\pi_{0} while retaining strong ID gains; it ranks first on OOD in every family while staying within 0.70.7–1.71.7 points of the best ID score.
Moreover, GeoSD achieves the highest OOD accuracy on each of the four benchmarks across all three model families (see Appendix Table 5 for the per-benchmark breakdown).
Its improvement in average OOD accuracy over the strongest baseline is statistically significant in every evaluated model family (Appendix Table 7).
To make this trade-off easier to compare visually, we plot ID and OOD gains together in Appendix Figure 7, with marker size indicating OOD pass@16. In every family, baselines that match or exceed GeoSD on ID accuracy fall well below it on OOD accuracy.
This separation also persists across learning-rate and early-stopping sweeps, where weakening or shortening FwdKL training recovers OOD accuracy at the expense of ID gains, without exceeding the base model’s OOD performance.
GeoSD, by comparison, improves both ID and OOD accuracy across the evaluated configurations (Appendix C.5).
These comparisons show that smaller updates or earlier stopping alone do not match the combination of ID performance and OOD gains achieved by GeoSD.
Transfer from mathematics to code generation.
We also evaluate code generation with Qwen3-8B after distilling exclusively on DAPO-Math, without any code supervision.
As shown in Table 1, GeoSD improves average OOD code accuracy from 46.646.6 to 48.548.5, a gain of 1.91.9 percentage points over π0\pi_{0} and 1.21.2 points over GRPO, the strongest evaluated code baseline.
SFT, FwdKL, and RevKL instead reduce average code accuracy, while JSD makes little difference, and SkewKL, GRPO, and the filtering baselines yield only small gains.
The improvement over π0\pi_{0} holds on all three benchmarks: HumanEval+, MBPP+, and LiveCodeBench (App. Table 6).
Although smaller than the gains in mathematics, the consistent improvements in code generation suggest that regulating predictive drift during mathematics distillation may preserve reasoning capabilities useful in other domains.
From partial to full solution context.
To test how the amount of privileged information affects generalization, we split each reference solution into sentences and reveal progressively longer prefixes to the teacher, up to the full solution, holding other settings fixed.
Figure 4 shows that FwdKL degrades even when only a small fraction of the solution is revealed, while RevKL remains near the base model until roughly half the solution is revealed, then declines sharply. Both FwdKL and RevKL reach their worst OOD accuracy at full privilege.
In contrast, GeoSD improves monotonically across the same range.
This pattern suggests that richer supervision is more useful when teacher preferences are weighted by the student’s current predictions, limiting pressure toward continuations to which the student assigns low probability.
Geometric control complements scale.
Figure 4 examines GeoSD across the Qwen3 ladder, from 1.7B to 32B. Larger base models are stronger on both ID and OOD, and GeoSD further improves OOD accuracy over the base model at every size.
The gain is non-monotone in scale, peaking at +8.6+8.6 points at 8B and remaining substantial at +6.1+6.1 at 32B, still larger than the gains at 1.7B and 4B.
Even for larger models, geometric regulation brings clear improvements during self-distillation.

3.3 Ablations
GeoSD couples three components: overlap-aware matching, a proximal penalty on cumulative drift, and a K-FAC preconditioned update.
Table 2 separates their contributions on Qwen3-8B. Replacing Hellinger with JSD, removing the proximal penalty, or replacing K-FAC with AdamW lowers OOD accuracy from 60.560.5 to 56.156.1, 53.853.8, and 56.756.7, respectively. Across the full grid, Fisher–Rao regularization outperforms a Euclidean parameter penalty, and K-FAC improves OOD accuracy in every matched loss–penalty comparison. Yet forward KL with both Fisher–Rao regularization and K-FAC remains below the base model (50.650.6 versus 51.951.9), indicating that regularization and preconditioning alone do not recover the full gain.
Simpler ways to limit teacher influence do not recover the same trade-off (Appendix C.6). Softening the forward-KL teacher improves OOD retention but sacrifices ID accuracy and leaves OOD accuracy below π0\pi_{0} at every tested temperature. Even with Fisher–Rao regularization, forward KL with per-state gradient clipping and Hellinger with clipped AdamW both fall well short of the full method on OOD accuracy. Fixing the anchor at π0\pi_{0} further reduces drift but lowers both ID (82.7→71.882.7\to 71.8) and OOD (60.5→55.660.5\to 55.6) accuracy.
OOD gains over the base model persist across teacher choices and tested hyperparameters, with little variation among moving teachers under the full objective and a benefit from proximal regularization even when the teacher is frozen (Appendices C.7 and C.8).
4 Alignment without collapse
Why does standard OPSD improve ID performance while hurting generalization?
To explore this, we analyze concentration in next-token distributions at high-entropy states (§4.1) and agreement among final answers from different rollouts (§4.2).
We find that rapid concentration at individual token positions is accompanied by strong agreement on incorrect final answers.
4.1 Local concentration at high-entropy states
We examine states where the base model is least certain and privileged context may substantially change its predictions.
For each rollout we select the top 10%10\% of positions by the base model’s (π0\pi_{0}) next-token entropy.
We hold these positions fixed across methods and checkpoints, ensuring that comparisons are not affected by changes in which states each model treats as uncertain.
At each selected position we rank the candidate tokens by the teacher’s probability, with rank 1 the teacher’s most probable token, and track how the student’s average mass on ranks 11–88 evolves over training.
Figure 5 compares FwdKL and GeoSD across post-training checkpoints.
Under forward KL, rank-1 mass increases sharply over training, concentrating probability on the teacher’s top choice.
Under GeoSD, rank-1 mass rises gradually and substantial mass remains on lower-ranked teacher tokens even after the top choice becomes the student’s modal token.
Both methods therefore increase agreement with the teacher’s top choice, but GeoSD retains more probability mass on alternatives.
The rapid concentration on the top choice, i.e., rank-1 collapse, leaves less probability mass for alternatives. When lower-ranked teacher tokens represent viable continuations, this makes paths that may be useful out of distribution less likely to be explored.
These token-level differences leave open whether rollouts converge on correct answers or shared mistakes. We next examine agreement and correctness at the answer level.

5 Related work
Privileged-context self-distillation uses richer training-time contexts to supervise the student on its own rollouts (Zhao et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026; Penaloza et al., 2026).
This asymmetry can suppress uncertainty and degrade OOD reasoning (Kim et al., 2026).
Similarly, Harne et al. (2026) show that privileged teachers can favor reference-specific trajectories and penalize exploratory tokens, reducing distillation loss without improving task accuracy.
Existing approaches address two aspects of teacher supervision. Signal selection uses reliability (Xing et al., 2026), uncertainty and mismatch (Xu et al., 2026), or consensus across multiple teacher traces (Stein et al., 2026). Objective adjustment mixes KL directions according to teacher entropy (Jin et al., 2026) or softens forward-KL targets where student support is low (Jang et al., 2026).
Signal selection and objective adjustment regulate local teacher pressure without bounding its accumulation; our ablations show that local regulation alone recovers little OOD accuracy (Table 2).
Drift control is well established in other learning settings: continual learning penalizes parameter or prediction changes (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017; Li and Hoiem, 2018), RLHF anchors policies to a frozen reference via KL (Ziegler et al., 2019; Ouyang et al., 2022), and TRPO/PPO limit policy changes per update (Schulman et al., 2015; Schulman et al., 2017). Spigler (2025) combines proximal policy optimization with student-driven distillation for RL control.
Our focus is on jointly regulating teacher influence and predictive drift in self-distillation: GeoSD combines overlap-aware matching with a moving checkpoint penalty, jointly optimized with a K-FAC preconditioner.
6 Conclusion
Privileged-context self-distillation provides dense supervision but can push the student toward confident teacher predictions even where it currently assigns little probability.
GeoSD regulates teacher influence and predictive drift through overlap-aware matching and a moving checkpoint penalty, both in a shared geometry and jointly optimized with a curvature preconditioner.
It retains strong ID gains while improving OOD mathematical reasoning and transfer to code.
Our analysis links forward-KL matching to rapid concentration at high-entropy states and to confident agreement on incorrect answers, whereas GeoSD preserves alternatives and reduces false consensus.
These findings suggest that regulating the local influence of teacher guidance and its cumulative effect on the student’s predictions can improve reasoning generalization.
We discuss limitations in Appendix E.

Appendix A Information geometry and the natural-gradient update
This appendix lays out the geometry behind our objective and update rule. We first introduce Fisher information (§A.1) and the spherical representation of next-token distributions, which relates Hellinger divergence to Fisher–Rao distance (§A.2). We then show that these quantities and both KL directions share the same local quadratic form up to scale (§A.3), describe Hellinger’s place in the α\alpha-divergence family (§A.4), and examine how its gradient weights teacher preferences (§A.5). Finally, we derive the local contribution of the Fisher–Rao proximal penalty to the natural-gradient update (§A.6).
A.1 Fisher information
Consider a family of distributions pθ​(x)p_{\theta}(x) that is smooth in θ\theta and has support independent of θ\theta. The score is the gradient of the log-likelihood, ∇θ​log​pθ​(x)\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(x). Assuming differentiation and integration can be interchanged, the score has mean zero under the model
𝔼x∼pθ[∇θlogpθ(x)]=∫∇θpθ(x)dx=∇θ∫pθ(x)dx=0.\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\theta}}\!\left[\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(x)\right]=\int\nabla_{\theta}p_{\theta}(x)\,dx=\nabla_{\theta}\!\int p_{\theta}(x)\,dx=0.
The Fisher information matrix is the covariance of the score:
F⁡(θ)=𝔼x∼pθ​[∇θ​log​pθ​(x)​∇θ​log​pθ​(x)⊤]=−𝔼x∼pθ​[∇θ2​log​pθ​(x)].F(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\theta}}\!\left[\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(x)\,\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(x)^{\top}\right]=-\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\theta}}\!\left[\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\log p_{\theta}(x)\right].
(9)
Curvature and score variability.
The Hessian form identifies F⁡(θ)F(\theta) with the expected curvature of the negative log-likelihood. For a parameter direction vv, the quantity v⊤​F​(θ)​vv^{\top}F(\theta)v measures this curvature along vv. The covariance form expresses the same quantity as the variance of the directional score v⊤​∇θ​log⁡pθ​(x)v^{\top}\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(x).
The Fisher metric.
Under smoothness assumptions, the KL divergence between neighboring distributions satisfies
KL(pθ∥pθ+d​θ)=12dθ⊤F(θ)dθ+O(∥dθ∥3),\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_{\theta}\,\|\,p_{\theta+d\theta}\right)=\tfrac{1}{2}\,d\theta^{\top}F(\theta)\,d\theta+O(\|d\theta\|^{3}),
(10)
with the reverse direction having the same quadratic term (§A.3). Thus, parameter steps of equal Euclidean length can produce different changes in the distribution, depending on their direction. Where F⁡(θ)F(\theta) is positive definite, it defines a Riemannian metric that measures local statistical distinguishability. In redundant parameterizations, including neural networks, F⁡(θ)F(\theta) may be singular: directions that leave the distribution unchanged to first order have zero length under this quadratic form.
Invariance and the policy geometry.
Under a smooth invertible reparameterization ϕ=ϕ⁡(θ)\phi=\phi(\theta) with Jacobian J=∂ϕ/∂θJ=\partial\phi/\partial\theta, the Fisher information transforms as Fθ=J⊤​Fϕ​JF_{\theta}=J^{\top}F_{\phi}J. Thus, d​θ⊤​Fθ​d​θ=d​ϕ⊤​Fϕ​d​ϕd\theta^{\top}F_{\theta}d\theta=d\phi^{\top}F_{\phi}d\phi: the local measure of distributional change is independent of the parameterization. A stronger property distinguishes the Fisher metric from other Riemannian metrics. On finite probability simplices, Čencov’s theorem characterizes it, up to an overall scale, by invariance under sufficient-statistic transformations (Amari, 2016). For next-token distributions, this metric has a closed form and a spherical representation (§A.2). Pulling it back through θ↦πθ(⋅∣s)\theta\mapsto\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s) and averaging over states gives the policy Fisher, whose inverse defines the ideal natural-gradient preconditioner (§A.6).
A.2 The sphere of next-token distributions
A next-token distribution over a vocabulary 𝒱\mathcal{V} is a point in the probability simplex Δ𝒱\Delta_{\mathcal{V}}. In its interior, where pi>0p_{i}>0, the Fisher metric has a simple form. For a displacement d​𝐩d\mathbf{p} satisfying ∑id​pi=0\sum_{i}dp_{i}=0, the directional score for observing category jj is d​pj/pjdp_{j}/p_{j}. Taking its second moment under 𝐩\mathbf{p} gives
d​s2=∑i(d​pi)2pi,∑id​pi=0.ds^{2}=\sum_{i}\frac{(dp_{i})^{2}}{p_{i}},\qquad\sum_{i}dp_{i}=0.
(11)
Thus, the metric is represented by diag⁡(1/pi)\mathrm{diag}(1/p_{i}) restricted to the tangent space of the simplex. It weights changes in each token’s probability inversely by its current mass, so the same absolute change contributes more at a low-probability token than at a high-probability one.
Square-root coordinates and the spherical geometry.
Let ui=piu_{i}=\sqrt{p_{i}}. Then
d​ui=12​pi​d​pi,(d​pi)2pi=4​(d​ui)2,du_{i}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{p_{i}}}\,dp_{i},\qquad\frac{(dp_{i})^{2}}{p_{i}}=4\,(du_{i})^{2},
so that
d​s2=4​∑i(d​ui)2.ds^{2}=4\sum_{i}(du_{i})^{2}.
(12)
The normalization ∑ipi=1\sum_{i}p_{i}=1 becomes ∑iui2=1\sum_{i}u_{i}^{2}=1. Thus, the map 𝐩↦𝐩\mathbf{p}\mapsto\sqrt{\mathbf{p}} sends the interior of the simplex onto the strictly positive orthant of the unit hypersphere 𝕊|𝒱|−1\mathbb{S}^{|\mathcal{V}|-1}. By equation 12, the Fisher metric is four times the round metric on this sphere, so the Fisher–Rao distance between two distributions is twice the length of the shortest spherical arc connecting their square-root representations.
Chord and arc distances.
The square-root representations 𝐩\sqrt{\mathbf{p}} and 𝐪\sqrt{\mathbf{q}} lie on the unit hypersphere. We can measure their separation by the straight-line chord or the shortest arc along the surface. Both depend only on the overlap
ρ⁡(𝐩,𝐪):=∑ipi​qi=cos⁡∠⁡(𝐩,𝐪),\rho(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}):=\sum_{i}\sqrt{p_{i}q_{i}}=\cos\angle(\sqrt{\mathbf{p}},\sqrt{\mathbf{q}}),
known as the Bhattacharyya coefficient.
Half the squared chord length gives the squared Hellinger divergence:
DH​(𝐩,𝐪)=12​‖𝐩−𝐪‖22=1−ρ⁡(𝐩,𝐪).D_{H}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\|\sqrt{\mathbf{p}}-\sqrt{\mathbf{q}}\|_{2}^{2}=1-\rho(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}).
(13)
The shortest arc has length arccos⁡ρ⁡(𝐩,𝐪)\arccos\rho(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}) on the unit hypersphere. Accounting for the factor of 44 in the metric, the Fisher–Rao distance is twice this length:
dF​R​(𝐩,𝐪)=2​arccos⁡ρ⁡(𝐩,𝐪).d_{FR}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})=2\arccos\rho(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}).
(14)
Since ρ∈[0,1]\rho\in[0,1], both quantities are bounded: DH∈[0,1]D_{H}\in[0,1] and dF​R∈[0,π]d_{FR}\in[0,\pi]. We use the squared Hellinger divergence for distillation and the squared Fisher–Rao distance to penalize displacement from a recent checkpoint.
A.3 Local equivalence of the divergences
Near agreement, squared Hellinger divergence, squared Fisher–Rao distance, and both KL directions share the same quadratic term up to scale. Fix 𝐩\mathbf{p} in the interior of the simplex and write 𝐪=𝐩+ϵ\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{p}+\bm{\epsilon}, where ∑iϵi=0\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}=0 and ϵ\bm{\epsilon} is sufficiently small that 𝐪\mathbf{q} remains in the interior. Let
Q⁡(ϵ):=∑iϵi2piQ(\bm{\epsilon}):=\sum_{i}\frac{\epsilon_{i}^{2}}{p_{i}}
denote the Fisher quadratic form of equation 11. Expanding to second order gives

(15)

=12​Q​(ϵ)+O⁡(‖ϵ‖3),\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\,Q(\bm{\epsilon})+O(\|\bm{\epsilon}\|^{3}),

(16)
dF​R2​(𝐩,𝐪)\displaystyle d_{FR}^{2}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})

(17)
The KL expansions follow by expanding log⁡(1+ϵi/pi)\log(1+\epsilon_{i}/p_{i}) and using ∑iϵi=0\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}=0. For Hellinger and Fisher–Rao, expanding

After accounting for scale, the squared chord and arc distances agree through third order and first differ at fourth order.
These expansions show that the losses have the same local geometry up to scale. Their differences become relevant away from agreement, where the quadratic approximation may no longer describe them well. This motivates examining their behavior at the low-overlap states encountered in privileged-context OPSD, where the teacher can assign high probability to tokens the student barely supports. The following sections compare the divergences and their gradients in this regime.
A.4 Hellinger and the two KL directions
The two KL directions.
Distillation can match the teacher qq and student pθp_{\theta} in either KL direction. The forward direction,
KL(q∥pθ)=∑iqilogqipθ​(i),\mathrm{KL}(q\|p_{\theta})=\sum_{i}q_{i}\log\frac{q_{i}}{p_{\theta}(i)},
(18)
diverges if the student assigns zero probability to any token with positive teacher probability. It therefore penalizes missing teacher-supported mass, a property often described as mass-covering or zero-avoiding. The reverse direction,
KL(pθ∥q)=∑ipθ(i)logpθ​(i)qi,\mathrm{KL}(p_{\theta}\|q)=\sum_{i}p_{\theta}(i)\log\frac{p_{\theta}(i)}{q_{i}},
(19)
instead diverges if the student assigns positive probability to a token with zero teacher probability. Because its terms are weighted by student mass, it places less pressure on teacher-supported regions to which the student assigns little probability. When the student cannot represent the teacher distribution exactly, this asymmetry can favor concentrating on a subset of teacher modes, a tendency known as mode-seeking. These descriptions characterize tendencies under approximation constraints; both KL directions are minimized at pθ=qp_{\theta}=q when an exact match is possible.
Hellinger within the α\alpha-divergence family.
The two KL directions arise as limits of the α\alpha-divergences,
Dα(𝐩∥𝐪)=41−α2(1−∑ipi(1−α)/2qi(1+α)/2),α∈(−1,1).D_{\alpha}(\mathbf{p}\|\mathbf{q})=\frac{4}{1-\alpha^{2}}\left(1-\sum_{i}p_{i}^{(1-\alpha)/2}\,q_{i}^{(1+\alpha)/2}\right),\qquad\alpha\in(-1,1).
(20)
For strictly positive distributions, the limits are
limα→1Dα(𝐩∥𝐪)=KL(𝐪∥𝐩),limα→−1Dα(𝐩∥𝐪)=KL(𝐩∥𝐪).\lim_{\alpha\to 1}D_{\alpha}(\mathbf{p}\|\mathbf{q})=\mathrm{KL}(\mathbf{q}\|\mathbf{p}),\qquad\lim_{\alpha\to-1}D_{\alpha}(\mathbf{p}\|\mathbf{q})=\mathrm{KL}(\mathbf{p}\|\mathbf{q}).
At α=0\alpha=0, the family recovers squared Hellinger divergence up to scale:
D0(𝐩∥𝐪)=4(1−∑ipi​qi)=4DH(𝐩,𝐪).D_{0}(\mathbf{p}\|\mathbf{q})=4\left(1-\sum_{i}\sqrt{p_{i}q_{i}}\right)=4\,D_{H}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}).
(21)
The family satisfies
Dα(𝐩∥𝐪)=D−α(𝐪∥𝐩)D_{\alpha}(\mathbf{p}\|\mathbf{q})=D_{-\alpha}(\mathbf{q}\|\mathbf{p}),
so α=0\alpha=0 is its self-dual member: exchanging the student and teacher leaves the divergence unchanged. Hellinger is therefore the symmetric midpoint in this parameterization. Its implications for teacher influence follow from the gradient, which we examine next.
A.5 Per-token teacher influence
The Hellinger gradient makes its dependence on student–teacher overlap explicit. Differentiating the Hellinger term in equation 13 while holding the teacher distribution qq fixed gives the form used in equation 4,
∇θDH(pθ,q)=−12∑i∈𝒱pθ​(i)​qi∇θlogpθ(i),\nabla_{\theta}D_{H}(p_{\theta},q)=-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\sqrt{p_{\theta}(i)\,q_{i}}\;\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(i),
(22)
using ∇θpθ​(i)=12​pθ​(i)​∇θ​log⁡pθ​(i)\nabla_{\theta}\sqrt{p_{\theta}(i)}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{p_{\theta}(i)}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(i).
The three matching losses considered here can all be written in weighted-score form:
∇θℒ=−∑i∈𝒱wi∇θlogpθ(i).\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}=-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}w_{i}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(i).
Their weights are
Forward KL KL(q∥pθ):\displaystyle\text{Forward KL }\ \mathrm{KL}(q\|p_{\theta}):\quad
wi=qi,\displaystyle w_{i}=q_{i},
(23)
Reverse KL KL(pθ∥q):\displaystyle\text{Reverse KL }\ \mathrm{KL}(p_{\theta}\|q):\quad
wi=pθ(i)(logqipθ​(i)+KL(pθ∥q)),\displaystyle w_{i}=p_{\theta}(i)\!\left(\log\tfrac{q_{i}}{p_{\theta}(i)}+\mathrm{KL}(p_{\theta}\|q)\right),
(24)
Hellinger ​DH​(pθ,q):\displaystyle\text{Hellinger }\ D_{H}(p_{\theta},q):\quad
wi=12​pθ​(i)​qi.\displaystyle w_{i}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{p_{\theta}(i)\,q_{i}}.
(25)
The term KL(pθ∥q)\mathrm{KL}(p_{\theta}\|q) in equation 24 centers the weights so that ∑iwi=0\sum_{i}w_{i}=0. Adding any token-independent scalar to the log-ratio term leaves the weighted-score sum unchanged, since
∑ipθ​(i)​∇θ​log⁡pθ​(i)=0\sum_{i}p_{\theta}(i)\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(i)=0.
With the stated baseline, wi<0w_{i}<0 whenever
log(pθ(i)/qi)>KL(pθ∥q)\log(p_{\theta}(i)/q_{i})>\mathrm{KL}(p_{\theta}\|q).
A positive weight contributes an update in the direction of increasing log⁡pθ​(i)\log p_{\theta}(i), but the net effect also depends on the other tokens. For example, if pθ=softmax⁡(z)p_{\theta}=\mathrm{softmax}(z), then
∂ℒ∂zi=pθ​(i)​∑jwj−wi.\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z_{i}}=p_{\theta}(i)\sum_{j}w_{j}-w_{i}.
For Hellinger, this becomes
∂DH​(pθ,q)∂zi=12​(ρ⁡(pθ,q)​pθ​(i)−pθ​(i)​qi).\frac{\partial D_{H}(p_{\theta},q)}{\partial z_{i}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\rho(p_{\theta},q)\,p_{\theta}(i)-\sqrt{p_{\theta}(i)\,q_{i}}\right).
The weights differ in how they treat teacher preferences with little student support. Forward KL assigns weight qiq_{i} regardless of pθ​(i)p_{\theta}(i), so a teacher-preferred token retains its weight even as the student’s probability approaches zero. Reverse KL weights the log-ratio signal by student probability; for a fixed strictly positive teacher distribution, this weight vanishes as pθ​(i)→0p_{\theta}(i)\to 0. Hellinger’s weight also vanishes in this limit, but scales as the geometric mean pθ​(i)​qi\sqrt{p_{\theta}(i)q_{i}}. For fixed teacher probability, it increases with the square root of student probability, allowing greater influence as the student assigns more mass to that token.
These expressions explain how Hellinger attenuates teacher preferences with little student support, as illustrated in Figure 1A. They do not, by themselves, imply that the parameter-gradient norm grows monotonically as overlap falls for forward KL, or establish a parameter-independent bound for Hellinger: the score vectors also determine the norm. Figure 2A shows the empirical relationship.
A.6 Natural-gradient update and the checkpoint pullback
Both terms of the objective compare predictive distributions using the geometry of §A.2. This also motivates using the Fisher metric to scale the update: parameter steps of equal Euclidean length can produce very different changes in the predictions.
The proximal term has a local Fisher form.
We analyze the proximal penalty with the sampled trajectories held fixed, consistent with the training objective. Let
Δk:=θk−θckpt,\Delta_{k}:=\theta_{k}-\theta_{\mathrm{ckpt}},
and write F⁡(θ,s)F(\theta;s) for the Fisher information of the next-token distribution πθ(⋅∣s)\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s) at a fixed state s=(x,y<t)s=(x,y_{<t}). A second-order expansion around the checkpoint gives
dF​R2(πθk(⋅∣s),πθckpt(⋅∣s))=Δk⊤F(θckpt;s)Δk+O(∥Δk∥3).d_{FR}^{2}\!\left(\pi_{\theta_{k}}(\cdot\mid s),\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{ckpt}}}(\cdot\mid s)\right)=\Delta_{k}^{\top}F(\theta_{\mathrm{ckpt}};s)\Delta_{k}+O(\|\Delta_{k}\|^{3}).
Let FckptF_{\mathrm{ckpt}} denote the average of
F⁡(θckpt,s)F(\theta_{\mathrm{ckpt}};s) over these states, using the same weighting as the proximal loss. Averaging the expansion gives
ℒprox​(θk,θckpt)≈Δk⊤​Fckpt​Δk.\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prox}}(\theta_{k};\theta_{\mathrm{ckpt}})\approx\Delta_{k}^{\top}F_{\mathrm{ckpt}}\Delta_{k}.
(26)
Thus, locally,
∇θℒprox​(θk,θckpt)≈2​Fckpt​Δk.\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prox}}(\theta_{k};\theta_{\mathrm{ckpt}})\approx 2\,F_{\mathrm{ckpt}}\Delta_{k}.
The matrix FckptF_{\mathrm{ckpt}} appears only in this local analysis; the algorithm does not need to construct it. Training evaluates the proximal penalty directly from the student and checkpoint distributions.
The ideal natural-gradient contribution.
Let FkF_{k} denote the current policy Fisher, evaluated at θk\theta_{k} and averaged over the same states. Assuming FkF_{k} is invertible, applying the natural-gradient preconditioner to the local proximal gradient gives
−η​λ​Fk−1​∇θℒprox​(θk,θckpt)≈−2​η​λ​Fk−1​Fckpt​Δk.-\eta\lambda\,F_{k}^{-1}\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prox}}(\theta_{k};\theta_{\mathrm{ckpt}})\approx-2\eta\lambda\,F_{k}^{-1}F_{\mathrm{ckpt}}\Delta_{k}.
(27)
Here, FckptF_{\mathrm{ckpt}} weights parameter displacement by its effect on the checkpoint’s predictions, while Fk−1F_{k}^{-1} scales the update according to the current policy’s geometry.
If Fk−1​Fckpt≈IF_{k}^{-1}F_{\mathrm{ckpt}}\approx I, the proximal contribution is approximately
−2​η​λ​(θk−θckpt).-2\eta\lambda(\theta_{k}-\theta_{\mathrm{ckpt}}).
This has the same form as a Euclidean gradient step on
λ​∥θ−θckpt∥22\lambda\lVert\theta-\theta_{\mathrm{ckpt}}\rVert_{2}^{2}.
The equivalence is about the local update, not the penalty itself. The Fisher–Rao penalty still compares predictive distributions. If the Fisher is singular, a pseudoinverse restricts this interpretation to the identifiable parameter directions.
The implemented preconditioner.
In practice, we use a damped, block-diagonal K-FAC construction described in Appendix B.6. Writing the implemented preconditioning operator as PkP_{k}, its local proximal contribution is
−η​λ​Pk​∇θℒprox​(θk,θckpt)≈−2​η​λ​Pk​Fckpt​Δk.-\eta\lambda P_{k}\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prox}}(\theta_{k};\theta_{\mathrm{ckpt}})\approx-2\eta\lambda P_{k}F_{\mathrm{ckpt}}\Delta_{k}.

B.1 Models

B.2 Data and privileged context
We sample 11K problems from the English-filtered portion of DAPO-Math, which provides verifiable integer answers but no reference solutions. During training, we draw the privileged context cc from the verified solutions for the current problem, c∼𝒞(⋅∣x)c\sim\mathcal{C}(\cdot\mid x).

Each distillation update uses a batch of 88 problems. The student samples one rollout per problem from the question alone. The teacher uses the same model parameters, receives a sampled verified solution as additional context, and scores the student’s trajectory at each response position. GRPO instead samples G=8G=8 rollouts for one problem per update. This matches the number of generated completions per update, although the number of prompts differs. For both distillation and GRPO, rollouts are sampled from the current student at each step, using temperature 1.01.0 and a maximum response length of 40964096 tokens.

We use top-KK truncation with K=1024K=1024 when computing the divergence losses. For GeoSD, the retained token set at each position is the union of the student, teacher, and checkpoint top-KK sets. This set contains >99.9%>99.9\% of the teacher’s probability mass at the loss-driving states, measured before renormalization. We then renormalize each distribution over the shared set by applying a restricted softmax.

Concurrent work.
Several concurrent studies explore related ways to regulate OPD. For distillation from an external teacher, Yu et al. (2026) handle degenerate agreement by combining bounded Hellinger shaping for student-excess tokens with a teacher top-KK injection that restores probability mass on underweighted teacher-preferred tokens. Xie et al. (2026) interpolate the student and teacher distributions into a proximal teacher, then use trust-region updates to train the student. Plyusov et al. (2026) change the rollout policy during warmup, moving it toward the teacher within a student-centered KL trust region but keep the distillation loss fixed. In privileged-context OPSD, Liu et al. (2026) combine verifier-based policy gradients with full-vocabulary reverse-KL matching and KL regularization to a fixed reference policy. We instead study OOD generalization under privileged-context self-distillation, using overlap-aware matching and a moving checkpoint penalty.
Unlike Yu et al. (2026), who apply Hellinger shaping to selected sampled tokens under a fixed external teacher, we match full next-token distributions in a setting where the teacher shares the student’s parameters, so overlap weighting alone slows but does not stop drift from compounding (Figure 2B). The gains depend on combining Hellinger matching with the checkpoint penalty. With K-FAC held fixed, Hellinger matching alone reaches 53.853.8 OOD accuracy, and the penalty with forward KL reaches only 50.650.6, below the base model’s 51.951.9, whereas together they reach 60.560.5 (Table 2).
B.6 Kronecker factorization
GeoSD uses a preconditioner based on K-FAC (Martens and Grosse, 2015). We construct its factors from activations and pre-activation gradients obtained by backpropagating the training loss on the on-policy batch. This reuses the existing backward pass.
For a linear layer W∈ℝdout×dinW\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm out}\times d_{\rm in}}, let aa denote the input activation and δ\delta the gradient of the training loss with respect to the layer’s pre-activation.
We form the second-moment factors
A=𝔼^​[a​a⊤]∈ℝdin×din,G=𝔼^​[δ​δ⊤]∈ℝdout×dout,A=\hat{\mathbb{E}}[aa^{\top}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm in}\times d_{\rm in}},\qquad G=\hat{\mathbb{E}}[\delta\delta^{\top}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm out}\times d_{\rm out}},
where 𝔼^\hat{\mathbb{E}} averages over sampled token positions in the training batch. With column-wise vectorization of WW, the Kronecker-factored matrix is A⊗GA\otimes G.
We maintain exponential moving averages of these factors and apply factored damping. Using AA and GG to denote the block-diagonal running estimates, the preconditioned gradient is
∇Wℒ~=(G+γ​I)−1​(∇Wℒ)​(A+γ​I)−1.\widetilde{\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L}}=(G+\sqrt{\gamma}\,I)^{-1}(\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L})(A+\sqrt{\gamma}\,I)^{-1}.
This follows from the Kronecker inverse identity and requires solving with the two factors rather than the full matrix. We apply this preconditioner to the seven projections (Q,K,V,O,gate,up,downQ,K,V,O,\mathrm{gate},\mathrm{up},\mathrm{down}) in each transformer block. Factor statistics are accumulated on a 1/81/8 subsample of token positions, with γ=10−3\gamma=10^{-3}. We use block-diagonal factors stored in fp32 with g=16g=16 for models up to 88B, and in bf16 with g=32g=32 for the 1414B and 3232B models. The memory requirements are reported in Appendix D, Table 12.

C.2 Teacher top-token support
To characterize teacher–student mismatch at initialization, we use Qwen3-8B and collect the top 10%10\% of response positions by π0\pi_{0} next-token entropy (as in §4.1) from the initial on-policy training batch, before any parameter updates. Figure 8 shows the joint density of these probabilities. The dashed diagonal marks equal teacher and student probability on the selected token. Positions satisfying q⋆≥0.8q_{\star}\geq 0.8 and p⋆≤0.1p_{\star}\leq 0.1 account for 27%27\% of these high-entropy positions. We express optimizer and scoring costs in model-pass units, where one unit is N​BtokNB_{\rm tok} FLOPs, NN is the parameter count, and BtokB_{\rm tok} is the number of student-sequence tokens per update. Under this approximate accounting, a forward pass costs 22 units and a backward pass 44. We assign 33 units to teacher scoring to account for its longer, privileged-context input. GeoSD additionally requires a checkpoint forward pass and K-FAC preconditioning. Rollout generation uses the same sampling settings across methods and is included in the runtime measurements but excluded from these unit counts. The Hellinger loss adds negligible computation relative to the model passes. The truncation width KK does not change the leading-order model-pass counts because every forward pass computes the full-vocabulary output head before truncation.
D.1 Memory
Checkpoint placement.
The checkpoint is used only for forward passes that produce stop-gradient target logits. It requires no parameter gradients or optimizer state and retains no activations for backward. Its persistent weight storage is 2​N2N bytes in bf16 (16.416.4 GB at 8B), as counted in Table 11; forward-pass buffers are temporary. By default, we place the checkpoint on inference workers, where it scores each rollout after generation. Its retained logits (<1<1 GB per batch) are transferred to the training workers with the batch, avoiding a second model copy on those workers. The additional persistent state on the training workers is therefore the 10.910.9 GB of K-FAC factors and inverses (≈8%\approx 8\% of the baseline footprint). We refresh θckpt\theta_{\mathrm{ckpt}} every KckptK_{\mathrm{ckpt}} updates using the existing trainer-to-inference weight synchronization path. Each refresh copies the student’s current bf16 compute weights without further quantization.

D.2 Runtime
On H200 GPUs, an 8B baseline run requires approximately 77 GPU-h for training and 2828 GPU-h for evaluation (avg@1616/pass@1616). The checkpoint forward pass and K-FAC operations increase GeoSD’s per-update optimizer and scoring FLOPs by 26%26\% (1.26×1.26\times) relative to standard OPSD. The measured wall-clock increase is approximately 10%10\% (1.10×1.10\times), with checkpoint scoring on inference workers overlapping rollout generation. Generation dominates runtime and uses the same decoding settings across methods. Across the nine post-training methods, training and evaluation require approximately 6363 and 252252 GPU-h, respectively. Including approximately 2020 GPU-h for offline construction of the shared privileged-solution pool, the comparison costs approximately 31703170 GPU-h for 1010 runs with different seeds.
Appendix E Limitations
Learning beyond the base model.
GeoSD encourages small, gradual changes to the student’s predictions. This may hurt learning when a correct continuation initially has very low student probability. Overlap weighting attenuates the teacher’s preference and the proximal penalty discourages large departures from recent predictions. Although this can protect the student from harmful changes in settings with privileged context, both terms can also hold back useful corrections.
We also exclude training problems for which the base model produces no verified solution within the sampling budget. It remains open whether GeoSD can learn from solutions to problems the base model cannot solve within that budget.
Limits of geometric control.
The moving checkpoint discourages local predictive drift but does not impose a hard bound on drift from the initial model. The checkpoint and student can drift together over longer training runs. The penalty is also evaluated only on sampled training prefixes, leaving changes at other states unconstrained by it.
Scope and interpretation of the evidence.
We evaluate mathematics across model families and scales, but code generation and teacher ablations are limited to Qwen3-8B. The concentration and false-consensus analyses cover mathematics only. They associate these behaviors with OOD performance but do not establish that preserving alternative token mass causes the accuracy gains.
We have not tested whether the same explanation applies to code or whether GeoSD preserves performance on tasks beyond the evaluated benchmarks.
