Abstract
Cross-tokenizer on-policy distillation (OPD) requires aligning teacher and student sequences before their scores can be compared. Decoded-text alignment provides such correspondence at the chunk level, but leaves a distinct problem unresolved: credit alignment. A chunk-level signal specifies how much a span should change, but not how that signal should be distributed across student tokens, while tokens without teacher correspondence remain unconstrained. We propose CAST (Chunk-aware credit Assignment with Selective Token Anchoring), a shift-or-stay framework that addresses both issues. For aligned chunks, CAST redistributes each chunk’s advantage across student tokens using predictive uncertainty and local teacher-signal smoothness, emphasizing learning opportunities supported by locally consistent teacher feedback while preserving the chunk’s total advantage. The resulting weights exactly solve a KL-regularized credit-allocation problem. For unaligned positions, CAST selectively anchors the student to its initial policy through a KL penalty, limiting drift where teacher supervision is unavailable. Because credit assignment is separated from chunk construction, CAST also applies to punctuation-defined or fixed-length chunks under shared tokenization. Across six mathematics, science, and code benchmarks, CAST improves the average over the strongest evaluated baseline by 2.452.45 and 1.321.32 points for cross-tokenizer transfer from Qwen3-8B to Mistral-7B and Llama-3.1-8B, respectively, and by 2.102.10 points for shared-tokenizer transfer to Qwen3-1.7B.

1 Introduction
On-policy distillation (OPD) trains a student on trajectories sampled from its
own policy using feedback from a stronger teacher
(Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024; Ko et al., 2024; Jang et al., 2026).
Because supervision is applied to states the student actually visits, OPD can
target student-specific errors and reduce the train–inference mismatch induced
by learning only from fixed teacher-generated responses. It has therefore
become an increasingly useful paradigm for language-model post-training,
particularly for transferring reasoning ability
(Yang et al., 2025; Bousselham et al., 2026).
A basic difficulty arises when teacher and student use different tokenizers.
With a shared tokenizer, the token-wise log-density ratio
log⁡πT​(yt∣x,y<t)−log⁡πθold​(yt∣x,y<t)\log\pi_{T}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})-\log\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})
compares probabilities of the same next-token event. Across tokenizers, however,
the same text can be segmented differently, so one student token may correspond
to multiple teacher tokens, or vice versa. Teacher and student logits are also
defined over different vocabularies, making direct token-wise comparison
ill-defined. As shown in Figure 2, only 19.3%19.3\% of the
Qwen3-8B vocabulary has a decoded-content match in
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, compared with 72.3%72.3\% in Llama-3.1-8B.
Moreover, vocabulary overlap alone does not imply token correspondence:
identical IDs need not represent the same event, and identical text can still
admit different segmentations.
Existing cross-tokenizer distillation methods address this mismatch by
constructing a common space in which teacher and student scores can be compared
(Boizard et al., 2025; Cui et al., 2025; Minixhofer et al., 2026; Singh et al., 2026).
Dynamic Probabilistic Cross-tokenizer Alignment (DPCA), for example, extends
decoded-text alignment to OPD by matching teacher and student sequences into
text-equivalent chunks and aggregating their token probabilities within each
chunk (Niu et al., 2026). Such methods establish
chunk-level alignment: the teacher and student scores now refer to the
same decoded text.
Our key observation is that chunk alignment does not resolve credit
alignment. Alignment determines what teacher and student quantities
are comparable, but OPD ultimately optimizes individual student-token
probabilities. A chunk-level discrepancy therefore still has to be translated
into token-level updates. Uniform propagation gives every student token in a
chunk the same advantage, implicitly treating all positions as equally useful
and equally reliable learning targets. This need not hold. Some positions are
already predicted confidently by the student, whereas others are uncertain and
offer greater opportunity for correction. At the same time, high student
uncertainty alone is insufficient: it can coincide with abrupt local variation
in the teacher-derived signal, where aggressively concentrating credit may be
undesirable. A second issue appears outside aligned regions. Tokens without
teacher correspondence receive no direct supervision, yet their predictions can
still drift because they share parameters with supervised positions. Thus,
decoded-text alignment leaves two coupled optimization questions:
where should aligned chunk credit be assigned, and how should the student
behave where teacher evidence is absent?
We address these questions with
CAST (Chunk-aware credit Assignment with
Selective Token Anchoring), a token-level
shift-or-stay framework for OPD
(Figure 1).
At aligned positions, CAST selectively shifts the student toward the
teacher by redistributing each chunk’s advantage across its student tokens.
Its credit rule combines two complementary signals:
student predictive uncertainty identifies where learning may be useful,
while local teacher-signal smoothness identifies where that supervision
is locally consistent. Their product therefore favors uncertain student
predictions supported by a stable local teacher signal. Normalization within
each chunk preserves the chunk’s total advantage, so CAST changes only
where the credit is assigned, not how much aggregate credit the
chunk receives. Moreover, the resulting weights exactly solve a
KL-regularized allocation problem that trades off an uncertainty-based prior
against local teacher-signal variation, with bounded relative reweighting from
the smoothness term.
At unaligned positions, CAST instead encourages the student to stay
close to its initial policy. Selective token anchoring applies a KL penalty
toward the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) initialization only where aligned
teacher supervision is unavailable, constraining incidental drift without
directly competing with teacher-guided updates. Together, the two components
separate the roles of teacher supervision and reference preservation:
shift where teacher evidence is available, stay where it is not.
This perspective also makes CAST applicable beyond tokenizer mismatch.
Credit assignment is conceptually separate from how chunks are constructed.
Decoded-text alignment provides chunks under heterogeneous tokenization, but
the same allocation rule can operate on punctuation-defined or fixed-length
chunks when teacher and student share a tokenizer. CAST therefore addresses a
more general problem: converting span-level learning signals into structured
token-level updates. We additionally find that multi-rollout sampling can
further improve performance by exposing the credit mechanism to multiple
student trajectories per prompt.
We evaluate CAST on six benchmarks spanning mathematics, science, and code:
AIME 2024, AIME 2025, AIME 2026 (Dekoninck et al., 2026), MATH-500
(Hendrycks et al., 2021), GPQA-Diamond
(Rein et al., 2024), and LiveCodeBench
(Jain et al., 2025).
For cross-tokenizer distillation from Qwen3-8B to Mistral-7B and
Llama-3.1-8B, CAST improves the six-benchmark average over the strongest
evaluated baseline by 2.452.45 and 1.321.32 points, respectively. Under shared
tokenization, CAST improves the corresponding average for Qwen3-1.7B by
2.102.10 points. On Mistral, its advantage over uniform and uncertainty-only
credit controls persists across evaluated seeds, together with fewer observed
training collapses
(Table 6; Appendix E). On Llama,
CAST also outperforms ULD across all three evaluated seeds
(Table 6; Appendix E).
Our contributions are:
• 
We distinguish chunk-level alignment from
credit alignment in cross-tokenizer OPD. Chunk alignment establishes
comparable teacher and student signals, but leaves their token-level
allocation and the behavior of unaligned positions unresolved.
• 
We propose CAST, a shift-or-stay framework that allocates
chunk credit according to student uncertainty and local teacher-signal
smoothness while selectively anchoring unaligned positions. The allocation
preserves each chunk’s total advantage and admits an exact
KL-regularized optimization interpretation.
• 
We show that credit alignment is useful beyond heterogeneous
tokenization by applying the same rule to punctuation-defined and
fixed-length chunks under shared tokenization. Across six benchmarks,
CAST outperforms the strongest evaluated baseline for both heterogeneous
students and shared-tokenizer Qwen3-1.7B, with additional studies of
robustness, training dynamics, and component interactions.
2 Related Work
On-policy distillation.
Generalized knowledge distillation (GKD) supervises student-generated sequences
with teacher token distributions and serves as our shared-tokenizer baseline
(Agarwal et al., 2024). MiniLLM adopts a reverse-KL objective, while
DistiLLM and DistiLLM-2 develop streamlined and contrastive objectives
(Gu et al., 2024; Ko et al., 2024; Ko et al., 2025). KDRL combines distillation with
reinforcement learning for reasoning post-training
(Xu et al., 2025). Training stability has also been
studied through group-relative baselines from multiple responses per prompt
and adaptive target reformulation (Guo et al., 2025; Jang et al., 2026).
These methods assume a shared tokenizer. In this setting, CAST retains its
token-level credit rule and uses punctuation-defined or fixed-length chunks.
Figure 3: CAST: token-level shift-or-stay optimization.
Shift normalizes uncertainty uiu_{i} times smoothness sis_{i} to allocate
token credit while preserving each chunk’s advantage. Stay anchors
unaligned predictions to the initial policy. Heterogeneous pairs use decoded-text
alignment and fixed-length credit chunks; shared-tokenizer training uses
punctuation-defined chunks here and regularizes all positions.
Cross-tokenizer distillation and credit assignment.
ULD compares sorted logit distributions through
optimal transport, and MultiLevelOT extends transport across multiple
granularities (Boizard et al., 2025; Cui et al., 2025). ALM matches likelihoods
over shared text chunks, while byte-level distillation uses a
tokenizer-independent byte interface (Minixhofer et al., 2026; Singh et al., 2026).
Minimum edit-distance (MinED) token mapping supports knowledge fusion
(Wan et al., 2024). These methods primarily determine
what can be compared across tokenizers. DPCA extends decoded-text
alignment to on-policy distillation by matching teacher and student sequences
into text-equivalent chunks and propagating aggregate teacher scores
(Niu et al., 2026). Shared-tokenizer methods use local divergence
reweighting or adaptive policy distillation
(Jung et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025).
E-SCOT constrains offline optimal transport by character overlap and reweights
token-level costs using teacher–student Rényi-entropy gaps
(Liu et al., 2026). CAST addresses token-level
shift-or-stay optimization after decoded-text alignment. It redistributes
each chunk’s on-policy advantage to individual tokens, using uncertainty to
highlight potential learning opportunities and smoothness to temper credit
where teacher scores vary abruptly, while preserving the total advantage.
Selective KL anchoring keeps unaligned predictions close to the initial
student policy, constraining drift from shared parameter updates.

4 Experiments
4.1 Experimental Setup
Baselines.
Cross-tokenizer experiments distill Qwen3-8B into Mistral-7B and Llama-3.1-8B
(Grattafiori et al., 2024), both initialized by SFT on
400,000400{,}000 OpenThoughts examples (Guha et al., 2026).
Figure 2 characterizes tokenizer mismatch for these pairs.
Heterogeneous baselines include the student initialization, teacher-reward
GRPO, DPCA, minimum edit-distance alignment (MinED), Universal Logit
Distillation (ULD), Approximate Likelihood Matching (ALM), and a matched
uniform-credit control. DPCA is the decoded-text-alignment OPD baseline;
the uniform control splits each chunk advantage equally while retaining
CAST’s clipping, selective anchoring, and two-rollout sampling to isolate
its shift credit rule. Shared-tokenizer experiments pair
Qwen3-8B or Qwen3-30B-A3B teachers with Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B students,
using raw instruction models or the same 400K SFT initialization as marked
in each table, with GRPO, GKD, MiniLLM, and DistiLLM as baselines.
We also directly evaluate the official Hugging Face releases of Qwen3-8B,
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct without additional training.
Training data.
We randomly sample both v1 and v2 from OpenThoughts using seed 4444.
The prompt-only v1 set contains 20,00020{,}000 prompts, predominantly competition
mathematics, with estimated shares of 78.4%78.4\% mathematics, 17.4%17.4\% code,
≈3.2%\approx 3.2\% science, and ≈1.0%\approx 1.0\% commonsense or logic. Without domain
labels, we infer mathematics and code from the \boxed{
answer format and executable-Python-function template, respectively, and
estimate other domains manually. v2 contains 15,00015{,}000 OpenThoughts3-1.2M
prompts, with 5,0005{,}000 each
from mathematics, code, and science. Both sets use responses generated on-policy
by the current student and are reported separately.

Cross-tokenizer distillation.
Table 1 shows that CAST achieved the
highest six-benchmark average for both student families: 47.2247.22 for Mistral
and 43.8743.87 for Llama, improving over ULD by 2.452.45 and 1.321.32 points,
respectively. CAST led ULD on five benchmarks for Mistral and four for Llama;
MATH-500 was lower for both, as was GPQA for Llama. Relative to the SFT
initialization, CAST improved five of six Mistral scores and raised the
average from 44.3344.33 to 47.2247.22, while LCB declined slightly from 28.028.0
to 27.727.7. Llama
improved on all three AIME benchmarks and MATH-500, while GPQA and LCB declined;
its average increased from 42.0842.08 to 43.8743.87.
Original DPCA reached averages of 41.4041.40 and 41.7541.75, respectively.
Shared-tokenizer distillation.
With Qwen3-8B as teacher and v1 prompts, CAST improves all six
benchmarks over raw Qwen3-1.7B, reaching 48.6848.68 Avg-6 versus 46.5846.58 for the
strongest baseline, DistiLLM (Table 2). It exceeds uniform and
shuffled credit by 2.262.26 and 3.203.20 points (Table 3).
Shuffling preserves chunk weights but changes their token assignments,
supporting the importance of credit placement. Punctuation-defined chunks
outperform fixed-length chunks (K=6K{=}6) by 1.561.56 points, although the latter
score higher on LCB (35.135.1 versus 34.634.6). These controls support CAST’s
shift rule under exact token correspondence. Broader comparisons show
that its gains vary with teacher–student scale and prompt composition
(Tables 7 and 8 in Appendix B.1).
4.3 Discussions
Credit assignment beyond chunk-level alignment.
In Table 4, the matched uniform control retains
decoded-text alignment, selective anchoring, and two-rollout sampling but
removes structured token credit, reducing the average from 47.2247.22 to 45.0745.07.
Shuffling preserves CAST’s within-chunk weights but breaks their token
correspondence; CAST improves five of six benchmarks over this control, raising the
average from 44.1744.17 to 47.2247.22, with gains of 4.84.8 on AIME-2026 and 6.06.0 on
GPQA, while LCB is slightly lower (27.727.7 versus 27.927.9).
These controls support CAST’s token-specific shift credit.
Appendix B.3 illustrates its allocation within a chunk and
across token categories in one rollout (Figure 6).
Performance across training seeds.
Tables 6 and 6 summarize three
seeds per configuration. CAST reaches 47.01±0.2847.01\pm 0.28 Avg-6 on Mistral and
43.72±0.1643.72\pm 0.16 on Llama, versus 42.08±0.4342.08\pm 0.43 for Llama ULD.
For both students, all three CAST scores exceed the corresponding comparators’
highest Avg-6 (Tables 10–11 in
Appendix E).
CAST yields more consistent gradient directions.
Figure 4 compares four credit rules on shared rollouts at
SFT initialization and an OPD-uniform checkpoint. CAST has the highest
gradient signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and training–held-out mean-direction
agreement at both; SFT SNR rises from 0.1110.111 under uniform credit to 0.2560.256
under CAST (protocol in Appendix B.5).
Complementary training traces in Figure 5 show higher
teacher endorsement and policy entropy, smaller gradient norms, and lower
repetition than DPCA over the final 5050 updates.
Appendix B.4 details these runs; selected 2024 AIME I
responses in Appendix D illustrate CAST reaching final answers
where SFT or DPCA responses repeat or remain unfinished.
Smoothness helps mitigate training collapse.
Smoothness produces a more balanced domain profile by moderating
uncertainty-driven credit: with selective anchoring and two rollouts fixed,
LCB rises from 17.017.0 to 27.727.7 and Avg-6 from 45.6045.60 to 47.2247.22, while the
other five benchmarks change by −0.20-0.20 points on average
(Table 4). This moderation discounts uncertain tokens
when teacher scores vary sharply, redistributing credit toward more locally
regular supervision. It primarily uses teacher structure: 79%79\% of
redistribution occurs at teacher-variation positions, and comparable variation
persists without projection under shared tokenization
(Figure 7 in Appendix B.6).
Separate seed-4444 diagnostics show slightly broader effective credit support
for CAST (2.8142.814 tokens versus 2.6322.632 for uncertainty-only weighting;
Figure 8, Appendix E).
This broader, teacher-informed allocation offers a plausible explanation for
CAST’s fewer observed collapses than uncertainty-only credit across six seeds
(Table 12, Appendix E).
The role of selective anchoring.
With CAST credit and n=2n{=}2 fixed, selective anchoring reaches 47.2247.22 Avg-6,
versus 44.3044.30 with global anchoring and 44.0044.00 without it; the largest
selective–global gain is on LCB (+7.8+7.8; Table 4).
With selective anchoring, two rollouts outperform one (+1.15+1.15 Avg-6) and four
(Appendix B.2). Over the final 5050 updates, global anchoring has higher entropy and
lower repetition than CAST, yet weaker downstream performance
(Figure 5b,d),
consistent with overconstraint: global anchoring penalizes departures from SFT
at aligned positions, potentially opposing teacher credit. Selective anchoring
instead constrains drift at unaligned positions while leaving aligned positions
to teacher supervision.
5 Conclusion
We introduced CAST for token-level shift-or-stay cross-tokenizer OPD:
uncertainty–smoothness credit redistributes aligned chunk advantages, while
selective anchoring keeps unaligned predictions close to the initial policy.
The advantage-preserving credit rule admits an exact KL-regularized
characterization and extends to shared-tokenizer punctuation-defined or
fixed-length chunks. CAST improves the six-benchmark average over the strongest
evaluated baseline by 1.321.32–2.452.45 points for heterogeneous students
and 2.102.10 for Qwen3-1.7B. Controls support token-specific credit placement,
more consistent LM-head gradients, and gains across Mistral and Llama seeds.
Smoothness recovers code performance and broadens effective credit support,
alongside fewer observed collapses; selective anchoring improves the
two-rollout configuration.
Limitations
Our evaluation covers a limited set of model families and mainly
reasoning-oriented training data, so domain gains may change with student
capacity or prompt composition. Training compute varies across methods, and
repeated-seed performance covers four Mistral and two Llama configurations.
Broader model and data coverage,
matched-compute evaluation, and direct tests of how credit allocation affects
full-model optimization and training collapse remain future work.

Appendix A Additional Method Details
A.1 Chunk Construction
Heterogeneous tokenizers.
We use DPCA’s decoded-text chunk alignment (Niu et al., 2026):
a greedy two-pointer scan extends the shorter side until the decoded student
and teacher strings match after Unicode and tokenizer-specific whitespace
normalization. Each matched pair of contiguous token groups (Ic,Jc)(I_{c},J_{c})
forms an alignment chunk. We discard excessively large or unreliable
matches; retained student positions form 𝒜\mathcal{A} and the remainder
𝒰\mathcal{U}. Each retained alignment chunk’s teacher score is projected onto
the student grid as
ℓ~iT=1|Ic|​∑j∈JcℓjT,i∈Ic.\widetilde{\ell}_{i}^{T}=\frac{1}{|I_{c}|}\sum_{j\in J_{c}}\ell_{j}^{T},\qquad i\in I_{c}.
(12)
After the projection in Eq. (12), we regroup contiguous
aligned student tokens into fixed-length KK-token credit chunks (plus a
shorter residual chunk), collected in 𝒢\mathcal{G}. These define the units for
advantage computation and token-level credit allocation; their boundaries need
not coincide with alignment-chunk boundaries. We use K=6K=6 unless stated otherwise.
Shared tokenizer.
The training path uses identity correspondence between response tokens, so
M=NM=N, ℓ~iT=ℓiT\widetilde{\ell}_{i}^{T}=\ell_{i}^{T}, and 𝒰=∅\mathcal{U}=\varnothing.
We split responses after commas, periods, exclamation marks, and question marks
(with any trailing suffix as the final chunk); these punctuation-defined credit chunks
directly form 𝒢\mathcal{G} without fixed-KK regrouping.
The fixed-length variant in Table 3 instead partitions
the response into consecutive K=6K=6 token chunks, with a shorter final chunk
when needed.

Equation (12) in Appendix A.1
assigns each alignment chunk’s aggregate

alignment-chunk boundaries, the projected signal is constant within each
alignment chunk. The smoothness term could therefore reward alignment
geometry rather than genuine teacher structure. We test this explanation with
two analyses that require no retraining (Figure 7).
First, alignment chunks containing multiple student tokens cover 19%19\% of
student tokens in the cross-tokenizer rollouts. Defining smoothness-only credit as

and 1:11{:}1 positions), compared with 21%21\% at projection-flat alignment-chunk
interiors. The ϵ\epsilon floor is active in 36%36\% of credit chunks, limiting the influence of
small local variations.

64%64\%. The median within-chunk smoothness-weight spread is also similar

that smoothness responds primarily to teacher-signal variation, while
projection geometry contributes a minority of its observed re-weighting.

Figure 8: CAST has slightly broader effective credit support than
uncertainty-only weighting in seed-4444 diagnostic runs.
Using the seed-4444 diagnostic trajectories from Figure 5,
we compare uniform credit, uncertainty-only weighting, and CAST.
For each chunk, the effective token count is the inverse participation ratio
Neff​(g)=1/∑i∈gwi2N_{\mathrm{eff}}(g)=1/\sum_{i\in g}w_{i}^{2}, with normalized weights
wi=A^i/Agw_{i}=\widehat{A}_{i}/A_{g}.
It equals KgK_{g} under uniform allocation and approaches 11 as credit
concentrates on one token, rather than counting tokens above a threshold.
The monitor averages this quantity over chunks with Kg>1K_{g}>1 and
|Ag|>10−8|A_{g}|>10^{-8}; faint traces show the exported training-step values, and
the overlaid curves show five-step moving averages.
Credit concentration and training stability.
Over steps 11–250250 of the seed-4444 diagnostic runs, CAST averages 2.8142.814
effective tokens versus 2.6322.632 for uncertainty-only weighting, a 6.9%6.9\%
increase, while uniform credit averages 5.9995.999
(Figure 8). Alongside the lower observed collapse
count across six seeds (1/61/6 versus 2/62/6; Table 12), this
is consistent with smoothness moderating uncertainty-driven credit concentration.
The bound in Eq. (7) controls the relative reweighting
introduced by smoothness; it does not guarantee diffuse product weights.

that credit dispersion alone does not explain training stability.
