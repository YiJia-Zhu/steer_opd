                                                      On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) uses a self-teacher conditioned on privileged
                                                      information to turn the sparse outcome signal of reinforcement learning with ver-
                                                      ifiable rewards (RLVR) into dense token-level supervision. Yet the teacher’s ad-
                                                      vantage may depend on information unavailable to the student at inference, rais-
                                                      ing a central question: which privileged changes are worth distilling? We use
                                                      cross-problem recurrence as an empirical cue for potentially reusable guidance.
                                                      Comparing the representation shifts induced by full solutions and compact hints,
                                                      we find that hint-induced shifts are more concentrated and directionally consistent
                                                      across problems, and yield more sustained downstream gains despite revealing
                                                      less information. Motivated by this association, we propose Privileged Represen-
                                                      tation Isolation for On-Policy Self-Distillation (PreSO). At the source, PreSO
                                                      uses compact hints to limit the solution-specific detail entering the privileged shift.
                                                      At the target, it estimates a leading low-rank subspace from shifts pooled across
                                                      related problems, projects the privilege-induced correction onto this subspace, and
                                                      adds it to the student’s unprivileged representation to form the representation-level
                                                      distillation target. The selected subspace serves as an operational proxy for recur-
                                                      ring structure, rather than a guaranteed separation of transferable and instance-
                                                      specific information. On five competition-level mathematics benchmarks, PreSO
                                                      consistently outperforms strong baselines across Qwen3-1.7B, 4B, and 8B, im-
                                                      proving average accuracy over the base model by 6.8, 9.0, and 10.0 points. Be-
                                                      yond peak accuracy, PreSO attains the highest final-checkpoint average at every
                                                      model scale, with improvements that persist through the end of training.


                                         1        I NTRODUCTION

                                         Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has become a central paradigm for training
                                         reasoning models (Shao et al., 2024; DeepSeek-AI, 2025). However, RLVR typically derives its
                                         learning signal from a sequence-level outcome reward, making credit assignment challenging for
                                         long-horizon reasoning trajectories (Lightman et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). On-policy self-
                                         distillation (OPSD) (Zhao et al., 2026a; Ye et al., 2026) offers a denser alternative: conditioned on
                                         privileged context such as a reference solution, the same model acts as a self-teacher and provides
                                         token-level supervision on student-generated rollouts. This approach can improve both reasoning
                                         performance and sample efficiency over RLVR baselines (Hübotter et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026).
                                         To make better use of privileged teachers, prior work has improved OPSD through token reweighting
                                         (Zhang et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026), privileged context design (Ye et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a;
                                         Han et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026b), and integration with RLVR (Lu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b;
                                         Yang et al., 2026a), with recent efforts extending online distillation to the representation level (Li
                                         et al., 2026b). Other approaches contrast the teacher against a weaker reference to preserve the
                                         student’s own reasoning ability (Pan et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026c). These methods refine how
                                         privileged guidance is provided or matched, but leave a more basic question underexplored: which
                                         parts of a privileged teacher’s advantage should the student actually internalize? A privileged

teacher is stronger precisely because it accesses information the student cannot obtain at inference
time, yet the changes this information induces are not uniformly worth internalizing: some reflect
reasoning regularities a student could in principle recover once the privilege is removed, while others
are shortcuts tied to the known answer, a specific reference trajectory, or stylistic preferences of the
teacher itself, which improve the teacher’s trajectory under privileged conditions but do not tell the
student how to reason when no such reference is available. Without separating the two, the student
is pushed to imitate a pattern that can only be reproduced when privileged information is present.
The key question is not how much additional information the teacher receives, but whether the
resulting changes exhibit stable patterns across related problems. A change that recurs among prob-
lems with similar content or reasoning structure may reveal a shared solving cue that the student can
learn to use independently. By contrast, changes tied to a particular answer or reference trajectory
offer little consistent signal across training examples and may be difficult to reuse without privileged
information. We therefore use cross-problem recurrence to prioritize potentially reusable guidance,
and evaluate its value through the student’s performance without privileged information.
To examine privileged changes across problems, we turn to the model’s internal representations. For
each rollout prefix, we define the privileged shift as the difference between hidden states obtained
with and without privileged information under the same prompt template. This lets us aggregate and
compare shifts across problems. To probe how the detail of privileged context shapes these shifts,
we compare full solutions, which expose the complete derivation, with compact hints, which pro-
vide only the key strategy. Despite revealing more, solutions lead to a steady decline in downstream
performance, whereas hints yield sustained gains (Figure 2a). Hint-induced shifts concentrate along
fewer dominant directions (Figure 2b), with lower effective rank and greater directional consis-
tency than solution-induced shifts (Figure 2c). This contrast motivates using the leading low-rank
subspace of pooled hint-induced shifts as an operational proxy for cross-problem structure and pro-
jecting privileged corrections onto it before constructing the distillation target.
Guided by this analysis, we propose Privileged Representation Isolation for On-Policy Self-
Distillation (PreSO), which restricts privileged guidance at both the source and the representation-
level target. At the source, PreSO replaces full reference solutions with compact hints to limit depen-
dence on solution-specific details. At the target, it routes each problem to a content-defined cluster,
estimates the leading low-rank subspace of pooled hint-induced shifts, and projects the privilege-
induced correction onto that subspace before constructing the distillation target. After warm-up,
PreSO projects only the privilege-induced correction and adds it back to the student’s unprivileged
representation, selecting directions of additional guidance without projecting away the representa-

2.1   P RELIMINARIES

On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD). OPSD (Zhao et al., 2026a; Hübotter et al., 2026; Ye et al.,
2026) has recently emerged as a promising paradigm for on-policy knowledge distillation, offer-
ing dense token-level supervision while avoiding the training–inference distribution mismatch that
plagues off-policy KD (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024). Given a rollout y ∼ πθs (· | x), OPSD
constructs a privileged self-teacher by conditioning the same model on additional information ξ, and
matches πθs (· | x, y<t ) to πθs (· | x, ξ, y<t ) at each position t:

where sg(·) denotes the stop-gradient operator. Compared with standard RLVR methods that op-

However, privileged information can induce changes with different degrees of cross-problem con-
sistency. A change that recurs across related problems may provide a reusable solving cue, while
one tied to a particular answer or reference trajectory may offer little consistent guidance. We
therefore use recurrence as an empirical cue for prioritizing privileged changes and evaluate their
value through performance without privilege. Comparing these changes requires a common space
in which their effects across problems can be aligned and aggregated. The model’s internal repre-
sentations provide such a space, which we study next.

Representation Self-Distillation. Representation distillation supervises intermediate features or
attention patterns rather than relying only on output distributions (Romero et al., 2015; Jiao et al.,
2020; Wang et al., 2020). In our on-policy setting, hidden-state matching bypasses the LM head and
uses a stop-gradient target computed on each student-generated rollout ŷ ∼ πθs (· | x). Following
OPRD (Yang et al., 2026b), we match the student and privileged self-teacher representations at each
position t under the shared prefix ŷ<t with an MSE loss between ℓ2 -normalized states:

More detailed privilege does not necessarily yield better supervision. In this comparison, hints raise
downstream accuracy from roughly 14% to above 16% and sustain that gain, whereas solutions fall
to 2–3% (Figure 2a). To examine the corresponding geometry, we stack per-position shifts across
problems and project them onto their leading principal components. Hint-induced shifts occupy
a narrow, consistently oriented region along PC1, which explains 52.2% of the pooled variance,
while solution-induced shifts spread more broadly toward PC2, which explains 8.2% (Figure 2b).
Hint-induced shifts also have lower effective rank and greater directional consistency than solution-
induced shifts (Figure 2c). In this comparison, the more concentrated and consistent shifts accom-
pany the more sustained downstream gain. We therefore use the leading low-rank subspace of pooled
hint-induced shifts as an operational proxy for recurring structure in Section 2.3.

2.3                           P RIVILEGED S UBSPACE P ROJECTION

Guided by the geometry in Section 2.2, we use the leading low-rank subspace of pooled privileged
shifts as an operational proxy for cross-problem structure. PreSO projects the privilege-induced shift
onto this subspace before adding it to the unprivileged student representation to form a distillation
target. We next describe how the subspace is estimated and applied.

Estimating the privileged subspace. During the first W optimization steps, we collect roll-
outs from the evolving on-policy student πθs . For each visited prefix (xi , ŷ<t ), we evaluate the
parameter-shared self-teacher twice under an identical template that differs only in whether the priv-
ileged information ξi is exposed, giving the privileged shift:

where ∅ denotes the same template with an empty privileged field. Holding both the template and
the rollout prefix isolates the representational effect of privileged information. We stack the collected

3     E XPERIMENTS

3.1   E XPERIMENTAL S ETUP

Models and Datasets. We conduct experiments with Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-
8B (Team, 2025), disabling thinking mode throughout training and evaluation; results under the
thinking mode are reported in Appendix C.6. To test robustness across training data, we use two
training sets, DeepMath-103K (He et al., 2026) and Open-R1 (Yan et al., 2025). The main ta-
bles report results trained on DeepMath-103K, and the Open-R1 results are given in Appendix C.3.
For DeepMath, following RLCSD (Pan et al., 2026), we assign difficulty levels 5–7 to the 1.7B
model, levels 6–8 to the 4B model, and levels 7–10 to the 8B model. Each model is trained for
500 optimization steps. We evaluate on five competition-level mathematical reasoning benchmarks:
AIME24 (Zhang & Math-AI, 2024), AIME25 (Zhang & Math-AI, 2025), AIMO (AI-MO, 2024),
HMMT25 (Dekoninck et al., 2026), and AMC23 (MAA, 2023). For efficiency, the privileged hints
are annotated offline with GPT-5.5 before training, and Appendix C.4 reports a self-hint variant.

Baselines. We compare PreSO against four representative baselines. (1) GRPO (Shao et al.,
2024), an RLVR baseline that optimizes the policy with group-normalized advantages from veri-
fiable outcome rewards. (2) OPSD (Zhao et al., 2026a), an on-policy self-distillation method in
which one model acts as both privileged teacher and student and minimizes their per-token diver-
gence over its own rollouts. (3) RLSD (Yang et al., 2026a), a hybrid method that sizes each policy
update from the teacher-student token-level discrepancy while taking its direction from RLVR out-
come feedback. (4) RLCSD (Pan et al., 2026), a contrastive self-distillation method that conditions
the teacher on successful and unsuccessful on-policy sibling solutions and uses their likelihood con-
trast to refine the policy advantages on task-relevant tokens. For a fair comparison, OPSD and RLSD
in the main results receive the same hints as privileged information, and results using the full solution
are in Appendix C.2. Implementation details are in Appendix E.1.

3.2   M AIN RESULTS

PreSO outperforms existing baselines. Table 1 shows that PreSO achieves the strongest results
across all model scales and benchmarks. On Qwen3-8B, it raises average accuracy from 36.9 to
46.9, gaining +10.0 points over the base model and +6.4 over the strongest baseline, RLSD.
Average gains over the base model reach +9.0 points on Qwen3-4B and +6.8 on Qwen3-1.7B.

3.4                                    A BLATION S TUDIES

Ablation on the source-side abstraction of privileged information. We first replace compact
hints with full solutions while keeping the rest of PreSO unchanged (w/o PI abstraction). On
Qwen3-8B, this variant trails PreSO from the outset and declines most sharply, ending 15.3, 10.6,
and 12.9 points lower on AIME24, AIME25, and AMC23 at the last checkpoint. These are the
largest gaps among the ablations. To test whether the loss stems solely from the subspace estimated
from solution-induced shifts, we also estimate the subspace from hints while retaining solutions as
privileged context (w/o PI abs. (hint subspace)). This recovers only part of the gap and still trails
PreSO by 12.6 points on AIME24. The same pattern holds on Qwen3-1.7B. Together, these results
show that compact hints contribute beyond their effect on subspace estimation. They are consistent
with source-side abstraction reducing potential interference from solution-specific details, which
target-side projection alone does not fully compensate for.

its gap to PreSO on AIME25 widens from 3.4 points at the best checkpoint to 5.6 at the last. Similar

4                    R ELATED W ORK

On-policy self-distillation. On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) conditions the current policy on
privileged information or feedback to obtain a self-teacher that provides dense token-level supervi-
sion on student-generated rollouts, avoiding the distribution mismatch of off-policy teachers (Agar-
wal et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2026a; Hübotter et al., 2026). Prior work has improved this supervision
through privileged context design (Ye et al., 2026; Buening et al., 2026), token reweighting (Liu
et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026), and integration with RLVR (Yang et al., 2026a; Lu et al., 2026; Li
et al., 2026a). Other approaches selectively correct the teacher signal to reduce style- or reference-
dependent effects (Pan et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026c; Shen et al., 2026). These methods primarily
operate on token-level distributions or logits. We instead study how privileged information shifts
hidden representations across problems. PreSO uses compact hints to shape these shifts and esti-
mates a leading low-rank subspace from shifts pooled across related problems as an empirical proxy
for cross-problem structure. It then projects the privilege-induced correction onto this subspace
before constructing the representation-level target.

Hidden representation distillation. Representation distillation complements output-distribution
matching by aligning hidden states (Romero et al., 2015; Jiao et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2020) or
relations among representations (Park et al., 2019; Tian et al., 2019). Recent work has brought such
supervision to on-policy rollouts. OPRD (Yang et al., 2026b) aligns student and teacher hidden states
on shared rollouts, while PHF (Li et al., 2026b) studies privileged self-distillation through hidden-
state transitions and trajectory geometry. Our use of representations serves an additional purpose:
they provide a common space for comparing the changes induced by privileged information across

problems and assessing whether dominant directions recur among related problems. We analyze
these shifts to examine how their directional structure relates to downstream learning. Guided by
this analysis, PreSO uses the leading subspace of pooled shifts as an empirical proxy for recurring
structure and projects the privilege-induced correction before constructing its distillation target.

5   C ONCLUSION

In this paper, we revisit on-policy self-distillation by asking which privileged changes can help
a student reason without privileged information. We use cross-problem recurrence as an empirical
cue and examine privilege-induced representation shifts across problems. Hint-induced shifts exhibit
lower effective rank and greater directional consistency than solution-induced shifts, alongside more
sustained downstream gains. Motivated by this association, we propose PreSO, which uses compact
hints at the source and projects subsequent privilege-induced corrections onto per-cluster leading
subspaces before constructing representation-level targets. Across Qwen3-1.7B, 4B, and 8B on
five competition-level mathematics benchmarks, PreSO outperforms strong baselines and sustains
its gains through training. These results support the selected subspace as an effective operational
proxy for privileged corrections in our setting. We hope our findings motivate further study of
which privileged signals are worth internalizing into a student, and of principled ways to identify
the components of those signals that recur across problems.

A     L IMITATIONS
We discuss several limitations of PreSO that we leave to future work:
• First, all models are trained with thinking mode disabled, as in our main evaluations. Ap-
  pendix C.6 additionally evaluates Qwen3-1.7B with thinking mode enabled at inference. We have
  not studied training or distillation under thinking mode, and leave that setting for future work.
• Second, PreSO encourages the model to internalize a more complete reasoning process rather than
  shortcut to the answer, which tends to make its outputs longer and, correspondingly, increases
  training cost relative to methods that do not. This is a trade-off we accept for more reliable
  reasoning, and reducing this overhead while retaining the benefit is an interesting direction.
• Third, our representation matching is applied at the last layer only. Whether matching across all
  layers, or dynamically selecting which layers to match during training, offers additional benefit
  remains open, and we regard it as a promising direction for future exploration.

B     W HAT THE L EADING L OW-R ANK S UBSPACE C APTURES
B.1   I NTERPRETATION AND L IMITATIONS

The leading subspace of a pooled shift matrix captures the directions with the greatest observed
energy. This property alone does not identify which directions will improve downstream learning.
Answer-specific information could occupy a leading direction, while a useful but infrequent reason-
ing pattern could lie in the spectral tail. We therefore treat the leading subspace as an empirical proxy
for cross-problem structure, not as a decomposition of transferable and non-transferable knowledge.
Three design choices motivate its use in our setting:

• Source-side abstraction. We form the shifts from compact hints rather than full solutions. This
  removes much of the solution-specific detail before estimating the subspace, although it does not
  guarantee that the remaining shift contains only useful guidance.
• Cross-problem checks. We examine whether the selected directions recur among related prob-
  lems and how their geometry relates to downstream performance. The overlap measure in Sec-
  tion B.2 does not use singular values after the subspaces have been estimated. Recurrence provides
  an empirical check on the selected directions, but does not by itself establish their usefulness.
• Cluster-specific estimation. We estimate a separate subspace within each content cluster. A
  pattern that is weak in a global pool may become more prominent among related problems. This
  is a motivation for routing rather than a guarantee that every useful low-frequency pattern will be
  retained. Removing routing reduces average accuracy on both backbones (Table 2).

The following analyses test what the selected subspace captures and where this interpretation has
limits. In particular, concentrated and consistent shifts need not be useful when the privileged con-
text provides little actionable guidance (Appendix D.2).

B.4                  E FFECT OF THE S UBSPACE R ANK

The main-text sweep selects r within a narrow range. Here we compare r ∈ {128, 512, 1024} with
the full-shift variant (P = I) on Qwen3-1.7B. All variants retain the leading directions of the same
pooled shift, with only the number of retained directions changed. Figure 10 shows that r = 128
achieves the strongest and most stable performance in this comparison, while larger ranks and the
full-shift variant plateau or fluctuate more. As r increases, the target incorporates additional, lower-
energy directions. The fraction of observed shift energy retained by Top-r is determined by the
leading singular values, not by r/d. This experiment does not establish that the added directions are
answer-specific or intrinsically harmful. It shows that retaining more of the observed shift does not
improve learning in this setting. Because rank also changes the correction norm, this sweep does
not isolate dimensionality from correction magnitude.

B.5                  S UMMARY

The controlled hint comparison shows that correct hints induce leading subspaces with greater cross-
problem overlap and alignment, alongside more sustained downstream gains. Norm matching shows
that the advantage of Top-r cannot be explained solely by the size of the retained correction. The
rank sweep shows that retaining more directions is not necessarily better. Together, these obser-
vations support the leading subspace as an effective empirical choice for constructing privileged
corrections in our setting. They do not establish that SVD separates useful from useless knowledge,
or that low rank and recurrence alone guarantee downstream benefit.

D.2                    A BSTRACTION VERSUS I NFORMATION C ONTENT

The main text compares compact hints with full solutions, finding that solutions induce more dis-
persed shifts and less stable downstream performance. This raises the opposite question of whether
making privileged information even more abstract would help. We therefore introduce a concept
source that names the broad mathematical topic without providing a solving strategy or answer,
and compare all three sources on Qwen3-1.7B (Fig. 14). On AIME24, the concept source under-
performs hints throughout training and declines at later checkpoints. Yet its shift has low effective
rank and directional consistency comparable to the hint-induced shift (Fig. 14c). Concentration and
consistency alone therefore do not guarantee useful guidance. One possible explanation is that a
concept omits actionable strategy-level information despite inducing a coherent shift. Among these
three sources, hints offer the best observed balance between limiting solution-specific detail and
providing guidance the student can use.

D.5   C ASE S TUDY

To make the effect of PreSO concrete, we compare the base model with PreSO on two problems
aligned with the themes of clusters in Section D.4, with condensed excerpts in Figure 17.
The first case comes from cluster C0, which groups constrained optimization problems. The task
is to maximize the real part of an expression in z under |z| = 4. The base model makes a sign
error early and, without noticing, optimizes the wrong objective to a wrong value. PreSO keeps the
intended objective, parameterizes z on the circle |z| = 4, and reduces the problem to an amplitude


                                                                  26
Privileged Representation Isolation for On-Policy Self-Distillation




computation with the correct answer. The improvement here comes from carrying a coherent line of
reasoning all the way through, the solution structure that recurs across C0.
The second case comes from cluster C1, which groups validity and existence problems, and asks for
all real k under which a system of distance constraints has exactly one solution. The base model
places the real parameter on the wrong axis and carries this inconsistent geometry forward to a
wrong answer. PreSO starts similarly, but partway through detects that one distance is incompatible
with its current picture, rebuilds the geometry from the squared distances, and applies the tangency
condition to reach the correct answer. Beyond following the right structure, this case shows the
model checking its own intermediate reasoning and revising it when it does not hold together.
Together the two cases suggest that PreSO does not merely memorize answers but internalizes the
reasoning behavior of each cluster, both sustaining a correct line of reasoning and catching and cor-
recting its own missteps. We view these examples as illustrative rather than a systematic evaluation
of reasoning quality.
