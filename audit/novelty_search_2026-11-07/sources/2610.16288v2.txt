Abstract
Frontier LLM agents perform complex interactive tasks, but their inference costs motivate transferring their experience to smaller open-source agents. Distillation and latent communication typically require target-model training or access to source-model internals. We study how textual frontier-model trajectories can provide reusable local controls for a frozen agent. Inspired by protocol reverse engineering, we introduce Agentic Latents, comprising anchored states and dynamic profiles of feedback–response cycles elicited by replay in the target model. Our training-free framework, REVEAL (REVerse Engineering Agentic Latents), uses these profiles and trajectory outcomes to match and select local events from successful and failed rollouts of the same task and source model. Their response-transition contrasts yield a target-specific steering direction, applied before each response. Within-benchmark evaluations on Terminal-Bench 2.1 and Humanity’s Last Exam with tools report higher accuracy than unmodified agents across three targets. Representation analyses and behavioral case studies support reusing frontier-model experience as local activation control.
1 Introduction
Large language model (LLM) agents perform complex tasks through repeated interactions with tools and environments. Frontier models navigate these interactions effectively, but repeated calls over long trajectories can incur substantial inference costs. Smaller open-source models offer a path to lower-cost and more flexible deployment, yet often struggle with complex tool use and sustained task execution (Qin et al., 2024b; Merrill et al., 2026). Frontier-model trajectories provide a record of how stronger agents reason, act, and respond to feedback. Reusing this experience could help smaller agents complete demanding tasks with low additional adaptation cost.
Existing approaches transfer model capabilities through several forms of supervision. Teacher-generated task labels and explanations can supervise smaller models (Hsieh et al., 2023), while interaction trajectories provide demonstrations of tool use and sequential decision-making (Qin et al., 2024b). Access to internal states further enables representation-level transfer, including latent communication (Zou et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2026). These routes rely on target-model training or access to source-model internals. Figure 1 contrasts the corresponding training and information-access requirements. These requirements leave a practical gap: training adds optimization costs for each target, while text-only interfaces preclude internal-state transfer. This leads us to ask: Can long-horizon text trajectories from frontier models be reconstructed as reusable local control signals for a frozen open-source agent?
Inspired by protocol reverse engineering, which infers message structure through comparisons of observed network traces (Cui et al., 2007), we organize frontier-model experience into local interaction units for representation analysis and control. We introduce Agentic Latents: anchored states and dynamic profiles of feedback–response cycles, obtained by replaying interaction traces in a frozen open-source model. Representation engineering provides tools for analyzing these states and intervening on model behavior (Zou et al., 2023). A central challenge is that trajectory outcomes provide coarse supervision for local control: successful rollouts may contain ineffective retries, while failed ones may include useful corrections. We therefore use local representation dynamics to guide comparisons between successful and failed rollouts of the same task and frontier model.
Figure 1: Three routes for reusing frontier-model experience: (1) feature distillation trains on internal states; (2) black-box distillation trains on textual trajectories; (3) REVEAL characterizes Agentic Latents through frozen target-model replay of textual trajectories and derives steering directions.
We introduce REVEAL (REVerse Engineering Agentic Latents), a training-free framework that derives activation-steering signals from Agentic Latents (Figure 1, bottom). Anchored states are read at corresponding stages of feedback reception and response generation across trajectories, providing a common basis for local comparison. Their dynamic profiles characterize local interaction through update magnitude (UU), directional retention (CC), verbal accessibility (VV), and cross-cycle consistency (SS), guiding event matching and selection. Each target model aggregates response-transition contrasts from the selected events into a direction in its own representation space and applies it before generating each response.
We evaluate REVEAL on Terminal-Bench 2.1 (Merrill et al., 2026) and Humanity’s Last Exam with tools (Phan et al., 2025). On Terminal-Bench, REVEAL improves task completion across all three evaluated target models; for Qwen3.6-27B, it also outperforms trajectory SFT with fewer online tokens and interactions. Complementary analyses examine how response anchors summarize local transitions, how feedback-associated directions evolve during responses, and which trajectory patterns are shared across target models. Together with behavioral case analysis, these results support using Agentic Latents to characterize long-horizon experience and derive local controls for open-source agents.
Our contributions are threefold:
• 
Agentic Latents for characterizing long-horizon interaction. We introduce Agentic Latents, comprising anchored states and dynamic profiles, to characterize local internal representations in long-horizon interaction. The states mark corresponding interaction stages, while the profiles describe within- and cross-cycle dynamics for event comparison and selection.
• 
Training-free control through trajectory reverse engineering. We propose REVEAL, which selects matched local events from successful and failed frontier-model rollouts and constructs steering directions from their response-transition contrasts in each target model. The method uses textual trajectories and outcome labels without updating target parameters or accessing frontier-model logits or hidden states.
• 
Evidence for control utility and latent interaction structure. We evaluate REVEAL across three target models on terminal interaction and tool-assisted reasoning. Performance and online-cost comparisons assess the utility of the reconstructed controls, while complementary analyses examine response-transition approximation, representation dynamics, cross-model consistency, and subsequent agent behavior.
2 Method
2.1 Overview
Given textual frontier-model trajectories and task outcomes, REVEAL characterizes Agentic Latents and derives steering directions while keeping target parameters fixed. In Figure 2(a), frozen replay yields anchored states at corresponding interaction stages and dynamic profiles of within- and cross-cycle interaction. REVEAL uses these profiles to match and select local events, then aggregates their target-space response-transition contrasts into a steering direction (Figure 2(b)). During online interaction, the target applies this direction once before each response (Figure 2(c)). State extraction, profile computation, and direction construction are offline; each target serves as its own observer in our experiments.
2.2 Preliminaries
Agent–environment interaction.
Given a task qq and an initial observation o0o_{0}, the agent’s history at round tt is Ht=(q,o0,a0,o1,…,at−1,ot)H_{t}=(q,o_{0},a_{0},o_{1},\ldots,a_{t-1},o_{t}). We suppress fixed system instructions, tool specifications, and serialization in this notation. Here, ata_{t} is an assistant response, which may include tool calls, and oto_{t} is the feedback received before that response. For a nonterminal interaction,
at∼πθ(⋅∣Ht),ot+1∼ℰ(⋅∣Ht,at),a_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid H_{t}),\qquad o_{t+1}\sim\mathcal{E}(\cdot\mid H_{t},a_{t}),
(1)
where πθ\pi_{\theta} is the agent policy and ℰ\mathcal{E} is the environment. A rollout τ\tau ends with task completion or an interaction limit. An external evaluator assigns its outcome yτ∈{0,1}y_{\tau}\in\{0,1\}; this label is excluded from the replay input.
Activation steering.
Let hℓϕ​(x)∈ℝdϕh^{\phi}_{\ell}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\phi}} denote the residual-stream state at position xx and layer ℓ\ell in a frozen model with parameters ϕ\phi; the corresponding causal token prefix is implicit. We use ψ\psi for the observer and θ\theta for the target; the same model may serve both roles. Additive steering modifies the target state during a forward pass (Rimsky et al., 2024):
hℓθ​(x)←hℓθ​(x)+α​vℓθ,h^{\theta}_{\ell}(x)\leftarrow h^{\theta}_{\ell}(x)+\alpha v^{\theta}_{\ell},
(2)
where vℓθv^{\theta}_{\ell} is a steering direction and α\alpha its strength. The remaining computation uses the modified state without updating θ\theta.
2.3 Characterizing Agentic Latents
Agentic Latents comprise two components: anchored states and dynamic profiles. The states are extracted at corresponding positions in feedback–response cycles, while the profiles characterize state changes, response-content accessibility, and cross-cycle relationships for event comparison and selection. A frozen observer replays recorded trajectories under teacher forcing with causal attention. We suppress the rollout index when describing an individual cycle.
Anchored states.
For each cycle t≥1t\geq 1, we read internal states at anchors marking the preceding response endpoint, the input boundary, and the current response endpoints:
At−1end⟶Bt⟶Atstart⟶Atend.A_{t-1}^{\mathrm{end}}\longrightarrow B_{t}\longrightarrow A_{t}^{\mathrm{start}}\longrightarrow A_{t}^{\mathrm{end}}.
(3)
At−1endA_{t-1}^{\mathrm{end}} is the last non-whitespace content token of the preceding response. BtB_{t} is the final token of the input prefix, including environmental feedback and the generation header, immediately before the current response. AtstartA_{t}^{\mathrm{start}} and AtendA_{t}^{\mathrm{end}} are the first lexical and last non-whitespace content tokens of that response. Structural chat-template markers are excluded from response endpoints. Each model maps the corresponding text spans to its own token positions. Cycles with missing anchors are omitted; token-mapping conventions are given in Appendix B.

(b) Contrastive sample selection identifies matched local events; steering vector calculation combines their response-transition differences through hierarchical averaging. (c) The frozen target applies the resulting direction before each response.
Dynamic profiles.
We characterize the anchored states through four measurement families, UU, CC, VV, and SS, for event comparison and selection. For UU, CC, and SS, we independently RMS-normalize observer states as h¯ℓψ​(x)=hℓψ​(x)/‖hℓψ​(x)‖22/dψ+ϵ\bar{h}^{\psi}_{\ell}(x)=h^{\psi}_{\ell}(x)/\sqrt{\|h^{\psi}_{\ell}(x)\|_{2}^{2}/d_{\psi}+\epsilon}, where ϵ>0\epsilon>0 is a numerical stabilizer (Appendix B).
Update magnitude (UU).
The displacement across the feedback interval and its root-mean-square magnitude are
ut,ℓ=h¯ℓψ​(Bt)−h¯ℓψ​(At−1end),Ut,ℓ=‖ut,ℓ‖2dψ.u_{t,\ell}=\bar{h}^{\psi}_{\ell}(B_{t})-\bar{h}^{\psi}_{\ell}(A_{t-1}^{\mathrm{end}}),\qquad U_{t,\ell}=\frac{\|u_{t,\ell}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{d_{\psi}}}.
(4)
UU quantifies the representation change associated with the feedback interval.
Directional retention (CC).
We project response states onto the feedback-associated update, relative to the preceding response endpoint:
Ct,ℓ​(X)=⟨h¯ℓψ​(X)−h¯ℓψ​(At−1end),ut,ℓ⟩‖ut,ℓ‖22+ϵ,X∈{Atstart,Atend}.C_{t,\ell}(X)=\frac{\left\langle\bar{h}^{\psi}_{\ell}(X)-\bar{h}^{\psi}_{\ell}(A_{t-1}^{\mathrm{end}}),u_{t,\ell}\right\rangle}{\|u_{t,\ell}\|_{2}^{2}+\epsilon},\qquad X\in\{A_{t}^{\mathrm{start}},A_{t}^{\mathrm{end}}\}.
(5)
We denote the two coefficients by Ct,ℓstartC^{\mathrm{start}}_{t,\ell} and Ct,ℓendC^{\mathrm{end}}_{t,\ell} at the response start and end, respectively. A coefficient near one indicates a projected displacement of comparable magnitude to the original update. Values may exceed one or become negative, indicating a larger or oppositely signed component along the same direction.
Verbal accessibility (VV).
We measure the accessibility of recorded response tokens using a frozen Jacobian lens (J-lens) (Gurnee et al., 2026), with pre-fitted matrices from Neuronpedia. The boundary vocabulary readout is
pt,ℓJ,ψ=softmax⁡(WUψ​Normψ⁡(J^ℓψ​hℓψ​(Bt))),p^{J,\psi}_{t,\ell}=\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(W^{\psi}_{\mathrm{U}}\operatorname{Norm}_{\psi}\left(\widehat{J}^{\psi}_{\ell}h^{\psi}_{\ell}(B_{t})\right)\right),
(6)
where J^ℓψ\widehat{J}^{\psi}_{\ell} is a fixed residual-transport matrix for the observer, and Normψ\operatorname{Norm}_{\psi} and WUψW^{\psi}_{\mathrm{U}} are the observer’s final normalization and unembedding. Its theoretical definition is given in Appendix A. Let YtY_{t} contain the unique non-structural content token IDs in the recorded response, tokenized by the observer, and let 𝒱ψ\mathcal{V}_{\psi} be its vocabulary. We measure their mean normalized rank:
Vt,ℓ=1|Yt|​∑y∈Yt[1−rankpt,ℓJ,ψ⁡(y)−1|𝒱ψ|−1].V_{t,\ell}=\frac{1}{|Y_{t}|}\sum_{y\in Y_{t}}\left[1-\frac{\operatorname{rank}_{p^{J,\psi}_{t,\ell}}(y)-1}{|\mathcal{V}_{\psi}|-1}\right].
(7)
Ranks are descending and one-based, and VV is defined for nonempty YtY_{t}. Higher values place the recorded response tokens nearer the top of the boundary ranking. These tokens supply offline readout targets; causal attention prevents them from affecting the boundary state.
Cross-cycle consistency (SS).
We measure the directional agreement between consecutive updates:
St,ℓ=⟨ut,ℓ,ut+1,ℓ⟩‖ut,ℓ‖2​‖ut+1,ℓ‖2.S_{t,\ell}=\frac{\langle u_{t,\ell},u_{t+1,\ell}\rangle}{\|u_{t,\ell}\|_{2}\|u_{t+1,\ell}\|_{2}}.
(8)
This cosine is defined for nonzero updates in immediately adjacent cycles. Positive values indicate directional agreement, while negative values indicate disagreement. Events without a valid successor are excluded from selection.
2.4 Trajectory Reverse Engineering and Steering
REVEAL selects contrastive event pairs using the dynamic profiles of Agentic Latents, then calculates steering directions from their anchored target states (Figure 2(b)).
Contrastive sample selection.
For each task, we form pairs p=(τ+,τ−)p=(\tau^{+},\tau^{-}) of successful and failed completed rollouts from the same frontier model, excluding infrastructure failures. Candidate samples are local feedback–response events i=(τ,t)i=(\tau,t) with complete dynamic profiles, including a valid successor for SS. In a matched pair (i,j)(i,j), ii is drawn from τ+\tau^{+} and jj from τ−\tau^{-}.
We summarize each measurement using fixed observer-layer sets and aggregation rules, restricting VV to lens-supported layers. For event ii, a summary mim_{i} is standardized as zm​(i)=(mi−μm)/(σm+ϵ)z_{m}(i)=(m_{i}-\mu_{m})/(\sigma_{m}+\epsilon) using construction-data statistics. Within each rollout pair, we match events one-to-one without replacement by their proximity in (zU,zV)(z_{U},z_{V}), prioritizing the same tool category and similar prefix lengths. We then rank matched pairs by their differences in directional retention and cross-cycle consistency. Writing Δ​zm​(i,j)=zm​(i)−zm​(j)\Delta z_{m}(i,j)=z_{m}(i)-z_{m}(j), the selection score is
Gi​j=12​(|Δ​zCstart​(i,j)|+|Δ​zCend​(i,j)|)+|Δ​zS​(i,j)|.G_{ij}=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(|\Delta z_{C^{\mathrm{start}}}(i,j)|+|\Delta z_{C^{\mathrm{end}}}(i,j)|\right)+|\Delta z_{S}(i,j)|.
(9)
The top half of matched pairs within each rollout pair form 𝒦p\mathcal{K}_{p}. Thus, UU and VV guide comparability, while CC and SS identify contrasting dynamics. Rollout outcomes determine the contrast’s orientation; larger CC or SS values are not assumed to be better.

Steering vector calculation.
Frozen target replay provides the raw anchor states for each selected event and its recorded prefix. For i=(τ,t)i=(\tau,t), we denote its anchors by BiB_{i}, AistartA_{i}^{\mathrm{start}}, and AiendA_{i}^{\mathrm{end}}, and compute the response-transition summary
rℓ,iθ=12​[hℓθ​(Aistart)+hℓθ​(Aiend)]−hℓθ​(Bi).r^{\theta}_{\ell,i}=\tfrac{1}{2}\left[h^{\theta}_{\ell}(A_{i}^{\mathrm{start}})+h^{\theta}_{\ell}(A_{i}^{\mathrm{end}})\right]-h^{\theta}_{\ell}(B_{i}).

This summary averages the response-start and response-end displacements from the input boundary. Each selected pair (i,j)∈𝒦p(i,j)\in\mathcal{K}_{p} contributes rℓ,iθ−rℓ,jθr^{\theta}_{\ell,i}-r^{\theta}_{\ell,j}, contrasting boundary-relative response displacements from successful and failed source rollouts in the same target layer. Hierarchical averaging averages these contrasts within each rollout pair, then averages pair means equally within each task and task means equally across tasks (Appendix B.5). We normalize the resulting nonzero aggregate as vℓθ=v~ℓθ/‖v~ℓθ‖2v^{\theta}_{\ell}=\widetilde{v}^{\theta}_{\ell}/\|\widetilde{v}^{\theta}_{\ell}\|_{2} and use it as a candidate direction for perturbing the target’s pre-response state. The recorded response endpoints are required only during offline construction.
Online steering.
For each target and benchmark, we use a fixed intervention layer ℓs\ell_{s} and strength α\alpha, chosen empirically. Before each response, we apply
hℓsθ​(Bt)←hℓsθ​(Bt)+α​vℓsθ.h^{\theta}_{\ell_{s}}(B_{t})\leftarrow h^{\theta}_{\ell_{s}}(B_{t})+\alpha v^{\theta}_{\ell_{s}}.
(11)
The addition occurs once during prefill at the final prefix token; B0B_{0} denotes this position for the initial response. Decoding then proceeds normally. The direction and strength remain fixed, and online execution requires no additional observer pass or frontier-model query.
3 Experiments
3.1 Tasks and Datasets
We evaluate REVEAL on terminal interaction and tool-assisted reasoning. Terminal-Bench 2.1 (Merrill et al., 2026) comprises 89 tasks that require agents to operate in containerized command-line environments. We use the complete task set and evaluate task completion with the benchmark’s task-specific verifiers through Harbor.11
 1
 https://github.com/harbor-framework/terminal-bench-2-1 Results are reported overall and by the official difficulty categories: 4 easy, 55 medium, and 30 hard tasks. Humanity’s Last Exam (HLE) (Phan et al., 2025) evaluates expert-level reasoning across academic disciplines. We use the fixed 500-question subset released by WebThinker (Li et al., 2025), which was randomly sampled from the text-only questions, and evaluate it in a tool-assisted setting. Final answers are graded by GPT-5.6-luna; we report accuracy overall and across eight subject categories.
3.2 Baselines
We compare REVEAL with three baselines. Vanilla uses the underlying agent without additional feedback-regulation instructions or activation steering. Prompt-Reg adds a fixed natural-language policy that instructs the agent to revise assumptions using feedback, carry updates into subsequent actions, avoid ineffective repetition, and preserve valid constraints. The full policy appears in Appendix C. REVEAL-Base constructs a steering direction from target-model activations elicited by successful and failed frontier-model trajectories, adapting contrastive activation addition (CAA) (Rimsky et al., 2024). It uses trajectory outcomes without REVEAL’s profile-guided selection of local feedback–response events. For the efficiency comparison, we also fine-tune Qwen3.6-27B by next-token prediction on the same 124 frontier-model trajectories used for direction construction.

3.3 Implementation Details
We use Qwen3.6-27B (Qwen Team, 2026), Gemma-4-31B-IT (Gemma Team, 2026), and GPT-OSS-20B (OpenAI, 2025) as target models, each serving as its own frozen observer. GPT-5.6-sol generates four rollouts per task. Tasks with both successful and failed rollouts contribute selected contrasts to one steering direction per target and benchmark. We evaluate within-benchmark adaptation using outcome-labeled source trajectories from the evaluation task collection, without a held-out task split. Target parameters remain frozen.
We choose the intervention layer and strength empirically and keep them fixed during evaluation. On Terminal-Bench, agents use Terminus-2 through Harbor, with vLLM serving at temperature 1.0. REVEAL intervenes at layers 40, 30, and 15 for Qwen3.6-27B, Gemma-4-31B-IT, and GPT-OSS-20B, respectively, with α=0.5\alpha=0.5, at the final input-prefix position before each response. Per-request generation caps are 81,920, 8,192, and 32,768 tokens, respectively. We set these caps separately based on the models’ observed response-generation behavior during agent interactions, and share each cap across methods for the same target. They bound individual responses; actual task-level consumption is measured separately in the efficiency analysis.

3.4 Main Results
Finding 1: REVEAL achieves higher reported performance across both benchmarks and all three target models. On Terminal-Bench, completion improves by 5.62–6.74 percentage points over Vanilla and by 1.12–3.37 points over REVEAL-Base (Table 1). On HLE, REVEAL likewise achieves the highest reported overall accuracy among the compared methods for each target model.
Finding 2: REVEAL reduces online interaction costs relative to trajectory SFT. Using the same 124 trajectories from 33 tasks within the evaluation collection, REVEAL solves 35/89 Terminal-Bench tasks versus 32/89 for trajectory SFT (Table 2). Relative to SFT, it uses 35.60% fewer input tokens and 50.43% fewer output tokens, with fewer rounds and tool calls. These measurements cover execution after adaptation; source collection, offline replay, profile computation, and SFT training are excluded.
3.5 Empirical Analysis
Finding 3: Response anchors provide a compact summary of local representation transitions. At the measured layer of each model and on both benchmarks, averaging the response-start and response-end states better approximates the boundary-relative mean response displacement than either endpoint alone (Figure 3). Mean cosine similarity reaches 0.750.75–0.920.92, while relative error decreases by 14.514.5–30.9%30.9\% against the better single endpoint. These results support the response-endpoint summary used to construct steering contrasts from Agentic Latents.

Finding 4: Update magnitude and directional retention reveal complementary interaction dynamics. Response-end projections are lower than response-start projections in 147/148 evaluated layers on Terminal-Bench and 144/148 on HLE. Figure 4 places this shared pattern alongside the distinct depth profiles of update magnitude, verbal accessibility, and cross-cycle consistency. Full HLE profiles and within-response curves extend this analysis in Appendices D.2–D.3.
Finding 5: The same trajectories induce correlated cross-cycle dynamics across models. For identical replayed trajectories, task-centered SS profiles retain cross-model Spearman correlations of 0.680.68–0.950.95 on Terminal-Bench and 0.710.71–0.840.84 on HLE (Figure 5). Agreement in UU is substantially weaker: shared temporal structure coexists with model-dependent update magnitudes. Outcome-conditioned contrasts retain this agreement (Appendix D.5). These correlations characterize scalar trajectory descriptors; steering directions are reconstructed separately in each target’s latent space.
Finding 6: In a counting task, the steered agent adopts the frontier reference’s verification pattern. In count-dataset-tokens, Vanilla recognizes a possible separator effect but finalizes the direct-concatenation total of 79,566 without testing alternatives. The frontier reference counts fields independently and checks separator variants. REVEAL uses a different counting script, compares the alternatives, and revises its initial preference for concatenation before selecting 79,586 (Figure 6). This case illustrates a verification pattern shared with the frontier reference, expressed through the target’s own computation and decision sequence.
4 Related Work
4.1 Representation Engineering and Activation Steering
Representation engineering examines how high-level concepts are encoded in LLM representations and how interventions on these representations alter model behavior (Bartoszcze et al., 2025; Wehner et al., 2025). The Representation Engineering framework (Zou et al., 2023) systematizes this perspective through two complementary operations: Representation Reading and Representation Control. Representative studies probe truth-related representations through Inference-Time Intervention (Li et al., 2023) and The Geometry of Truth (Marks and Tegmark, 2024), while Activation Addition (Turner et al., 2023) and Contrastive Activation Addition (Rimsky et al., 2024) construct steering directions from prompt-pair or contrastive activation differences. Related vector-based studies examine refusal (Arditi et al., 2024), in-context functions (Todd et al., 2024), and task representations (Hendel et al., 2023), while evaluation work shows that measured steering effects depend on protocol design (Pres et al., 2024). REVEAL compares feedback–response dynamics across trajectories through the anchored states and dynamic profiles of Agentic Latents.
4.2 Tool Use and Long-Horizon Agents
Research on tool-using LLM agents increasingly examines multi-step interactions shaped by observations, tool outputs, and environmental feedback (Qin et al., 2024a; Wang et al., 2024). Representative systems include WebGPT (Nakano et al., 2022) for browsing-grounded question answering, ReAct (Yao et al., 2023) for reasoning–action interleaving, Toolformer (Schick et al., 2023) and ToolLLM (Qin et al., 2024b) for API use, and Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023) for feedback-based retry. Recent work reads tool necessity from pre-generation states and controls it through textual prefill, diagnoses capability-relative knowing–doing gaps, and extracts steerable tool-identity directions (Sun et al., 2026; Cheng et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026). Trajectory-level methods further decode tool dependencies and predict episode failure from agent states, or audit safety risks through observer representations (Sun and Kazakov, 2026; Ruan et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026). Closer to long-horizon control, From Actions to Understanding (Padhi et al., 2026) derives success/failure directions over agent trajectories, whereas TACT (Sui et al., 2026) steers behavioral drift axes at reasoning–action boundaries. REVEAL uses trajectory outcomes and local representation profiles to select interaction events, then derives steering directions from their target-model response transitions.
5 Conclusion
Long-horizon experience can be reused as local control within an agent’s existing representation space. REVEAL represents this experience as Agentic Latents, comprising anchored states and dynamic profiles, and uses the profiles to select response-transition contrasts for steering. Our results connect these controls to improved task execution and, on Terminal-Bench, more economical online interaction. This reuses frontier-model experience through the executing model’s own representations, without accessing frontier-model internals or updating target parameters.
Limitations
Our evaluations measure within-benchmark adaptation using outcome-labeled trajectories from the evaluation task collection; transfer to held-out tasks and new environments remains untested. Future work could evaluate explicit construction/evaluation splits and transfer across frontier sources. Event selection also depends on compatible pre-fitted Jacobian lenses and valid feedback–response anchors, excluding events with missing measurements. Alternative readouts and selection rules for incomplete interaction records could extend the settings in which Agentic Latents can be studied, while testing whether their control utility persists.

A.3 Why This Readout Fits REVEAL
REVEAL needs a descriptor of response-content accessibility before a response begins. A logit lens reads intermediate states with the output head directly, equivalent to the identity transport in Equation (17). This omits the transformation between the source layer and the output representation. A tuned lens learns affine maps to approximate the model’s final prediction distribution (Belrose et al., 2023). That objective addresses next-token prediction fidelity, rather than the accessibility of content distributed throughout a response. Here, YtY_{t} includes content throughout a recorded response, and VV asks how highly that content ranks at the boundary. The boundary state uses its causal prefix; YtY_{t} is obtained retrospectively solely for offline scoring. We therefore use the averaged-transport readout to characterize this broader content set.
The lens operates only during offline profiling. Its VV score is paired with UU to match events with similar update size and content accessibility; CC and SS then identify contrasting dynamics. The resulting event indices determine which raw response transitions contribute to steering. Thus the lens affects the choice of evidence used to construct a direction, without defining that direction’s coordinates or adding a lens computation to online execution.
A.4 Interpreting the Empirical Readout
Our readout diagnostics distinguish relative rank from top-token recovery. Alongside VV, we compute
Recall​@​50t,ℓ=1|Yt|∑y∈Yt𝟏{rankpt,ℓJ,ψ(y)≤50}.\operatorname{Recall@50}_{t,\ell}=\frac{1}{|Y_{t}|}\sum_{y\in Y_{t}}\mathbf{1}\{\operatorname{rank}_{p^{J,\psi}_{t,\ell}}(y)\leq 50\}.
(20)
Both statistics weight unique content token IDs equally. In task-balanced layer-band summaries of our HLE source-trajectory cohort (1,303 tasks), Qwen has late-layer V=0.635V=0.635 with Recall@50 of 1.4089%1.4089\%, while GPT-OSS has early-layer V=0.710V=0.710 with Recall@50 of 0.0245%0.0245\%. The respective supported bands are zero-based layers 43–62 and 0–7. This replay cohort is separate from the 500-question online evaluation. Above-chance average ranks can therefore coexist with low top-50 coverage. This supports interpreting VV as relative accessibility of recorded content, rather than recovery of the complete response. It also motivates using VV within an observer and layer convention instead of treating its absolute value as a model-independent quality score. These diagnostics characterize the chosen readout; they do not establish comparative superiority over alternative lenses.
Appendix B Agentic Latents: Anchors and Dynamic Profiles
Agentic Latents combine states at corresponding stages of interaction with profiles of their local dynamics. The anchors define which events can be compared; the profiles specify what is compared. We give the extraction conventions and derive two properties that explain the information retained by these measurements.
B.1 Anchored States and Causal Prefixes
For each model, anchors are mapped from response-content and generation-header spans in its chat serialization. At−1endA_{t-1}^{\mathrm{end}} is the last non-whitespace content token of the preceding response; BtB_{t} is the final generation-header token after feedback; AtstartA_{t}^{\mathrm{start}} and AtendA_{t}^{\mathrm{end}} are the first lexical and last non-whitespace content tokens of the current response. Structural chat markers are excluded from response endpoints. Correspondence refers to interaction roles, not equal token indices or shared hidden-state coordinates across models.
Each anchor is evaluated with its recorded causal prefix. In particular, hℓψ​(Bt)h^{\psi}_{\ell}(B_{t}) includes feedback and the generation header but no token of the current response. The state at AtstartA_{t}^{\mathrm{start}} already includes the first lexical response token. Teacher forcing makes the recorded response available for extracting its later states and content set YtY_{t}, while causal attention prevents those later tokens from changing the boundary state. A one-token response may have coincident start and end anchors.
The interval from At−1endA_{t-1}^{\mathrm{end}} to BtB_{t} includes new feedback, serialization, and a change of token position. Its displacement is therefore feedback-associated, not an isolated causal effect of the feedback text. The response anchors delimit the subsequent state change using the same reference point. Their endpoint average is a compact response summary whose fidelity is evaluated in Figure 3.

The response supplies a set YtY_{t} of unique non-structural content token IDs. Using a set avoids weighting a token repeatedly because it occurs many times. B.4 From Profiles to Event Comparisons
The profiles serve different roles in REVEAL. Matching on standardized UU and VV seeks events with comparable update size and boundary accessibility of response content. Selection on absolute differences in CC and SS then identifies contrasting within-response and cross-cycle dynamics. The final rollout outcomes orient each contrast; large CC or SS is not intrinsically rewarded. A negative SS, for example, can accompany a correction that reverses an earlier update.
The two propositions justify interpreting these quantities separately and establish what their numerical values measure. They do not identify the correctness of an intermediate action or remove every contextual difference between matched events. The matched raw target-state transitions supply the steering direction, whose behavioral utility is evaluated by online intervention. This keeps the descriptive role of Agentic Latents distinct from the experimental test of their use for control.
B.5 Hierarchical Averaging of Response-Transition Contrasts
For each task qq, let 𝒫q\mathcal{P}_{q} denote the retained successful–failed rollout pairs with nonempty selected event sets 𝒦p\mathcal{K}_{p}, and let 𝒬={q:𝒫q≠∅}\mathcal{Q}=\{q:\mathcal{P}_{q}\neq\varnothing\} denote the eligible tasks. For each (i,j)∈𝒦p(i,j)\in\mathcal{K}_{p}, event ii comes from the successful rollout and event jj from the failed rollout. Their response-transition summaries are computed from raw target-model states using Equation (10).
For nonempty 𝒬\mathcal{Q}, hierarchical averaging gives
v~ℓθ=1|𝒬|​∑q∈𝒬1|𝒫q|​∑p∈𝒫q1|𝒦p|​∑(i,j)∈𝒦p(rℓ,iθ−rℓ,jθ).\widetilde{v}^{\theta}_{\ell}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}|}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{q}|}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{q}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{K}_{p}|}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{K}_{p}}\left(r^{\theta}_{\ell,i}-r^{\theta}_{\ell,j}\right).
(29)
Thus, selected event pairs receive equal weight within each rollout pair, rollout-pair means receive equal weight within each task, and task means receive equal weight across tasks. This prevents tasks with more eligible rollout pairs, or rollout pairs with more selected events, from receiving greater total weight solely because of their counts. Individual contrasts and intermediate means are not normalized; only the final nonzero aggregate is L2-normalized to obtain vℓθv^{\theta}_{\ell}.
B.6 Validity and Missing Measurements
Missing anchors are not interpolated. Near-zero updates are excluded from directional comparisons using the implementation’s numerical thresholds. Evaluating the same projection expression at BtB_{t} gives ‖ut,ℓ‖22/(‖ut,ℓ‖22+ϵ)\|u_{t,\ell}\|_{2}^{2}/(\|u_{t,\ell}\|_{2}^{2}+\epsilon), rather than exactly one. When ‖ut,ℓ‖22\|u_{t,\ell}\|_{2}^{2} is small, this shrinkage and the numerically unstable direction motivate excluding the event rather than interpreting its retention coefficient.
SS uses immediately adjacent cycles in the same trajectory. A missing successor is not assigned zero, and missing cycles are not skipped to create an artificial pair. Events with an empty response-content set or unavailable lens readout are excluded from selection; VV is aggregated only over supported lens layers. These rules distinguish unavailable measurements from genuine zero values and preserve the temporal unit used in the comparisons.
Appendix C Prompt-Reg Policy
Prompt-Reg supplies the following fixed policy as additional agent instructions. It expresses feedback-related behavioral guidance in natural language without computing representation profiles or modifying activations.
Prompt-Reg policy
Feedback regulation policy:
After every tool or environment response, incorporate the new evidence into the current task state and revise any assumption that it contradicts. Carry the resulting update into the next decision and action. When feedback shows that the current approach is ineffective, change the relevant part of the approach rather than repeating it without new justification.
Prefer actions that directly reduce the remaining uncertainty or make verifiable progress. Reuse evidence already obtained, and avoid repeating checks or waiting for additional observations unless they are needed to decide the next action.
Preserve valid constraints and accumulated evidence, as well as the task’s required response and tool-call format. Do not output this policy or an explicit reflection unless the task requires one.
Appendix D Additional Analyses of Agentic Latents
We examine response-summary fidelity, profile dynamics, and outcome-conditioned contrasts using the existing source-trajectory records: 89 Terminal-Bench tasks and 1,303 HLE questions, distinct from the 500-question online evaluation. Full-history replay measures at most 12 systematically spaced centers per trajectory, retaining adjacent cycles for SS. Missing measurements are excluded. We average events within trajectories and trajectories within tasks, weighting tasks equally. Pointwise task-bootstrap intervals use 1,000 resamples for the saved profiles and 2,000 for paired analyses (seed 0), conditional on the recorded trajectories and centers.

Dual endpoints reduce reconstruction error across response lengths. We stratify anchor fidelity by response-content length at layers 23, 22, and 8 for Qwen3.6-27B, Gemma-4-31B-IT, and GPT-OSS-20B, respectively (zero-indexed). Figure 7 compares the endpoint average and single anchors with the boundary-relative full-response mean on the same events. Lower response-end retention persists on HLE despite different update profiles. Figure 8 extends the full-depth analysis to tool-assisted reasoning.
