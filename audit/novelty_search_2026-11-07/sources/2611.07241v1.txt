Abstract
On-policy distillation (OPD) trains a student model to match a teacher’s next-token distributions on student-generated prefixes.
Extrapolative OPD further extends the teacher’s log-probability shift relative to a reference policy to construct stronger learning targets. However, existing methods couple extrapolation direction and distance, allowing the magnitude of the teacher’s shift to dictate how far the target moves.
We propose D2-OPD, decoupling these two factors: a per-prefix hard KL constraint bounds the target’s distance from the teacher, while an adaptive combination of the teacher’s post-training shift and the initial student–teacher gap determines the extrapolation direction.
We optimize the student toward this target using forward KL, providing learning signals even for low-probability tokens.
We evaluate three settings: where standard OPD performs well, where it struggles with base-model initialization, and where no teacher reference is available.
On mathematical reasoning benchmarks, D2-OPD attains the highest score in all three settings, outperforming the strongest baseline by up to 9.45 points.
Ablations validate the contributions of each component.

1 Introduction
On-policy distillation (OPD) trains a student language model on its own responses: the student generates a response and, at each prefix it visits, learns from the teacher’s next-token distribution (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024; Lu and Thinking Machines Lab, 2025).
Training on student-generated prefixes reduces the mismatch between the responses seen in training and those the student generates at inference (Ross et al., 2011; Lin et al., 2020).
Prior work has studied several aspects of on-policy distillation, including the efficient use of student-generated responses (Ko et al., 2024; Ko et al., 2025),
teacher entropy (Jin et al., 2026), teacher–student disagreement (Xing et al., 2026),
and training stability (Xie et al., 2026). These studies show that the way teacher supervision is constructed and
transferred matters beyond the choice of teacher alone.
However, the teacher’s distribution may not be the best target: prior work shows that a more capable teacher does not necessarily yield a better student (Mirzadeh et al., 2020; Li et al., 2026).
Extrapolative OPD aims to construct a better target beyond the teacher by amplifying the teacher’s log-probability shift from a reference policy.
If the teacher was obtained from the reference by KL-regularized post-training, this shift equals its regularized QQ-function up to scale and a prefix-dependent constant (Geist et al., 2019; Rafailov et al., 2024), so amplifying it moves probability toward tokens with higher teacher QQ-values.
Constructing an extrapolative target involves two decisions: the direction in which the target moves away from the teacher, and the distance it moves.
In existing methods, the distance is tied to the direction.
For example, ExOPD (Yang et al., 2026) fixes the direction through its reference, but under a fixed coefficient its distance varies with the scale of the teacher–reference gap across prefixes; in Direct-OPD (Feng et al., 2026), changing the batch-shared adaptive coefficient generally changes both the direction and the distance.
In both cases, the distance follows from the coefficient and the log-ratios at each prefix, rather than from how far the target can be trusted beyond the teacher.
We show that, under fixed-coefficient extrapolation, the target can place substantial probability on tokens the teacher rarely predicts, even when the error in the extrapolation signal is small on average under the teacher.
To address this coupling, we propose D2-OPD, which decouples distance from direction.
First, a per-prefix hard KL constraint bounds the target’s distance from the teacher, independently of the scale of the extrapolation signal; this also bounds the probability the target can assign to tokens the teacher rarely predicts.
Second, we construct the direction by combining the teacher’s post-training shift and the initial student–teacher gap.
The weights adapt at each prefix, giving more weight to the nearer signal.
Finally, forward KL transfers the target while maintaining non-vanishing gradients for tokens rarely generated by the student.
We evaluate D2-OPD on three Qwen3 teacher–student pairs covering a setting where standard OPD
performs well, a setting for which prior work recommends a supervised cold start (Li et al., 2026), and a setting
without a separate teacher reference. D2-OPD achieves the highest average score across AIME24, AIME25, and AMC23 in all three settings. In Qwen3-8B →\rightarrow
Qwen3-4B-Base distillation, it outperforms OPD by 19.62 points and the strongest baseline by 9.45
points. In the cold-start setting, it exceeds the released SFT + OPD recipe without supervised fine-tuning.
Ablations confirm the contributions of the distance constraint, the combined direction, and forward-KL distillation.
Response-length and entropy analyses indicate that the gains are not
merely due to longer answers or more concentrated student policies.
2 Related work
Distillation objectives.
Knowledge distillation trains students using teacher predictions, with applications to policy learning
and reasoning
(Hinton et al., 2015; Kim and Rush, 2016; Rusu et al., 2016; Teh et al., 2017; Schmitt et al., 2018; Hsieh et al., 2023; Shridhar et al., 2023).
On-policy imitation and distillation reduce the mismatch between training and inference by training
on the learner’s own trajectories (Ross et al., 2011; Lin et al., 2020; Agarwal et al., 2024).
Related work studies divergence choices and response reuse for transferring teacher supervision
(Wen et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2024; Ko et al., 2024; Ko et al., 2025).
Teacher–student gaps.
A larger or more capable teacher does not necessarily produce a better student
(Mirzadeh et al., 2020; Zhong et al., 2024; Shing et al., 2025; Furlanello et al., 2018; Burns et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025).
EOPD (Jin et al., 2026) adds forward KL when teacher entropy is high, while TrOPD (Xing et al., 2026) applies
forward KL where teacher–student disagreement is large.
Li et al. (2026) link training failure to low initial overlap between
the teacher’s and student’s high-probability token sets.
Extrapolation and distance constraints.
Under KL-regularized optimization, the log-ratio between the optimized policy and its reference encodes the regularized QQ-function (Ouyang et al., 2022; Geist et al., 2019; Rafailov et al., 2023; Rafailov et al., 2024).
Extrapolative methods use this relation to construct new distillation targets.
ExOPD uses a fixed coefficient to scale a direction set by its reference (Yang et al., 2026).
Direct-OPD transfers the teacher’s post-training shift through an objective anchored at the initial student, with an adaptive regularization coefficient applied uniformly across the batch (Feng et al., 2026).
Changing this coefficient generally changes both the target’s direction and its distance relative to the teacher.
TOP-D constructs a proximal teacher and uses trust-region updates (Xie et al., 2026); ERPD constrains
distillation relative to the rollout policy (Chen et al., 2026).

4.1 Limiting the extrapolation distance
As discussed in Section 3.2, existing extrapolation coefficients control the distance from q+(⋅∣h)q^{+}(\cdot\mid h) only indirectly: a coefficient multiplies a log-ratio whose scale varies across prefixes, so one value can hold the target close to q+(⋅∣h)q^{+}(\cdot\mid h) at one prefix and carry it much farther at another.
The distance is worth controlling on its own, because the teacher is a policy we can observe and evaluate, whereas a target farther from q+(⋅∣h)q^{+}(\cdot\mid h) rests more heavily on extrapolation.
For a candidate target distribution q(⋅∣h)q(\cdot\mid h) supported on S⁡(h)S(h), let 𝒦(q;h)=DKL(q(⋅∣h)∥qS+(⋅∣h))\mathcal{K}(q;h)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(\cdot\mid h)\|q_{S}^{+}(\cdot\mid h)). We impose 𝒦⁡(q,h)≤ϵ\mathcal{K}(q;h)\leq\epsilon at every prefix, which bounds how far the target may move.

4.2 Determining the extrapolation direction

tη​(a∣h)∝qS+​(a∣h)​exp⁡[−dη​(h,a)].t_{\eta}(a\mid h)\propto q_{S}^{+}(a\mid h)\exp[-d_{\eta}(h,a)].
(12)
After normalization over S⁡(h)S(h), log⁡(qS+​(a∣h)/tη​(a∣h))\log(q_{S}^{+}(a\mid h)/t_{\eta}(a\mid h)) equals dη​(h,a)d_{\eta}(h,a) up to a token-independent constant.
This constructed reference requires no additional model.
4.3 Transferring the target to the student
The constructed target can place probability to tokens that are rare under the student. We use forward
KL because its gradient remains non-vanishing in this case. The forward-KL gradient with respect to the student logit is πθ​(a∣h)−q~​(a∣h)\pi_{\theta}(a\mid h)-\tilde{q}(a\mid h).
For tokens with positive target probability, it approaches −q~​(a∣h)-\tilde{q}(a\mid h) as πθ​(a∣h)→0\pi_{\theta}(a\mid h)\to 0, whereas the reverse-KL gradient vanishes.
In practice, S⁡(h)S(h) contains the top-KK tokens under the teacher distribution q+(⋅∣h)q^{+}(\cdot\mid h). We construct and renormalize q~(⋅∣h)\tilde{q}(\cdot\mid h) on S⁡(h)S(h), but keep the student distribution over the full vocabulary.
Estimating forward KL from student-sampled tokens can have high variance.
The importance weights q~​(a∣h)/πθ​(a∣h)\tilde{q}(a\mid h)/\pi_{\theta}(a\mid h) can be large when the target assigns substantial probability to tokens the student rarely samples.
We therefore compute the exact sum over all tokens in S⁡(h)S(h) at each visited prefix.
With support mass πθ​(S​(h)∣h)\pi_{\theta}(S(h)\mid h) and renormalized student πθ,S(⋅∣h)\pi_{\theta,S}(\cdot\mid h) defined in Section 3, we have
DKL(q~(⋅∣h)∥πθ(⋅∣h))=−logπθ(S(h)∣h)+DKL(q~(⋅∣h)∥πθ,S(⋅∣h)).\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\tilde{q}(\cdot\mid h)\|\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h))=\\
&-\log\pi_{\theta}(S(h)\mid h)+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\tilde{q}(\cdot\mid h)\|\pi_{\theta,S}(\cdot\mid h)).\end{aligned}
(13)
The first term shifts student probability mass onto S⁡(h)S(h), while the second matches its relative
distribution within it. Renormalizing the student removes the first term and its mass-transfer effect.
Unlike Eq. (13), reverse KL has no such mass-transfer term and only adjusts relative probabilities within S⁡(h)S(h). We examine the importance of this forward-KL choice in the ablation experiments in Section 5.

Hyperparameters.

Appendix A provides training and evaluation details.

5.3 Analysis
Figure 4: The gains are not due to lower entropy. We measure full-vocabulary student entropy on each method’s on-policy prefixes.
Faint lines show per-step values; solid lines show a 9-step rolling mean.
How restrictive is ϵ\epsilon?
To start with, we give a simple interpretation of the scale of ϵ\epsilon. For a one-hot target δa(⋅∣h)\delta_{a}(\cdot\mid h) that assigns all probability to a token a∈S⁡(h)a\in S(h), DKL(δa(⋅∣h)∥qS+(⋅∣h))=−logqS+(a∣h)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\delta_{a}(\cdot\mid h)\|q_{S}^{+}(\cdot\mid h))=-\log q_{S}^{+}(a\mid h), which means that ϵ=1\epsilon=1 permits the target to concentrate entirely on a token with qS+​(a∣h)≥e−1≈0.37q_{S}^{+}(a\mid h)\geq e^{-1}\approx 0.37. Although this makes ϵ=1\epsilon=1 fairly permissive, Figure 5 shows that it still binds a meaningful fraction of positions in practice. The constraint binds on more than 60% of positions early in training and has a median active fraction of 34% over the second half of training.
As expected, increasing κ\kappa makes the constraint less active, since the soft objective itself proposes less aggressive extrapolation. Even at κ=0.3\kappa=0.3, the hard constraint still binds on about 11% of positions.

Are the gains explained by longer responses?
ExOPD observes that extrapolation can increase response length (Yang et al., 2026). We observe the same behavior for ExOPD, but not for extrapolative methods in general. In Figure 3, D2-OPD and Direct-OPD
remain among the shorter methods across all three settings. TOP-D has a similar length and also remains well below the generation limit. In contrast, ExOPD and EOPD generally produce
much longer responses. It is the most obvious in Settings B and C, where several methods nearly exhaust the 31,744-token budget while D2-OPD does not.22
 2
 Our evaluation length limit matches the limit used by Li et al. (2026); Feng et al. (2026) and is larger than those used by Jin et al. (2026); Yang et al. (2026); Xie et al. (2026).
D2-OPD achieves the highest average score in all three settings while generating shorter responses than several baselines.
Are the gains explained by lower entropy?
A common intuition is that a lower-entropy policy is more concentrated and may therefore achieve higher single-response accuracy (pass@1). Figure 4 shows that this does not explain our results.
Across all three settings, D2-OPD remains among the higher-entropy students while achieving the best average score. TOP-D and Direct-OPD, which are also among the stronger baselines, generally
lie in the higher-entropy group as well.

6 Conclusion
We presented D2-OPD, decoupling extrapolation direction from distance in extrapolative on-policy distillation.
The method combines two directional signals while enforcing a per-prefix KL bound on the target’s distance from the teacher.
On three teacher–student settings, D2-OPD outperforms OPD baselines on mathematical reasoning benchmarks.
Ablations show that combined direction signals, the distance constraint, and forward-KL distillation each contribute to these gains.
Limitations
Our experiments cover three teacher–student pairs from the Qwen3 family and
three mathematical-reasoning benchmarks. They do not establish whether the
same gains hold for other model families or tasks. The direction construction
uses a teacher reference checkpoint when one is available. Without that
checkpoint, the two directions coincide, so the method cannot use the teacher’s
post-training shift as a separate signal. The distance bound and soft
extrapolation coefficient are hyperparameters; the method does not determine
an optimal extrapolation distance. The parameter study also shows that the
choice of distance bound matters across settings.
Ethical Considerations
Distillation can transfer undesirable behavior from the teacher, including biased or harmful outputs.
Our mathematical-reasoning evaluations do not assess these behaviors or privacy risks.
Training and evaluation also incur energy costs.

Appendix A Training and evaluation details
Our experiments focus on mathematical reasoning, with evaluation on English-language competition problems.
We do not assess performance across languages, specific linguistic phenomena, or demographic groups.
Training.
All methods share the same default training setup of Li et al. (2026), except that we set the maximum
response length to 4,096 rather than 7,168 tokens. This lies between the 3K and 7K moderate
lengths that they report as yielding the strongest results (Li et al., 2026).
Table 6 lists the default training hyperparameters.
Evaluation.
We report avg@16 using temperature 0.7, top-pp sampling with p=0.95p=0.95, and a maximum response length of 31,744 tokens.
Responses are graded using the rule-based verifier of Sheng et al. (2025). Each setting is evaluated with the same template used during training.
Hardware and compute.
All methods were trained on AWS nodes with eight NVIDIA A100 40GB GPUs per node.
Settings A and B used one node, and Setting C used two nodes.
Training for the reported experiments required approximately 9,000 GPU-hours across 50 runs:
33 in Setting A, eight in B, and nine in C.
These runs cover the main comparisons, ablations, parameter sweeps, and constraint-activity analysis.
The calculation uses approximate average training times of 20, 16, and 18 hours per run in Settings A, B, and C, respectively.

Appendix F Computational-cost experiment setup
We use the model checkpoints from Setting A on two NVIDIA A100 40GB GPUs on one node.
All methods process the same saved batch of 16 prompts with four responses per prompt.
The maximum prompt and response lengths are 1,024 and 4,096 tokens, respectively.
Timing a full training step would include differences in the responses generated by each method.
In particular, response length affects both generation and training time.
Reusing the same saved responses fixes the token sequences and their lengths, allowing us to compare the cost of computing supervision and updating the student.
We discard the first two training steps as warm-up and report the mean time over the following three steps.
The timed computation includes model scoring, target or reward construction, and student optimization.
Throughput counts valid prompt and response tokens across both GPUs.

Connection to response length.
ExOPD reports increasing response length under stronger extrapolation and identifies amplification of biased log-ratios as a possible source of instability (Yang et al., 2026).
The fixed-coefficient counterexample above describes one mechanism that could contribute to this behavior.
If teacher-rare tokens that prolong or repeat reasoning receive spuriously large scores, fixed-strength extrapolation can assign them substantial probability.
Our KL constraint limits this amplification at each prefix.
