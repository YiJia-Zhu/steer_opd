Abstract
On-policy distillation (OPD) is emerging as an important paradigm for language-model post-training, combining dense teacher supervision with training on student-generated trajectories. This creates a feedback loop where each update changes the student and thereby the training distribution that generates subsequent updates. Yet, how this feedback shapes learning and generalization remains poorly understood.
We conduct a mechanistic analysis of OPD’s learning dynamics, distinguishing the two feedback loop stages: conversion of the distillation signal into parameter updates, and broadcast through which these updates change predictions across prefixes and reshape the rollout distribution.
This framework helps us understand both limited student responsiveness despite substantial teacher-student disagreement, and degeneration when updates that improve local teacher agreement also increase the probability of unsuccessful continuations, exposing the student to unreliable supervision in subsequent rollouts.
Finally, we show that offline distillation can prepare a student for OPD without significantly improving immediate held-out accuracy. While OPD alone may degrade generation, it can yield substantial accuracy gains after sufficient offline preparation. Together, our results connect teacher supervision, student geometry, and the evolving rollout distribution in determining the learning dynamics of OPD and its effects on downstream performance.
1 Introduction
On-policy distillation (OPD) is becoming an important component of language-model post-training (Lu and Thinking Machines Lab, 2025; Song and Zheng, 2026a). By matching a teacher’s next-token distributions at student-generated prefixes, it combines dense supervision with training on the states the student actually visits (Gu et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2024). Yet, how this local supervision becomes useful learning over an evolving training distribution remains insufficiently understood. It is therefore important to theoretically understand both the conditions under which OPD improves generalization and its role alongside offline distillation.
The difficulty is that each OPD update changes both the student’s current behavior and the prefixes on which it will subsequently learn. Reducing teacher-student divergence on currently supervised predictions does not necessarily produce parameters from which further updates are useful (Li et al., 2026b; Armandpour et al., 2026). Modelling this requires a view of how supervision changes behavior beyond the current batch and how that impacts learning at later stages. We conduct a mechanistic analysis, aided by a first-principles account, of OPD learning dynamics to study this process, explain existing empirical challenges, and justify their empirical diagnostics and fixes.
We first characterize how the teacher-student log-probability difference can lead to meaningful weight updates, which depends on the overlap between the distillation signal and the logit-space directions the student’s current parameters can express. We then describe how learning at one prefix affects predictions at future ones through their dependence on shared parameter directions. We formalize this broadcast relationship through the student’s tangent kernel between the two prefixes, building on Ren and Sutherland (2025); Malladi et al. (2023). Finally, we connect each update to its effect on the outcomes of student-generated sequences. The effect of these updates in broader settings also depends on what the training data covers: matching the teacher on narrow tasks may transfer only the capabilities that those tasks exercise. Integrating these effects over training characterizes what OPD can reach from a given starting point, accounting for how much successive updates improve or degrade performance.
This framework helps us understand two primary failure modes of OPD: stagnation under stronger teachers and degeneration into incoherent outputs (Li et al., 2026b; Fu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026). In the first case, despite substantial teacher–student log-probability differences, we show that the initial distillation signal can induce smaller parameter gradients as training proceeds, contributing to stalled improvement. In the second case, updates that reduce teacher–student disagreement at supervised prefixes also change predictions at other prefixes through shared parameters. These changes can shift the rollout distribution toward continuations the student cannot execute reliably, making those unstable continuations the source of subsequent supervision, making the teacher struggle to rescue, let alone improve the student.

In principle, OPD propagates current teacher requests Γt​(s)|s∼μt\Gamma_{t}(s)|_{s\sim\mu_{t}} to motion at any future prefix xx through shared parameters, captured by the Neural Tangent Kernel K⁡(x,s)K(x,s). Such policy motion shapes how the autoregressive rollout (prefix visitation) and supervision distribution changes (μ˙t,Γ˙t\dot{\mu}_{t},\dot{\Gamma}_{t}). In practice, a healthy dynamics needs not only locally “tangent-accessible” and coherent requests (Section 3.2), but also the student to be sufficiently “prepared”, so that it can sustain meaningful rollouts with learning value as it absorbs corrections requested by the teacher, instead of primarily performing local mimicry and falling into chaotic rollouts hard for the teacher to rescue, let alone improve on (Sections 3.3 and 3.4).
The dependence of OPD on the student sustaining stable rollouts (prefixes) which induce meaningful supervision motivates offline preparation. Here, the weak student is first forced to visit the teacher’s prefixes and learn predictions there, enabling the subsequent OPD process to sustain productive learning as the student exposes finer correction opportunities to the teacher, while maintaining stabler and more recoverable changes in its rollout distribution. Across Qwen-2.5 and Llama-3.1 models on AIME 2024 and 2025 problems, we find that under sufficient offline training, the subsequent OPD phase substantially improve pass@kk (for kk up to 32) and per-problem reliability compared to offline training alone. In the rest of the paper, we present these results and insights organized under the following three questions. What determines the conversion of teacher supervision into policy motion (Section 3.2)? Does the broadcast of these updates across prefixes improve subsequent reasoning or steer the student toward unreliable continuations and supervision (Section 3.3)? Can offline preparation strengthen the student’s continuation ability and enable productive on-policy learning (3.4)?
Related works. We briefly discuss related works here, and leave the expanded version to Appendix Appendix B. On-policy distillation. Training on learner-generated states with expert feedback is a classical approach to addressing distribution shift in imitation learning (Ross et al., 2011), applied to autoregressive distillation by Lin et al. (2020). Gu et al. (2024) develop reverse-KL distillation using student rollouts, while Agarwal et al. (2024) unify on-policy and off-policy training across multiple divergences. Lu and Thinking Machines Lab (2025) demonstrate its effectiveness for reasoning and personalization in language-model post-training. More recently, Yang et al. (2026) interpret OPD as dense KL-constrained reinforcement learning and introduce reward extrapolation. Empirical studies identify teacher-student compatibility on student prefixes as important factors in transfer and training failure (Li et al., 2026b; Fu et al., 2026).
Learning dynamics of finetuning. Kernel-based analyses connect parameter updates to changes in language-model predictions (Malladi et al., 2023). Ren and Sutherland (2025) decompose per-step prediction changes using the loss gradient and empirical tangent kernel, analyzing confidence dynamics in SFT and DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024). Tomihari (2026) study how RL updates propagate across examples, while Shen et al. (2026) identify persistent low-dimensional subspaces in OPD updates. Building on these perspectives, we connect the student’s evolving tangent kernel to its response to teacher supervision, the quality of its continuations, and the resulting distribution of future training prefixes. This distinguishes limited response to supervision from policy changes that worsen generation, and explains how either can shape subsequent learning.
2 Setup
On-policy distillation (OPD) trains a student on its own samples. The student generates a continuation from a prompt. At every prefix of that continuation, the training loss pulls the student’s next-token distribution toward the next-token distribution of a fixed teacher (Gu et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2024; Lu and Thinking Machines Lab, 2025). This section fixes the notation for the objects that recur in the paper, including the policies, the source distribution that a run trains on, the request field that the teacher induces on that source, and the evaluation targets that we score runs against.

The kernel KtK_{t} measures how source and receiver logits move together through shared parameters; 𝖠tp\mathsf{A}_{t}^{p} converts logit motion into log-probability motion. Therefore, the model’s prediction at any “receiver” prefix xx can change without appearing in the training batch.
In particular, since every prefix xx in a future rollout (from the future policy) is a receiver, (1) OPD updates will affect local per-prefix predictions of the student, captured by the logit motion z˙​(x)\dot{z}(x) and policy motion ∂logp(⋅∣x)\partial\log p(\cdot\mid x), (2) the updates will also shape which prefixes the student visits (changed μ\mu) and expose to teacher supervision (changed Γ\Gamma), as the policy motion along the ancestor tokens of any prefix xx accumulate to change its reach-probability.
Let us look at the illustrative process in Figure 1. Intuitively, imagine that the earlier requests locally promote the student to reason more and in specific manners (for instance, by requesting more “wait”-based backtracking, suppressing early “Answer:” commitment, asking for a specific theorem, and so on). Due to the autoregressive nature of the policies, this will not only affect how likely the student emits specific tokens such as “wait” or “answer:” in a future rollout of the student, but will also alter the overall rollout distribution, by lengthening the reasoning trace, exposing new reasoning sub-trajectories, different errors, etc. In the benign case, such online dynamics can be beneficial, as the teacher can correct the student in real time as if in a “tutorial” session.
However, one can also imagine that such a dynamical process can be quite fragile for a ill-prepared student. A weak student’s tangent map Φ\Phi simply might not be very sensitive to the requests, resulting in limited motion propagated from training to future prefixes via kernel KK, thus limited changes in task accuracy. Moreover, even if there is substantial motion, such motion can be harmful. Taking our analogy further, an ill-prepared student which has not “gone to class before the tutorial” might benefit very little from online corrections on its own rollouts and even become more confused. That is, the ill-prepared student which has not been pretrained properly on the task and sustain sufficiently strong rollouts might only mimic superficial local patterns requested by the teacher, and push its rollout population μ\mu towards chaos, which can then be hard for the teacher to generate the right request population Γ\Gamma to even rescue, let alone improve.
The rest of our analysis builds on the OPD loop and the response law to make precise the above intuitions, complemented by empirical analysis of real OPD runs, and examine how offline-distillation preparation before OPD helps alleviate the problems for weak student models. Section 3.2 (conversion) studies the student’s response to the supervised requests, which primarily concerns whether and how well a student model can convert different request populations Γ\Gamma (from different teachers) into policy motion z˙\dot{z} along the request fields. We develop early-training diagnostics of OPD runs from this perspective. Section 3.3 (broadcast) then focuses on where policy motion z˙\dot{z} goes and how it influences future training sources μ\mu, and analyze how and why policy motion which improves local per-prefix readouts (such as training loss) might not make positive progress on terminal readouts such as answer accuracy. Motivated by the failure mechanisms, we then show in Section 3.4, across Qwen-2.5 and Llama-3.1 models, that with sufficient offline preparation, OPD runs improve pooled held-out accuracy (up to pass@32) and aggregate per-problem reliability on AIME 2024 and 2025 problems.
In the rest of this section, we primarily rely on the continuous gradient flow for cleaner theoretical discussions. Please see Section C.2 for further details on the finite step approximations.
3.2 Conversion: Teacher requests and the student’s responsiveness
Figure 2: Initial/end AIME accuracy; diamonds mark teachers. Red: sibling-taught; blue: larger-teacher taught.
A strong teacher can transfer little. We open with an intriguing empirical puzzle identified in the OPD community (Li et al., 2026b): a larger (same-family) teacher can transfer less than an RL-trained sibling even when they are nearly matched in bechmark performance, and Li et al. (2026b) suggests cancellation among token-level gradient signals as a possible explanation. We revisit this comparison at 1.5B and 7B (Figure 2), holding the starting DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen student and OPD recipe fixed within each scale. JustRL-1.5B and AceReason-Nemotron-7B yield substantial gains, whereas the larger DeepSeek-7B and DeepSeek-14B teachers transfer little throughout training despite higher benchmark scores (Section D.1). We call these runs healthy and stalled. We find that the teachers’ request fields differ substantially in semantic functions, and that the student’s responsiveness to these requests separates in two axes: coherence of the gradient signals, and kernel adaptation toward the requests. Along the way, we develop early-training diagnostics that help reveal the “request-conversion” health of a training run.

How do the request fields differ?
As we see in the OPD update loop in Section 3.1, the teacher’s request population Γt\Gamma_{t} at time 0 “kickstart” the whole learning process, from which the shared-parameter student model propagates into policy motion via its kernel KtK_{t}. Since the student starts out to be the same while the teachers are different across the healthy and stalled runs, their request fields become the natural entry point for our analysis.
On fixed initial-student rollouts, Figure 3 compares where each teacher’s largest-normed requests occur. (a) shows the coarse tendencies of the semantics of the requests as we move up the request-norm percentile, while (b) and (c) show the finer semantic categories and contrast the teachers’ distinct request fields. We find that the RL-sibling teachers increasingly emphasize reasoning-management and initiating positions, while the larger teachers place relatively more of their strongest requests at technically oriented (e.g. digit-bearing, recalling mathematical fact) positions. This separation connects to findings in the reasoning-interpretability community, that RL tends to improve how well the model “orchestrates” the base reasoning mechanisms (including epistemic control abilities) (Venhoff et al., 2025a). Full setup and analysis results are in Section D.2.

3.3 Broadcast: why local agreement need not improve reasoning
Under JustRL-1.5B, both DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B and Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct substantially reduce their training loss. Yet DeepSeek gains accuracy (Section 3.2), while the base Qwen 1.5B model declines substantially on MATH within 100 steps. Why does learning from the same teacher help one learner and harm the other?

Policy motion and the shifting value of continuations.
So far we see that the weak Qwen model’s failed run has the OPD update loop spending later, more “stable” drift-dominated updates learning from unreliable requests on the low-quality generative distribution. This helps us better understand how the training loss declines. Intuitively, fitting unreliable teacher supervision on high-entropy rollouts already suggests that this will not yield a better-reasoning student model. To understand how and why this process worsens terminal score more precisely, we need to turn to Equation 9: policy motion influences non-local readouts (including terminal score) through the value / advantage of the student’s continuations (namely the Q−VQ-V advantage summed across the prefixes), which can be in conflict with local teacher agreement.
While it is prohibitively expensive to directly compute the “velocity×\timesadvantage” term in Equation 9, we find on select banks of probing rollouts and continuations that as training proceeds, the model increasingly make local decisions whose continuation correctness decline, and complementarily, visit prefixes that are difficult to reach the right answer at increasingly shallower depth. This tells us that the per-token policy velocity of the model tend to align with “negative advantage” over the course of training, resulting in the overall decline of terminal accuracy. We elaborate on these studies below.
First, to probe the continuation-value term in Equation 9, we freeze a set of answer-decision prefixes from the initial student’s held-out MATH rollouts and intervene on the local “answer-versus-continue” decision token position. The student’s probability of the answer branch falls from 0.74→0.19→0.070.74\to 0.19\to 0.07 at steps 0, 20, 100, below the teacher’s 0.300.30 on the same prefixes. At update 20, while “routing” is dominating, counterfactual continuations from the two branches have similar downstream value (correct 0.300.30 for the “answer” branch and 0.290.29 for the “continue” branch), i.e. the student still maintains reasonable answer-reaching abilities. By update 100, however, the continue-branch rarely produces the answer (8%8\% of continuations). The teacher’s own continuations from the same prefixes is correct 0.600.60 of the time, which is much higher, so the gap is in the student’s degraded execution of the action branch, not in the branch itself. For a complete treatment of how we decompose and estimate Equation 9 see Section E.4.

Preparation permits a different trajectory.
The base Qwen model, prepared with offline distillation training on teacher rollouts, also undergoes substantial prefix visitation shifts, but sustains useful learning. Unlike DeepSeek’s smoother refinement, its loss and checking behavior are nonmonotone: a routing-dominated overshoot in loss and reasoning (epistemic) behaviors are followed by lower-entropy rollouts and conditional moderation of reasoning behaviors dominated by drift. Its held-out gains suggest that a stronger starting policy with higher prefix quality and continuation-readiness makes these excursions amenable to correction. Full analysis in Section E.5.

Section 3.3 suggests a direct intervention. As we saw in the last sub-section, OPD can fail because early policy changes route the weak student model into reasoning states it cannot yet execute reliably, and the training run spends a large chunk of time fitting unreliable requests on increasingly lower-quality prefixes. Then, a natural candidate intervention is that, before OPD, we force the student to visit higher-quality prefixes from the teacher itself and learn the teacher’s stronger continuations on those prefix populations. This suggests offline distillation to prepare the student model’s continuation abilities before running OPD, which can then expose finer student errors to the teacher and sustain more stable learning trajectory for online corrections when OPD changes prefix visitation, without the requests looking so foreign to the student in the OPD phase that it routes into irrecoverable rollout distributions.

4 Conclusion
Findings.
We studied on-policy distillation as a feedback loop between teacher supervision, student response, and the rollout distribution that supplies future supervision.
Our analysis separates two mechanisms in this loop: conversion, and broadcast. Across our settings, these mechanisms explain both stalled transfer and deterioration despite improving local teacher agreement. Offline preparation changes the starting point of this loop: even with little immediate accuracy improvement, it can enable
substantial gains from subsequent OPD.
Limitations and discussion.
Our empirical analysis is confined to mathematical reasoning and a select set of LLM models due to resource limitations. We also present a more detailed Q&A in Appendix A to further clarify potential ambiguities in our work.

Appendix A Mechanistic interpretation and key questions
Q1. What do the response law and decompositions in Sections 3.1, 3.2 and 3.3 explain beyond their familiar mathematical ingredients?
They provide a connected mechanistic account of how dense local supervision can produce either weak transfer or deteriorating generation. OPD updates the policy that generates its next training population. Understanding its dynamics therefore requires following supervision beyond the current loss, through its effects on future behavior and training histories (student’s rollouts and the induced supervision signals / request field).
The theory separates three links in this process. First, Section 3.2 explains how a teacher’s requests become parameter motion: accessibility determines their expression through the student’s tangent map, while coherence determines how the resulting gradients combine. Teacher benchmark performance, magnitude of (KL-based) disagreement, or gradient norm alone cannot characterize this conversion. Second, Section 3.3 distinguishes changing a prediction from changing the ancestral decisions that make its history likely. Shared-parameter broadcast can increase exposure to a continuation without making the student better at executing it; the resulting histories then change the supervision received from the same fixed teacher. Third, Equation 9 connects the delivered response to completed outcomes through what the student’s remaining policy can achieve, separating teacher matching from useful reasoning.
Together, these links explain the structure of the empirical puzzles. The stronger-teacher study exposes the importance of gradient coherence and tangent accessibility; the degenerate run exposes that, under substantial policy motion, the degree of freedom granted to the learner to generate its own rollouts (training histories) can make it generate the new rollouts (reasoning states) which it has difficulty managing and grows uncertain on, inducing poorer-quality supervision signals. Loss reduction dominated by local fitting on these poorly supervised rollouts cannot improve the learner’s terminal scores. The mechanisms revealed in the degenerate run motivates preparing the student for the continuations that OPD will elicit, even when preparation does not substantially improve current accuracy by itself. The theoretical contribution is the integrated explanation that makes these mechanisms distinguishable, connects them to outcomes, and motivates an intervention that boosts the model’s learning stability and generalization.
Q2. Why is every training run reported with a single seed?
Cost. The runs behind this paper were trained and evaluated on GH200 nodes, and the single-seed set already consumed roughly 4,000 GPU-hours, which is at the limit of our computing resources. OPD is known to be computationally demanding, we therefore designed the experiments so that the main comparisons do not rest on seed variance. The mechanism measurements of Sections 3.2 and 3.3 are exact identities evaluated within a run, on fixed receiver banks and fixed checkpoints, and their uncertainty is reported at the level of problems or sites (paired bootstrap intervals). The outcome comparisons of Section 3.4 are paired within a student-teacher pair on the same problems and replicated across three pairs and two student scales. What single seeds leave unquantified is run-to-run variation of the training trajectories themselves.
Q3. Why does Section 3.3 need both prefix-level routing/drift (Equation 5) and trajectory-level continuation advantages (Equation 9) to explain degeneration?
For loss or entropy, JY=𝔼νt​Yt​(x)J_{Y}=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}}Y_{t}(x) describes predictions on the currently observed histories. Cross-scoring separates changes in those histories from changes in predictions on the same histories. In Figure 5, the initial student is already highly uncertain on the deep histories that later become prevalent, while subsequent fitting sharpens them. On identical batch-41 histories, discrepancy falls 0.79→0.060.79\to 0.06 and entropy 4.99→3.994.99\to 3.99. Together with the stopping/reach probes, these measurements distinguish routing followed by fitting from uniform entropy-increasing drift (Sections E.3 and E.3.1).
But fitting a prefix and improving the completion from it are different effects. For terminal correctness, JY=𝔼Πt​YJ_{Y}=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi_{t}}Y, and the conditional value VtY​(x)V_{t}^{Y}(x) depends on every downstream decision. At an intermediate cut of the autoregressive trace, its derivative separates changes in reaching the cut from changes in the remaining policy. Unrolling the latter gives the continuation-advantage weighting in Equation 9; replacing it with a derivative of the logits at xx alone would omit downstream effects (Section C.8). Thus, a teacher-preferred next token need not help if the student cannot complete the reasoning it initiates. The prefix analysis establishes the observed routing-then-fitting pattern; the outcome analysis explains why local fitting need not restore correctness. Entropy enters the argument only as a prefix-level readout.
Q4. Could the responsiveness changes in Section 3.2 (Figure 4) simply reflect changing teacher–student disagreement or generic kernel movement?
A live accessibility measure At​[Bt]A_{t}[B_{t}] can change because either the requests or the kernel change. We therefore fix the initial numerical requests B0B_{0}, exact histories, and output coordinates across checkpoints. Since only the kernel varies in At​[B0]A_{t}[B_{0}], its change measures responsiveness to the same supervision (Section C.4).
To test whether eigenspace movement reflects teacher-specific adaptation, we evaluate both teachers’ frozen fields in the same checkpoint kernel and compare their projection gains, separating preferential reorientation from shared movement that benefits both fields. Healthy 1.5B exhibits preferential reorientation; healthy 7B mainly amplifies existing directions; stalled runs lose sensitivity to the fixed requests (Section D.5). These findings complement the initial accessibility/coherence decomposition, which exposes conversion differences concealed by comparable gradient norms across the runs.
Q5. What does preparation in Sections 3.4 and 8 contribute beyond offline warm-up, and how does Section 3.3 motivate it?
The contribution is an explanation of why the value of a warm-up stage, already used in prior work (Appendix B), should be assessed through subsequent learning. The failure mechanism identifies a mismatch between making extended reasoning likely and being able to execute it. Offline distillation supplies teacher-generated reasoning histories without requiring the initial student to generate them first. It can therefore prepare continuation skills and reasoning-state management that become useful as OPD changes visitation: continuation readiness, a benefit beyond higher starting accuracy – our pass@k results clearly demonstrate this.
Appendix B Expanded related work
B.1 Core related works
Distillation and the OPD loop.
Knowledge distillation transfers teacher outputs (Hinton et al., 2015), including teacher-generated sequences (Kim and Rush, 2016). DAgger, the seminal online imitation algorithm, addresses distribution shift through expert feedback on learner-visited states (Ross et al., 2011); Lin et al. (2020) develop this connection for autoregressive distillation. MiniLLM uses reverse KL and student-generated samples (Gu et al., 2024), (Agarwal et al., 2024; Piskorz et al., 2026) study the choice of divergence loss, and Lu and Thinking Machines Lab (2025) demonstrate practical OPD for reasoning and personalization. These methods motivate our question: how does dense supervision on student histories produce successful transfer, stagnation, or degeneration? We develop a systematic first-principles account of the coupled loop through which shared-parameter updates change predictions, renew visited histories, and reshape subsequent supervision.
Kernel response laws.
The neural tangent kernel connects parameter learning to function-space change (Jacot et al., 2018). Malladi et al. (2023) analyze language-model finetuning through kernels, including an Adam extension; Ren and Sutherland (2025) decompose prediction changes through normalization, empirical-kernel, and loss-gradient terms for SFT and preference finetuning, including offline DPO. Tomihari (2026) studies update propagation in RL post-training. OPD needs different theoretical and empirical machinery for two reasons. Its on-policy sampling moves the learning distribution itself, so the analysis must track this motion, which is absent from the offline algorithms (SFT and offline DPO) studied by Ren and Sutherland (2025). Its supervision is also dense and token-level and can be disconnected from the reasoning outcome (such as accuracy), whereas RL gives only sparse, outcome-level learning signals.
Nevertheless, the fundamental response law of the shared-parameter neural learner is shared with Ren and Sutherland (2025). We then follow the induced motion through the rest of the OPD loop: changes at ancestor decisions alter reach probabilities, renew the training source, and thereby change the requests driving future updates. Combining the evolving kernel, routing/drift split, and continuation-value analysis yields a mechanistic account of how optimization changes future supervision and outcomes (Sections 3 and C).
Cumulative weight geometry vs the geometry of request conversion.
Shen et al. (2026) find low stable rank and early stabilization of dominant subspaces in cumulative weight-matrix changes; constrained-training experiments test those subspaces’ functional sufficiency. We instead analyze the current logit Jacobian and the response it delivers to specified teacher requests. The Jacobian can change even when cumulative displacement is low-rank. Frozen histories, coordinates, and requests isolate amplification and reorientation; crossed teacher fields test preferential adaptation in the same kernel (Sections C.4 and D.5). Distinguishing local accessibility from collective coherence further explains why individually expressible requests can cancel. These analyses concern how geometry enables or obstructs transfer.
Local fitting vs task performance.
Policy-gradient theory (Sutton et al., 1999) and cost-to-go-aware imitation learning (Ross and Bagnell, 2014) connect actions to subsequent outcomes. G-OPD interprets distillation through KL-constrained RL and reward extrapolation (Yang et al., 2026). Armandpour et al. (2026) use student continuation-success probabilities to derive an ideal local logit direction and measure its alignment with distillation signals (however, they acknowledge that such estimations in long-CoT reasoning can be very noisy). Uemura and Suzuki (2026) theoretically studies the dynamics of self-distillation for a single-layer neural network, and analyzes how the supervision affects long-horizon traversal of directed acyclic graphs, invoking tools such as future advantage in their analysis. Our analysis connects this type of outcome perspective to delivered motion: ∑sKt​(x,s)​Γt​(s)\sum_{s}K_{t}(x,s)\Gamma_{t}(s) includes the effects of all coupled source prefixes in addition to the request at xx. Crucially, prefix readouts 𝔼νt​Yt​(x)\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}}Y_{t}(x) and terminal outcomes 𝔼Πt​Y\mathbb{E}_{\Pi_{t}}Y are different objects. At an intermediate cut-position in the autoregressive rollout, terminal change splits into the change in reaching that prefix and the change in the continuations; unrolling the latter and recombining both terms yields the advantage-weighted contribution of every decision (Equations 9 and C.8). This separates reaching a prefix, fitting there, and completing it successfully –the distinction underlying the degeneration mechanism and continuation readiness.
Degeneration involves source feedback as well as supervision quality.
Fu et al. (2026) study unreliable supervision and implementation choices; Jin et al. (2026a) augment high-entropy teacher supervision with forward KL; and Zhu et al. (2026) analyze signals induced by top-KK truncation, renormalization, and stop-gradient conventions. Li et al. (2026b) also observe deterioration with prefix depth. Contemporaneous work by Wang et al. (2026) studies teacher mismatch and length exploitation. Our first-principled analysis reveals a phased failure mechanism: early routing makes already-uncertain continuations prevalent, after which substantial fitting can sharpen those very histories without restoring useful generation (Sections E.3 and E.3.1). Our routing-drift machinery, coupled with the learning-value decomposition (for terminal-accuracy readout) distinguish the failure mechanism from a naive, uniform entropy-increasing learning process. The important feedback is that the histories made common by earlier updates become the source of later supervision. The prepared learner’s different trajectory further shows why source movement itself is not synonymous with failure, and its continuation-readiness helps in exposing useful subsequent learning.
Offline preparation can improve readiness for learning without improving immediate accuracy.
Li et al. (2026b) motivate cold-start SFT by reducing thinking-pattern mismatch and improving overlap. Their §5.1/Figure 8 studies a strong standalone-SFT regime: the SFT initialization already exceeds the direct-OPD endpoint on all three plotted benchmarks, with little further gains from OPD. We study a different regime, where the student-teacher gap is significant, and preparation leaves starting accuracy near the unprepared student’s but changes subsequent OPD from deterioration to substantial improvement. The principled motivation is continuation readiness: the outcome analysis and observed failure mechanisms in Section 3.3 identify a distinction between increasing a history’s reach and executing its continuation successfully. Teacher-generated trajectories provide training on such histories before the student must reach them itself. Our dose study exposes deferred benefits and a sharply nonmonotonic stability pattern at 7B; the prepared-run analysis traces a different subsequent routing/fitting process (Tables 14 and E.5).
In contemporaneous work Simple-OPD (Liu et al., 2026) studies warm-up data, duration, and LoRA parameterization, including benefits from incorrect teacher-compatible traces and differences between SFT and downstream rankings. These findings complement our mechanistic account linking preparation to source feedback and continuation values. The key result is an altered learning trajectory; the preparation dose-response experiments demonstrate this interesting benefit.
B.2 More related works
Reasoning interpretability. Part of the focus of our work is on how LLMs learn to reason (from a teacher model), and our analysis methodology is partly inspired by the discoveries in the reasoning interpretability community. Venhoff et al. (2025a) is especially relevant, as they discovered that RL-trained models tend to acquire the ability to manage “when” to fire a reasoning mechanism (be it an “epistemic control” mechanism such as backtracking or planning, or computation mechanisms like estimating an inequality); Marjanovic et al. (2026) directly studies the chain-of-thought reasoning mechanisms of DeepSeek-R1 models, which our DeepSeek student models are distilled from, also inspiring our analysis on the epistemic control transmission in OPD. More broadly, Bogdan et al. (2025); Baek and Tegmark (2025); Venhoff et al. (2025b); Troitskii et al. (2025); Hong et al. (2025); Ward et al. (2025) identify causal mechanisms behind LLMs’ reasoning behaviors in logic, mathematics and other related domains.
OPD and its variants. On-policy distillation is becoming increasingly popular in industry post-training pipelines (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2026; Team et al., 2026). Many variants are being proposed, including selecting which tokens to perform supervision on (Jin et al., 2026b; Yu et al., 2026), and curriculum-styled variants (Ziheng et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a). The survey on OPD Song and Zheng (2026b) is also a good reference for understanding the research landscape in this area.
Learning dynamics. Many classic works lie in this space and are predecessors to recent studies of LLM learning dynamics. Biehl and Schwarze (1995); Saad and Solla (1995); Saxe et al. (2014); Goldt et al. (2019) are early representative works, studying how learning trajectories and generalization depend on the model, initialization, and training process, and providing foundations for our analysis of how teacher supervision changes the student over time. Furthermore, a particular line of work study feature learning in nonlinear neural networks: Baratin et al. (2021); Atanasov et al. (2022); Hong et al. (2024); Allen-Zhu and Li (2022); Allen-Zhu and Li (2023a) study how the nonlinear neural network’s geometry evolves and aligns with the task, showing feature re-orientation early on in (pre-)training that precedes appreciable loss reduction (sometimes called the “lottery winning” phase, where certain neurons or features better align towards loss-reducing directions early in training without substantial loss-reduction yet). These works are especially relevant to our analysis of the student’s changing responsiveness to teacher requests in Section 3.2, and to offline preparation whose benefits become apparent through subsequent learning. Additionally, another relevant line of work in the pre-LLM era study dynamics of DNN distillation. Phuong and Lampert (2019); Ji and Zhu (2020); Hong et al. (2021); Allen-Zhu and Li (2023b); Panahi et al. (2022); He and Ozay (2022) examine how the geometry of the learning problem, teacher knowledge, and the student’s representation affect transfer, using linear, deep linear, or kernel-based analyses of the SGD-based learning trajectory.

Appendix D Conversion: protocols and additional measurements
This appendix gives the experimental details behind Section 3.2. We first describe the model pairs and the fixed prefixes used for measurement. We then ask where the teachers place their strongest requests, how well those requests produce parameter updates, and how the student’s responsiveness changes during training. The derivations are in Sections C.3 and C.4.
D.1 Setup
We compare two teachers for the same starting student at each scale. At 1.5B, the student is DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B and the teachers are JustRL-1.5B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B. At 7B, the student is DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B and the teachers are AceReason-Nemotron-7B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B. The AIME comparison uses 30 AIME 2024 problems at temperature .6.6. We draw 16 responses per problem at 1.5B and 4 at 7B.
At each scale, eleven rollouts from the initial student provide the prefixes for measurement. Each local kernel is computed from logit gradients with respect to all student parameters. Please note that, computing the gradients is expensive, the eigendecomposition itself is inexpensive. The kernel measurements therefore use sampled positions, while Ξ\Xi serves as a less expensive monitor.
D.2 Where the strongest requests occur
We analyze which kinds of positions receive the strongest teacher requests. The annotated rollouts contain 36,416 positions at 1.5B and 38,168 at 7B. For each teacher, the strongest 1%1\% contains 364 and 381 positions, respectively.
Annotations and enrichment.
We use Opus 5 to label the sentences in the rollouts. Each sentence receives a semantic category, which describes its role in the reasoning. We also classify positions by their location within a sentence and by token kind. These are three separate classifications of the same positions, examined from different semantic aspects. We use the following prompt to create the semantic labels (this rubric is the result of iterating on the model’s reasoning traces a few times, to settle on the most prevalent reasoning categories the model writes in its chain of thought).
LLM Judge Prompt
You are annotating every sentence of a model’s reasoning trace (for example, a model solving a math problem) with a label describing the act the sentence performs.
REASONING TRACE: "{reasoning_trace}"
Emit per sentence: {"sent_idx": int, "fine": [1 label], "uncertain": bool, "note": "..."} Read the FULL trace first; judge each sentence’s FUNCTION IN CONTEXT.
## Fine labels
CONTROL family (steering the reasoning process):
- check-initiate: starting a verification act on ALREADY-ESTABLISHED material -- re-computing a prior result, re-reading the problem statement, re-deriving an earlier step. First-time testing of a fresh candidate or case is NOT check-initiate (see TECHNICAL).
- check-conclude: verdict closing a check episode. Covers: work-correctness verdicts ("Yes, that’s correct", "that step was wrong"); verdicts about the check itself, including redundancy ("I already did this case", "we’ve already considered that"); resolutions of a problem re-read that settle interpretation ("So it’s allowed to be prime").
- doubt-flag: hesitation OR bare deliberation cue WITHOUT an act ("Wait.", "Hmm, that seems too big.", "Let me think.", "Let’s see."). Judged sentence-locally: a bare cue stays doubt-flag even when a check follows in the next sentence. Never label bare cues as plan.
- plan: stating an approach or opening a new sub-task, with the approach/task STATED ("I’ll use coordinates", new-sub-task colon lead-ins like "Compute the exponent:"). Includes proceed-decisions ("Let me try that").
- reframe: abandoning/switching approach. NOT routine next-case moves in an enumeration ("Alternatively, x=2" = TECHNICAL).
- repair: announcing/making a correction of an earlier error.
- recap: MULTI-ITEM consolidation of established state used to steer. Single value-echoes = compute/derive.
- commit: FINAL-ANSWER commitment only ("So the answer is 34", "Answer: 34").
TECHNICAL family (object-level mathematics):
- derive: algebraic/logical derivation steps; this includes stating the associated problem facts, notation, coordinates, case setup, and within-computation connective lead-ins (including "Simplify:", "Comparing coefficients:").
- recall: invoking a theorem/definition/known mathematical fact.
OTHER family:
- style: pure filler/transition with no content and no steering.
- format: formatting artifacts, the end-of-reasoning marker (e.g. </think>), bare list numerals or LaTeX delimiters split onto their own lines (format, not fragment).
- unclear: genuinely unclassifiable.
## Fixed vocabulary and note rules
Use only the labels above; do not propose or introduce new labels. A sentence the rules do not settle or hard to classify takes the best available existing label, "uncertain": true, and leave a short note -- residual uncertainty is data itself, please do not treat it as a defect to legislate away. Furthermore, write in the note for the sentence if it genuinely has more than one of the labels above.

Appendix E Broadcast: protocols and additional measurements
This appendix gives the experimental details for Section 3.3. We examine how OPD changes prefix visitation and the student’s predictions on fixed prefixes. We then ask whether the student can complete its reasoning from those prefixes and reach a correct answer. The theoretical backbone is derived and presented in Sections C.5, C.6, C.7, C.8 and C.9.
E.1 Models and measurement setup
Models and training.
All three learners use JustRL-1.5B as the teacher. The strong learner is DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B which we also studied in Section 3.2. The unprepared learner is Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct. “Prepared Qwen” starts from the same Qwen 1.5B instruct model after four passes of offline distillation on OpenMathInstruct2 (see the preparation section, Appendix F for more details). The Qwen runs use 200 training steps, 256 rollouts per update, temperature 1.0, and an 8,192-token generation cap. DeepSeek’s generation cap is 4,096 tokens.
Rollout banks.
We save 32 rollouts from each batch ℓ∈{1,21,41,…,161,181,196}\ell\in\{1,21,41,\ldots,161,181,196\}. A bank is this fixed set of rollout texts. Batch ℓ\ell is generated after ℓ−1\ell-1 updates. Thus batch 41 appears at update 40 in the routing and drift plots. Checkpoint 0 is each learner’s starting model, including the offline-prepared model for prepared Qwen.
When does the model initiate checking?
The WAIT family 𝒲\mathcal{W} contains the 13 tokens that decode to “wait” (after whitespace is removed and text is lowercased). We examine candidate sentence starts: the first response position and positions after a token ending in a period, question mark, exclamation mark, colon, or newline. The student and teacher probabilities of this family are denoted as wt​(x)=pt​(𝒲∣x)w_{t}(x)=p_{t}(\mathcal{W}\mid x) and qW​(x)=q​(𝒲∣x)q_{W}(x)=q(\mathcal{W}\mid x). We measure the mean log⁡wt\log w_{t} across candidate starts. For the above- and below-teacher comparisons, we use log-odds bt=log⁡[wt/(1−wt)]b_{t}=\log[w_{t}/(1-w_{t})] and bq=log⁡[qW/(1−qW)]b_{q}=\log[q_{W}/(1-q_{W})].
We form the two groups using a reference checkpoint rr: checkpoint 0 for DeepSeek and checkpoint 20 for the Qwen learners. We include a prefix only if max⁡(wr,qW)≥10−3\max(w_{r},q_{W})\geq 10^{-3} and |br−bq|≥0.5|b_{r}-b_{q}|\geq 0.5. The sign of br−bqb_{r}-b_{q} determines whether the student initially assigns more or less probability than the teacher. Each prefix keeps the same group at every evaluated checkpoint.
We also count emitted WAIT tokens per thousand response tokens, and measure the arithmetic mean of wtw_{t} at candidate starts. These quantities answer different questions. The emission rate counts what the model actually generates. Mean probability and mean log probability describe its actual predictions at the selected prefixes.
To assess agreement with the teacher, we use the binary discrepancy cW=DKL(Ber(qW)∥Ber(wt))c_{W}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\operatorname{Ber}(q_{W})\,\|\,\operatorname{Ber}(w_{t})\right). E.7 Fixed-reference continuation experiments
The answer-versus-continue experiment in Section E.4 conducted a case study probing continuation from fixed prefixes. This, however, is not enough in understanding the decline in terminal score of the trained student model. Recall that, at a high level, the broadcast not only affects continuation behaviors of the model, but also changes its prefix visitation probabilities. Therefore, to understand the terminal-score decline during OPD, we must also consider how the prefixes that the student checkpoints generates as training proceeds. At any rollout depth, successful completion depends on two quantities: which prefixes the student reaches, and how successfully it continues from those prefixes. We first express terminal score in terms of these two quantities, then show how the reference-policy experiment separates their changes.
Prefix visitation and continuation.
Fix a cut depth hh. Recall our notation from before, that dt​(x)d_{t}(x) is the probability that checkpoint tt reaches prefix xx, and VtY​(x)V_{t}^{Y}(x) is its expected terminal score when it completes that prefix. The set of rollout cuts 𝒞h\mathcal{C}_{h} includes depth-hh prefixes and responses that ended earlier (an earlier completed response just retains its terminal score). Conditioning on 𝒞h\mathcal{C}_{h} gives
JY​(t)=∑x∈𝒞hdt​(x)​VtY​(x).J_{Y}(t)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{h}}d_{t}(x)V_{t}^{Y}(x).
This is just the usual average over the “continuation problems” presented to the student by its own generation. Training changes both the weights dtd_{t} and the continuation values VtYV_{t}^{Y}. Comparing each checkpoint’s prefixes using that checkpoint as the continuer therefore only measures their combined effect.
Hold the continuer fixed.
Using the initial student as the reference, add and subtract ∑x∈𝒞hdt​(x)​V0Y​(x)\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{h}}d_{t}(x)V_{0}^{Y}(x) to obtain
JY​(t)−JY​(0)\displaystyle J_{Y}(t)-J_{Y}(0)
=∑x∈𝒞h[dt​(x)−d0​(x)]​V0Y​(x)⏟prefix visitation, evaluated by the initial continuer+∑x∈𝒞hdt​(x)​[VtY​(x)−V0Y​(x)]⏟continuation change on the same current prefixes.\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{h}}[d_{t}(x)-d_{0}(x)]V_{0}^{Y}(x)}_{\text{prefix visitation, evaluated by the initial continuer}}\quad+\underbrace{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{h}}d_{t}(x)[V_{t}^{Y}(x)-V_{0}^{Y}(x)]}_{\text{continuation change on the same current prefixes}}.

The first term compares the initial and trained prefix distributions while keeping the continuer fixed. A negative value means that the initial student finds the trained prefixes harder to finish than its own prefixes. The second term keeps the trained prefix distribution fixed and compares the two continuers. A negative value means that the trained student is less successful than the initial student on those same prefixes. Thus the comparison changes the prefix-generating policy first, then the continuing policy.
Our experiment implements the intermediate case directly: checkpoint tt generates the first hh tokens, and the initial student completes the rest of the rollout. Let us write its expected score as
Ft0​(h)=∑x∈𝒞hdt​(x)​V0Y​(x).F_{t}^{0}(h)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{h}}d_{t}(x)V_{0}^{Y}(x).

The two terms in Equation 25 are therefore Ft0​(h)−JY​(0)F_{t}^{0}(h)-J_{Y}(0) and JY​(t)−Ft0​(h)J_{Y}(t)-F_{t}^{0}(h). They can be estimated by averaging completed responses. The first supplies a common measure of prefix difficulty across checkpoints; the second compares continuers on each checkpoint’s own prefix distribution.
Experimental setup and results.
The study uses 250 held-out MATH problems, two source rollouts per problem from each of checkpoints 50, 100, and 200, and cuts at 256, 512, and 1,024 response tokens. Each active prefix is continued four times by the generating checkpoint, the initial student, and the teacher. All continuers receive the remainder of an 8,192-token total response budget. The readout is extracted-answer success on the full prefix and continuation; the answer need not be immediately followed by an end-of-turn token. Stopping is recorded separately. The estimator averages continuation scores within each prefix, then over the two source rollouts and uniformly over problems, retaining earlier completed responses as above. This population and budget differ from those of the selected answer-boundary probe in Section E.4. The reported values below are rounded sample means; independently sampled self-continuations estimate the same JY​(t)J_{Y}(t) but need not give identical means at different cuts.

Connection to Equation 9.
The same two factors describe the instantaneous dynamics. Differentiating the cut identity gives
J˙Y​(t)=∑x∈𝒞hd˙t​(x)​VtY​(x)+∑x∈𝒞hdt​(x)​V˙tY​(x).\dot{J}_{Y}(t)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{h}}\dot{d}_{t}(x)V_{t}^{Y}(x)+\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{h}}d_{t}(x)\dot{V}_{t}^{Y}(x).
(27)
Under the regularity assumptions in Proposition C.18, the first term computes the contributions to Equation 9 from decisions before the cut, and the second collects those from the cut onward. Both arise from the same shared parameter update. If the reference is checkpoint tt instead of checkpoint 0, and the compared policy is an infinitesimally later checkpoint t+d​tt+dt, the differences in Equation 25 (divided by elapsed training time) recover the derivative, hence recovering these two terms. Such measurements are unrealistic to implement and too noisy to measure in practice, however, so our experiment instead use the initial policy as the reference continuer to magnify the trend of visited-prefix quality / continuation difficulty.
What does moving the cut measure?
Another interesting question is, at a fixed checkpoint tt, moving the rollout cut from h1h_{1} to h2h_{2} lets the trained student generate an additional block of tokens before the initial student takes over. A decrease in Ft0F_{t}^{0} means that this additional trained-student generation leaves the initial continuer less able to reach a correct answer. To connect it to continuation advantages, let us follow the initial student’s value along a trained rollout. For each action decision, 𝒜0Y​(xj,aj)=V0Y​(xj+1)−V0Y​(xj)\mathcal{A}_{0}^{Y}(x_{j},a_{j})=V_{0}^{Y}(x_{j+1})-V_{0}^{Y}(x_{j}). Summing over a block yields a telescoping series over depth of the cuts, then averaging over the rollouts gives
Ft0(h2)−Ft0(h1)=∑x∈𝒮h1≤h⁡(x)<h2dt(x)𝔼a∼πt(⋅∣x)[𝒜0Y(x,a)].F_{t}^{0}(h_{2})-F_{t}^{0}(h_{1})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{S}\\
h_{1}\leq h(x)<h_{2}\end{subarray}}d_{t}(x)\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{t}(\cdot\mid x)}[\mathcal{A}_{0}^{Y}(x,a)].
(28)
Here 𝒮\mathcal{S} contains decision prefixes; completed responses contribute no further decisions and retain their scores. At depth zero, the initial student generates the whole response, so Ft0​(0)=JY​(0)F_{t}^{0}(0)=J_{Y}(0). At the total response cap, the trained student has generated the whole response, so Ft0​(Hmax)=JY​(t)F_{t}^{0}(H_{\max})=J_{Y}(t). Consequently, the differences across any partition of the full response sum to JY​(t)−JY​(0)J_{Y}(t)-J_{Y}(0). Fixed cuts therefore measure aggregate contributions of token blocks to this finite policy comparison, using completed outcomes in place of separate continuation-value estimates at every token.

F.1 Three-family AIME comparison
Students and teachers.
The pairs are Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct / JustRL-1.5B, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct / AceReason-Nemotron-7B, and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct / Llama-3.1-Nemotron-Nano-8B-v1. Each pair shares a tokenizer vocabulary. The fixed teacher supplies the preparation text, the offline-control targets, and the on-policy log-probabilities. The Llama teacher uses its reasoning mode with the system prompt “detailed thinking on.”
Stage 1: offline preparation on OMI2.
Each teacher generates solutions to problems drawn from OpenMathInstruct-2 (Toshniwal et al., 2024), at temperature one with an 8,192-token response cap. The student uses token-level forward KL on the teacher’s recorded top-16 next-token support. The plotted cold start uses 512 optimizer steps of 256 sequences, or 131,072 sequence presentations. The Qwen 1.5B, 7B and Llama corpora each contain 32,768 teacher solutions, so this is four corpus sweeps.
The offline settings are AdamW, learning rate 10−610^{-6}, momentum coefficients (0.9,0.95)(0.9,0.95), no weight decay, gradient clipping at 1.0. The cold-start sequence limit is 8,192 tokens.
Stage 2: DAPO comparisons.
The prompt pool is DAPO-Math-17k (Yu et al., 2025), recorded as 17,398 unique problems. Training uses consecutive blocks of 6,400 rows in the released ordering. Each block contains 6,235–6,318 unique problems.
OPD samples four student responses for each of 64 prompts per step, giving 256 responses and one optimizer step per update. Sampling is at temperature one, capped at 8,192 tokens for Qwen and 14,336 for Llama.
The offline continuation instead uses four teacher-generated solutions for each prompt row in the same blocks: 25,600 sequences per 6,400-row block. It uses the teacher forward-KL objective and optimizer settings as preparation, at 256 sequences per step. The sequence limits are 8,192 for Qwen and 15,360 for Llama, the latter retaining the reported teacher solutions generated with a 14,336-token response cap. Thus the comparison matches the implemented offline and on-policy recipes in prompt blocks, sequence counts, and total optimizer steps.
