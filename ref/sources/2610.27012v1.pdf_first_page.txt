A BSTRACT
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) trains a language model
by sampling responses, scoring them with an automatic verifier, and updating the
policy from the resulting rewards. The policy therefore receives direct learning
signals only from trajectories that appear in its rollouts. Yet repeated sampling
from the same policy can concentrate on similar reasoning paths, leaving useful
alternatives unexplored under a fixed rollout budget. We introduce ActivationSpace Exploration (ASE), an online method for diversifying training rollouts. By
clustering activation differences between correct and incorrect responses to the
same problem, ASE builds a structured bank of latent exploration vectors. Different vectors are then applied to subsequent rollouts to explore alternative reasoning
paths. On Qwen3-1.7B-Base and Qwen3-8B-Base trained on DAPO-Math-17k,
ASE improves average Mean@8 across six mathematical reasoning benchmarks
over GRPO by 4.87 and 1.87 percentage points, respectively, under the same rollout budget.

I NTRODUCTION

Reinforcement learning improves a model by repeatedly sampling behaviors, evaluating their outcomes with rewards, and increasing the probability of behaviors that receive higher rewards (Sutton
& Barto, 2018). Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) makes this process scalable
by using automated verifiers to assign outcome-based rewards to generated responses, such as mathematical solutions and programs (Le et al., 2022; Shao et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025). A fundamental
constraint, however, is that the policy can only learn from behaviors that appear in its sampled rollouts (Shao et al., 2024; Yue et al., 2025). Consequently, policy optimization is shaped by which
trajectories the rollout procedure makes available for learning, a dependence that creates an exploration bottleneck under a finite rollout budget. Standard GRPO independently samples multiple
trajectories from the same current policy (Shao et al., 2024). Increasing the number of rollouts provides more Monte Carlo samples, but leaves the underlying proposal distribution unchanged. As the
policy concentrates probability mass on a limited set of reasoning patterns, multiple samples may
repeatedly visit similar solution modes, reproduce the same intermediate steps, or make correlated
errors. Useful alternatives that occupy low-probability regions of the policy can therefore remain
rarely observed and receive little direct reward feedback. The bottleneck is thus not merely how
many trajectories are sampled, but how a fixed rollout budget is distributed across the policy’s
reasoning space.
Several approaches seek to use a finite RLVR rollout budget more effectively. Adaptive sampling
allocates more rollouts to difficult problems (Liao et al., 2025), while data filtering and curricula
prioritize more informative training problems (Yu et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025b). These methods change where sampling effort is spent, rather than how trajectories for the same problem are
generated. Adjusting decoding temperature increases token-level variation (Dang et al., 2026), but
