Abstract
When a user says “Keep it short”, how short should the LLM’s response be?
More generally, users and applications require different levels of instruction adherence, but prompting does not reliably provide fine-grained control over them. We therefore study “steerable language models” that provide fine-grained numerical control over the degree to which models follow general instructions specified in text.
However, existing approaches do not jointly offer fine-grained control, efficiency, and scalability across diverse instructions. To address this gap, we introduce DIAL (Distilled Instruction-Adherence Levels) to achieve all three properties. For fine-grained control, we perform classifier-free guidance (CFG) with a frozen LLM to provide training signals for on-policy distillation of instruction adherence at different levels.
For efficiency, we design a lightweight steering adapter that uses a classifier over the LLM’s hidden states to compute a closed-form steering edit.
The classifier adaptively sets the steering magnitude at each token based on the target adherence level,
while accounting for the geometry of the LLM’s hidden states to preserve response coherence.
For scalability, the classifier is text-conditioned, allowing a shared adapter to support new instructions without per-instruction fitting.
Training requires only instruction and request text, without labeled responses, external generators, or judges. Evaluation across multiple LLM families shows that DIAL achieves a competitive balance between concept expression and response fluency, with concept scores increasing monotonically across adherence levels.
We introduce SteerIF, an instruction-only dataset of $98,625$ concept, persona, verifiable, and descriptive instructions. When trained on SteerIF, DIAL provides fine-grained control over personas and, when combined with prompting, improves verifiable instruction following. In vLLM, the LLM steered by DIAL retains 96–99% of the base LLM throughput, with per-token inference latency approximately matching that of the base LLM.
1 Introduction
Figure 1: Steerable Language Models with DIAL and Applications.
(a) DIAL provides fine-grained control over instruction adherence, allowing users to choose among responses
generated at different adherence levels. (b) Illustrative examples of controlling adherence levels for general instructions with
DIAL in personalization, parental control, and adherence to company specifications.
Large language models (LLMs) are deployed across users and applications with different requirements for instruction-following [1].
Meeting these requirements involves choosing which instructions an LLM should follow and the appropriate
level of adherence for each instruction. Without fine-grained control over adherence levels, users may need to revise an instruction and regenerate responses repeatedly to achieve the desired level of adherence. As an illustrative example, Figure 1(a) shows a user asking for a shorter answer, but the LLM’s response is too short for the user.
Further prompting is then needed to obtain the desired response length, creating a frustrating user experience.
Ideally, users could view responses generated at different adherence levels and select one with the desired length.
More generally, fine-grained adherence control for different instructions would support a range of applications (Figure 1(b)). For personalization, a user could select the adherence level for writing-style or persona instructions according to their preferences [2, 3].
For parental control, parents could set the adherence level for child-safety instructions to help safeguard children [4].
A company could configure the adherence level for product-policy instructions to reflect its specifications [5, 6]. Motivated by these applications, we study “steerable language models” [7, 8] that provide fine-grained numerical control over adherence levels for general text instructions.
Existing approaches for controlling adherence levels in LLMs are not jointly fine-grained, efficient, and scalable across general instructions. The most straightforward approach is prompting, but it does not reliably provide fine-grained control over the adherence level [9, 10].
In contrast, classifier-free guidance (CFG) [11] enables fine-grained control by computing two next-token distributions: one conditioned on the instruction and one unconditioned. It then combines their log probabilities to achieve the desired adherence level [12, 13]. However, this requires two forward passes per generated token, increasing inference cost.
Activation steering provides a more efficient approach by editing the hidden states of a frozen LLM within a single inference pass [14, 15, 16, 17].
Adjustable steering strength offers a promising way to adapt an LLM at inference time [18].
However, conventional steering methods are not fine-grained because they are fitted to labeled examples that indicate whether an instruction is followed, without supervision at intermediate adherence levels.
Increasing steering strength therefore does not reliably increase adherence and can reduce fluency and coherence [19].
Moreover, these methods require fitting for each instruction, limiting scalability.
Text-conditioned steering methods learn to steer an LLM from instruction text and support instructions unseen during training [20, 21, 22].
However, they are trained on labeled responses without supervision at intermediate adherence levels, and flow-based variants add per-token inference overhead.
Contributions. We introduce DIAL (Distilled Instruction-Adherence Levels) to jointly achieve three key properties: fine-grained control, efficiency, and scalability.
For fine-grained control, we perform CFG on a frozen LLM to provide training signals for on-policy distillation of instruction adherence at different levels (Section 3.2).
For efficiency, we design a lightweight steering adapter [23] that uses a classifier over the LLM’s hidden states to compute a closed-form steering edit.
The classifier adaptively sets the steering magnitude at each token according to the target adherence level, while accounting for the geometry of the LLM’s hidden states to preserve the base LLM’s response coherence (Section 3.3).
For scalability, the classifier is text-conditioned, allowing
a shared steering adapter to support previously unseen instructions without per-instruction fitting.
Furthermore, CFG provides training signals without requiring labeled responses, external generators, or judges, allowing training to extend to new instruction types using only instruction and request text.
Empirical evaluation across multiple LLM families demonstrates the advantages of DIAL. When steering LLM generation toward concepts such as “Shakespeare,” DIAL achieves a better balance between concept expression and response fluency than existing methods.
Importantly, DIAL provides fine-grained control over concept expression, with the concept score increasing monotonically across the range of adherence levels.
To evaluate general instruction following, we construct SteerIF, an instruction-only dataset comprising $98,625$ instructions, including concept, persona, and verifiable instructions.
We train a shared steering adapter for each base LLM and evaluate it on persona instructions, such as “Be humorous,” and verifiable instructions, such as “Respond in JSON.”
Persona scores increase with the adherence level across LLM families, and combining steering with prompting improves verifiable instruction following.
In terms of efficiency, DIAL’s $20.6$ M-parameter steering adapter retains 96–99% of base-LLM throughput in vLLM [24] on Gemma-2-9B [25], while per-token inference latency approximately matches that of the base LLM.
2 Related Work
We review three lines of work on steering LLMs. Guided inference is fine-grained but incurs substantial inference overhead; activation steering is efficient, but neither fine-grained nor scalable; and text-conditioned control generalizes to unseen instructions, but lacks fine-grained control and requires labeled responses, limiting scalability. See Appendix A for details.
Guided Inference.
In diffusion models [26, 27, 28], guided inference controls how strongly a condition, such as a text prompt, influences the generated image. Given the prompt “a cat in the style of Van Gogh,” a low guidance level gives an image that only hints at the style, and a high level gives one that is unmistakably Van Gogh.
Classifier guidance pushes each sampling step along the gradient of a separately trained classifier [29], while classifier-free guidance (CFG) removes this classifier by using the diffusion model itself as an implicit classifier [11]. In LLMs, CFG combines the next-token predictions with and without the instruction [12, 13].
A low guidance (i.e., adherence) level weakens the instruction relative to prompting, and a high level strengthens it. Related contrastive inference methods for LLMs improve faithfulness, instruction following, and open-ended generation [30, 31, 32, 33].
Because every guidance level defines a next-token distribution for any instruction, guided inference is fine-grained. However, CFG is not efficient, because every generated token needs two forward passes. This motivates guidance distillation in diffusion models, which trains a single-pass student conditioned on the guidance level, thereby recovering CFG at multiple levels [34].
In autoregressive models, CFG has been distilled into the weights only at a single
level [35].
Unlike a diffusion model, an LLM takes only tokens and has no natural input for the guidance level. DIAL serves as a lightweight steering adapter that conditions the LLM on the adherence level, approximating CFG at multiple levels in a single pass without changing the LLM’s weights or prompt.
Activation Steering.
Activation steering offers a promising way to adapt an LLM cheaply at multiple levels [18].
It edits the hidden states of a frozen LLM in a single forward pass while leaving the weights unchanged, allowing the steering level to be set for each input at inference time. A common intervention is to use a steering vector obtained from contrastive hidden states [14, 15], which has been used to control truthfulness [16], refusal [17], and instruction following [36].
Classifier-guided steering methods project the hidden state onto the half-space of a trained classifier in closed form [37, 38, 39], or onto the constraint set given by a first-order condition on a learned nonlinear safety score [40]. Geometry-aware methods rotate the state rather than translate it, preserving the hidden state’s norm [41, 42, 43].
These interventions are efficient, and their steering level can be varied continuously. However, they are fitted to labeled examples that either follow the instruction or do not, with no training at intermediate levels of adherence, and are therefore not fine-grained. Increasing the steering level therefore does not reliably yield a corresponding increase in adherence, and high levels often reduce the output fluency and coherence [19].
Moreover, each intervention is fitted to a single instruction, such as one concept or behavior, so each new instruction requires a new intervention.
Text-Conditioned Control.
To avoid refitting for every instruction, text-conditioned methods train a single network that maps a text description to an intervention.
HyperSteer maps a concept description to a steering vector [20], FLAS integrates a description-conditioned flow [21], and UniSteer trains a text-conditioned flow-matching network that also covers personas and multi-instruction following [22].
Their training, however, suffers from the same two problems as activation steering.
They are fitted to labeled examples, here concept-aligned completions written by an external LLM or labeled datasets for each behavior, so scaling to a new kind of instruction requires collecting new data.
These examples also either follow the instruction or do not, so the methods are never trained for intermediate levels of adherence and are not fine-grained.
Persona Dosing partly addresses this for personas by fine-tuning FLAS on persona-labeled data and calibrating its flow time against measured persona expression [44], which requires measuring each persona, whereas DIAL learns its levels during training and needs no such measurement for a new instruction.
Moreover, flow-based methods integrate a flow over several steps per token, which adds latency and limits practicality.
To address these limitations, DIAL distills the frozen LLM’s own
CFG distributions at multiple adherence levels into a single steering adapter.
Since the teacher requires only instruction and request text, training can scale to new types of instructions without collecting labeled responses.
The adapter learns to approximate the CFG-guided distribution at each target level, enabling fine-grained control while keeping inference cost close to that of the base LLM.
DIAL thus provides a fine-grained, efficient, and scalable way to condition an LLM on
general instructions and adherence levels.
3 Method: DIAL (Distilled Instruction-Adherence Levels)
DIAL uses a text-conditioned steering adapter to control instruction adherence in a frozen LLM at different levels.
We first define adherence levels via CFG (Section 3.1), and then describe how on-policy distillation trains the adapter at different adherence levels (Section 3.2).
Finally, we introduce the adapter’s lightweight classifier-based architecture and closed-form steering edit (Section 3.3).
3.1 Instruction-Adherence Levels via Classifier-Free Guidance
Let $x$ denote a request (e.g., “How do vaccines work?”), $c$ an instruction (e.g., “Keep it short”), and $\alpha>0$ an adherence level.
Given a response prefix $y_{<t}$ , the frozen LLM yields two next-token distributions: $\pi_{\mathrm{base}}$ , conditioned on the request alone, and $\pi_{\mathrm{prompted}}$ , conditioned on both the request and the instruction.
For brevity, we write $\pi_{\cdot}(\cdot\mid y_{<t})$ and leave the conditioning on $x$ and $c$ implicit.
Following CFG [12, 13], we define the teacher distribution at level $\alpha$ as
 $\pi^{T}_{\alpha}(\cdot\mid y_{<t})\ \propto\ \pi_{\mathrm{base}}(\cdot\mid y_{<t})^{1-\alpha}\,\pi_{\mathrm{prompted}}(\cdot\mid y_{<t})^{\alpha}.$ 
(1)
For $\alpha=1$ , the teacher recovers $\pi_{\mathrm{prompted}}$ . For $0<\alpha<1$ , it interpolates toward $\pi_{\mathrm{base}}$ , weakening the instruction’s influence.
For $\alpha>1$ , it extrapolates beyond $\pi_{\mathrm{prompted}}$ , strengthening the instruction’s influence.
Thus, larger $\alpha$ corresponds to stronger instruction adherence, and
CFG provides a next-token distribution at each adherence level for any instruction text, without labeled responses, external generators, or judges.
The prompt format for each instruction type is given in Section B.4.
Figure 2: DIAL’s Training Pipeline.
Given a request $x$ and an instruction $c$ , we sample adherence levels. CFG defines the teacher distributions on prefixes sampled from the student (on-policy), and the frozen LLM steered by DIAL defines the student distributions. DIAL’s steering adapter is trained with a Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD) loss across adherence levels.
3.2 On-Policy Self-Distillation across Adherence Levels
Training across Adherence Levels.
We train DIAL’s steering adapter to match the CFG teacher distributions at different adherence levels.
The student $\pi^{S}_{\alpha}$ is the frozen LLM with generation steered by DIAL at layer $\ell$ .
The request $x$ is passed to the frozen LLM, while the instruction $c$ and adherence level $\alpha$ are passed to DIAL’s steering adapter.
At each prompt and generated position, the adapter replaces the residual hidden state $h_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{LLM}}}$ with an edited state $h^{\prime}_{t}$ .
As in guidance distillation in diffusion models [34], a single adapter is trained to match the CFG distributions across levels.
For each request–instruction pair, we sample $\alpha\sim\operatorname{LogUniform}[0.5,2]$ , a distribution that is symmetric in $\log\alpha$ about $\alpha=1$ , where the CFG teacher recovers the prompted distribution.
This range covers weaker and stronger instruction adherence than prompting. For $0.5\leq\alpha<1$ , the teacher weakens the instruction’s influence relative to prompting, providing fine-grained intermediate training signals between unprompted and prompted generation.
Beyond $\alpha=2$ , the CFG teacher’s coherence degrades (Section B.4), consistent with prior work [12].
We empirically found that sampling 24 adherence levels per instruction–request pair worked well for training DIAL across LLM families.
We also verify that sampling $\alpha$ is important for fine-grained instruction adherence levels, and that training on more diverse instructions works better than training longer (see Section D.4).
On-Policy Self-Distillation.
The student’s response distribution varies with adherence level and can differ from the prompted LLM’s.
We therefore use on-policy self-distillation [45, 46], sampling student responses and computing the CFG teacher distribution at each response prefix.
We minimize the Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD) between teacher and student distributions per response token:
 $\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{E}_{(x,c)\sim\mathcal{D},\,\alpha,\,y\sim\pi^{S}_{\alpha}}\!\left[\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t}\operatorname{JSD}\!\left(\pi^{T}_{\alpha}(\cdot\mid y_{<t}),\,\pi^{S}_{\alpha}(\cdot\mid y_{<t})\right)\right],$ 
(2)
where $\mathcal{D}$ is the distribution of training request–instruction pairs.
Both distributions are restricted to the union of the base, prompted, and student top-64 tokens.
We also add a regularization term to encourage stable training and generalization by penalizing large relative edits (Section B.4).
Our loss ablation in Section D.4 suggests that JSD performs best, with forward KL performing substantially worse and reverse KL
performing slightly worse.
We refer to Sections B.4 and B.5 for training details and pseudocode of the full on-policy training loop across adherence levels.
3.3 Lightweight and Adaptive Classifier-Based Steering Adapter
Instruction and Memory Conditioning.
We design the adapter to condition each steering edit on the instruction and the response generated so far.
We encode the instruction through the frozen LLM and cache its layer- $\ell$ hidden states $\mathcal{I}$ .
At each position, the current hidden state $h_{t}$ attends to $\mathcal{I}$ and a memory of the 16 most recent edited states, $\mathcal{M}_{t}=\{h^{\prime}_{t-16},\dots,h^{\prime}_{t-1}\}$ :
 $z_{t}=\operatorname{Attn}_{\ell}\!\left(\mathrm{query}=h_{t};[\mathcal{I};\mathcal{M}_{t}]\right),\qquad f_{t}=\operatorname{GELU}\!\left(\operatorname{LayerNorm}(\operatorname{Linear}(z_{t}))\right).$ 
(3)
Here, $\operatorname{Attn}_{\ell}$ reuses the frozen layer’s normalization and query, key, and value projections.
The current state is the query, while the instruction and memory states supply the keys and values.
Since these states come from the same LLM layer, the adapter does not require a separate text encoder for the instructions.
Attention combines instruction and memory states, followed by a linear map, LayerNorm, and GELU to produce the 512-dimensional feature $f_{t}$ .
In our experiments, a memory window of the 16 most recent states performed well while keeping attention cost low.
Adaptive Classifier-Based Steering.
From $f_{t}$ , the adapter predicts a unit direction $u_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{LLM}}}$ , a threshold $\tau_{t}(\alpha)\in\mathbb{R}$ , and a norm weight $\lambda_{t}(\alpha)\in[0,1]$ .
The direction and threshold define a linear classifier with half-space $\{h:\langle u_{t},h\rangle\geq\tau_{t}(\alpha)\}$ . Unlike classifiers fitted separately for each instruction, DIAL’s classifier is generated from instruction and memory information at each token.
Rather than linearly scaling a fixed steering edit, DIAL learns the threshold and norm weight as functions of $\alpha$ .
On-policy distillation provides rich supervision from the CFG teacher for learning steering magnitude and geometry at different adherence levels.
The classifier $(u_{t},\tau_{t}(\alpha))$ determines whether and how much to steer the current hidden state.
If $h_{t}$ lies outside the half-space, we project it onto the boundary with a minimum-norm step, following classifier-based steering [37, 38, 39].
We then adjust the projected state’s norm using the norm weight $\lambda_{t}(\alpha)$ while retaining its direction.
Both operations have closed-form expressions:
 $h^{P}_{t}=h_{t}+\big[\tau_{t}(\alpha)-\langle u_{t},h_{t}\rangle\big]_{+}\,u_{t},\qquad h^{\prime}_{t}=\Bigl[\lambda_{t}(\alpha)+\bigl(1-\lambda_{t}(\alpha)\bigr)\frac{\lVert h_{t}\rVert}{\lVert h^{P}_{t}\rVert}\Bigr]\,h^{P}_{t},$ 
(4)
where $[a]_{+}=\max(a,0)$ .
The gap $[\tau_{t}(\alpha)-\langle u_{t},h_{t}\rangle]_{+}$ sets the projection magnitude.
Thus, the edit depends on the current state as well as the generated classifier, rather than applying a fixed steering magnitude to every token.
If the state already lies inside the half-space, the edit leaves $h_{t}$ unchanged.
The norm weight lets the adapter interpolate between two steering geometries, which we visualize in Section B.2.
At $\lambda_{t}=1$ , the edited state is the minimum-norm projection $h^{\prime}_{t}=h^{P}_{t}$ [37, 38, 39].
At $\lambda_{t}=0$ , it preserves the original norm, $h^{\prime}_{t}=\lVert h_{t}\rVert\,h^{P}_{t}/\lVert h^{P}_{t}\rVert$ , giving a rotation in the span of $h_{t}$ and $u_{t}$ [41, 42, 43].
Intermediate values preserve the projected direction with norm $(1-\lambda_{t})\lVert h_{t}\rVert+\lambda_{t}\lVert h^{P}_{t}\rVert$ .
Learning this weight allows the geometry to adapt at each token.
We found that adapters trained for different LLM families learn different norm weights and edit magnitudes, suggesting that appropriate steering magnitudes and geometries depend on the base LLM (see Section D.3).
DIAL’s steering adapter has 20.6M trainable parameters on Gemma-2-9B (see Section B.1).
Efficient Inference.
During inference, instruction and memory keys and values can be cached, so each token requires only a
single-layer attention computation and a closed-form steering edit.
4 Experiments
We evaluate DIAL when the instruction is to express a concept, adopt a persona, or satisfy a verifiable requirement.
We also compare its training and inference efficiency against baselines.
Base LLMs.
We train DIAL’s adapters for instruction-tuned LLMs: Gemma-2-9B [25], Qwen2.5-7B [47], Llama-3.1-8B [48], Phi-4-14B [49], LFM2.5-1.2B [50], and Ministral-8B [51].
These cover six LLM families with 1.2–14B parameters, including Transformer and hybrid architectures.
Training Setup.
We train DIAL on $14,880$ concept descriptions from Concept16k [19].
We also construct SteerIF, an instruction-only dataset of $98,625$ instructions covering concepts, personas, and verifiable and descriptive requirements, given as single or composite instructions.
We train one shared adapter per LLM on SteerIF, denoted DIAL-IF.
For both datasets, we remove exact and near matches to held-out evaluation instructions to assess generalization.
We select a steering layer for each LLM and train its adapter at that layer.
See Section B.3 for more details on dataset construction and to Section B.4 for the steered layer chosen for each LLM.

Training and Baselines.
We train DIAL for one epoch on 14,880 concept descriptions from Concept16k [19].
We use FLAS’s released Gemma-2-9B checkpoint, trained on Concept16k with LLM-generated labeled responses [21], whereas DIAL uses only concept descriptions.
We compare with prompting, per-concept methods (LoReFT, LoRA, ReFT-r1, DiffMean, and SAE [19], SV + RePS [52], and S-PSR [53]), and text-conditioned methods (HyperSteer and FLAS).
We also include CFG [12, 13] for fine-grained control over concept expression, and prompting w/ level [54], which uses the same prompt as prompting but also asks the LLM to follow it at adherence level $\alpha$ , on a scale from 0 (ignore) to 2 (follow strongly).
See Section C.1 for implementation details.
Evaluation Protocol.
We evaluate on AxBench’s Concept500 (Held-In)
and FLAS’s 100 held-out concepts (Held-Out).
HyperSteer’s published Held-Out score uses its own held-out concepts.
Neither Concept500 nor the held-out concepts appear in DIAL’s training pool.
Following the AxBench protocol, we limit responses to 128 tokens and steer DIAL at layer 20 for Gemma-2-9B.
Per-concept methods select a steering level for each concept, while DIAL uses a fixed adherence level $\alpha$ across concepts.
FLAS controls concept expression through its flow time $T$ , with larger $T$ applying the learned flow for longer [21].
We use $T=2$ as in FLAS’s reported evaluation and vary $T$ in Figure 3.
Protocols, baselines, and further results are in Section C.1.
Evaluation Metrics.
Each response receives three scores from 0 to 2: concept expression, request following (AxBench’s instruction score), and fluency.
AxBench defines the overall score as the harmonic mean of the three scores.
We use gpt-4o-mini [55] as a judge to compare with prior work (Table 1) and the median of three judges for the curves in Figure 3.
Concept Steering Performance.
DIAL achieves overall scores of 1.059 and 1.036 on Held-In and Held-Out concepts, compared with 0.997 and 0.987 for FLAS (Table 1).
Combining DIAL with prompting raises the scores to 1.198 and 1.218, compared with prompting’s 1.049 and 1.060.
DIAL-IF, trained on SteerIF, steers persona and verifiable instructions and also performs well on AxBench, scoring 1.039 and 0.993.

Fine-Grained Control.
DIAL’s concept score increases smoothly with the adherence level (Figure 3).
From $\alpha=0.5$ to 2, it rises from 0.20 to 1.42, covering a range of 1.21 points compared with 0.66 for FLAS over flow times 0.5–2.
We also find that prompting w/ level does not provide fine-grained control, consistent with prior work [54].
At $\alpha=1.5$ , DIAL matches prompting’s concept score of 1.29 and surpasses it at $\alpha=2$ .
Combining prompting with DIAL further raises concept expression, with scores from 1.35 to 1.69.
The DIAL adapter thus covers intermediate expression levels below and above prompting, mirroring CFG.
Although trained only on single-turn responses, DIAL also shows potential for multi-turn steering.
Since an instruction given once in the context tends to lose its effect as the conversation grows [56], whereas DIAL steers the hidden state at every token during generation.
Section D.5 demonstrates this advantage in multi-turn settings.
Inference Efficiency.
We measure inference efficiency on Gemma-2-9B on an H200 at batch sizes of 1, 32, 128, and 256, generating 128 tokens per response.
Table 2 demonstrates that DIAL retains most of the frozen LLM’s inference efficiency.
In vLLM [24], it retains 96–99% of the base LLM’s throughput, compared with 41–86% for CFG and 84–86% for FLAS.
Its per-token inference latency approximately matches the base LLM’s.
Processing the instruction before generation increases time to first token and contributes to the remaining throughput overhead.
Similar trends hold for Hugging Face.
We refer to Section C.4 for absolute values and time to first token.

4.3 Verifiable Instruction Following
Evaluation Setup.
We evaluate DIAL-IF on 541 IFEval prompts, which contain verifiable instructions on format, lexical content, and language [9].
We note that all 25 instruction types in IFEval are covered by SteerIF, though matching instruction texts are removed and 376 of its requests occur in training.
The official checker assigns a binary pass/fail result to each instruction.
A prompt passes only when all its instructions are followed.
Unlike concept and persona expression scores, these binary checks do not measure intermediate adherence levels per response.
We compare prompt-level strict accuracy at four adherence levels ( $\alpha=0.5,1,1.5,2$ ).
Instruction Following Performance.
DIAL-IF alone generally improves instruction following as the adherence level increases (Table 4).
On Gemma-2-9B, accuracy rises from 9.6 without instructions to 53.8 at $\alpha=2$ .
At $\alpha=1$ , combining prompting with DIAL-IF improves over prompting on five of the six evaluated LLMs, while Qwen scores 71.9 compared with prompting’s 72.3.
At their best levels, CFG and prompt+DIAL-IF are within two points of each other on five of the six LLMs, and CFG is higher on Phi-4-14B (72.5 against 68.2), while CFG requires two LLM passes per token.
On Gemma, prompt+DIAL-IF reaches 76.9, compared with prompting’s 72.8.
Llama reaches 75.6, compared with 73.6.
On Phi-4-14B, LFM2.5, and Ministral-8B, it reaches 66.7, 85.6, and 65.1, versus 61.7, 83.4, and 53.4.
Thus, DIAL’s adapter carries information from unseen instruction texts.
We observe similar gains on the IFEval questions that do not appear in training (Section C.3).
Discussion.
We differentiate instructions that apply to the whole response, such as its length or format (global), from instructions that specify particular words or positions (local).
On Qwen2.5-7B, prompt+DIAL-IF at $\alpha=2$ follows local instructions better than prompting but global instructions worse, which helps explain why its overall accuracy stays below prompting’s on this LLM (Table 4).
Section C.3 covers evaluation details, category analysis, and unseen instruction types.
5 Conclusion
We introduce DIAL to build “steerable language models” that provide fine-grained control
over instruction-adherence levels while keeping the LLM frozen.
On-policy distillation from CFG trains a lightweight, text-conditioned steering adapter. DIAL provides fine-grained control over concept and persona
expression, and combining it with prompting can improve verifiable instruction following. Training is scalable, using only instruction text and a shared adapter without labelled responses or
per-instruction fitting. However, high adherence levels can still reduce response
coherence, and DIAL does not improve every verifiable instruction type. Future work should explore better
classifier architectures and training losses, and combine on-policy distillation with verifiable rewards or
judge feedback to filter training rollouts. Future work could also explore training at negative $\alpha$ , matching CFG with negative weights to steer
away from specific styles, topics, or vocabulary.

Steerable Language Models.
SteerLM conditions supervised finetuning on labelled attribute levels that users select at inference [7], and Conditional Language Policy learns policies conditioned on weights over multiple reward objectives [8].
Both finetune the whole LLM for numerical control over predefined attributes or objectives, using labelled data or a reward model.
DIAL keeps the LLM frozen and conditions a lightweight adapter on free-form instruction text, controlling adherence levels for new instructions without labelled responses, reward models, or retraining.
Guided Inference and Distillation.
Besides CFG for language models [12, 13], proxy-tuning combines the logits of several language models [58].
Our training also draws on distillation.
Context distillation trains a language model to behave as if it had been prompted without seeing the prompt [59, 60], and prompt baking internalizes a prompt into the language model’s weights [61].
In on-policy distillation (OPD), the student is trained on its own samples with token-level feedback from a teacher (typically forward KL, reverse KL, or JSD) [45, 46].
This approach has been used with a copy of the model that sees extra context as the teacher [62], and for self-distillation of a reasoning model given privileged context [63].
ExOPD distills a sequence-level geometric mixture [64], while DIAL’s mixture is defined separately at each token, so the two sequence distributions generally differ.
Guidance itself has also been distilled.
In diffusion, a student conditioned on the guidance weight learns every strength [34], and PDM supervises the conditional branch and the guided combination separately, after finding that matching only the combination corrupts the conditional branch [65].
Diversity-rewarded CFG distillation [35] distills CFG into all weights of an autoregressive music model at one fixed scale with a negative prompt, training on the student’s own samples, and adding a diversity reward because distillation alone reduces diversity.
DIAL uses the same ingredients, namely the language model as its own teacher with privileged context, on-policy rollouts, and a JSD loss that is an instance of GKD, but it distills every adherence level into one lightweight, text-conditioned adapter rather than one fixed scale into all weights.
Activation Steering.
Steering vectors can be extracted by contrasting activations on paired inputs [15] or the outputs of attention heads [16].
They can also be trained for each concept.
For instance, RePS trains them with a preference-style objective [52].
AxBench compares these steering methods against prompting and finds that it is difficult for them to beat prompting [19].
Several methods decide when to apply the intervention.
CAST turns a vector on only when it detects a condition [66], MAT-Steer learns per-token gates over a fixed set of attributes [67], and DSAS learns a gate for each layer [68].
StTP edits only the tokens whose projection falls below a certain boundary [69], and GCAD gates each token by its attention to system-prompt positions [70].
Instruction-vector steering instead moves the projection onto an instruction vector to its average value under instructed inputs [36].
Other methods learn directly from prompted behavior.
PSR trains a per-token gate on a fixed direction to imitate prompting [53], and we adopt its IFEval and persona protocols.
CDAS matches the output of an interchange intervention to the model’s output on an input that elicits the concept [71], and a system prompt alone can yield a difference-of-means vector [72].
Rather than fixing the strength, some methods sample it during training [52] or learn it for each concept [73].
In every case, the parameters are fitted to one concept, attribute, or instruction.
Classifier-guided and geometric edits. Projecting a hidden state onto the half-space of a probe with the smallest possible change has often been used in steering [37, 38, 39], where the probe is fitted for each attribute.
A similar projection under a state-dependent linear constraint is used in safe control [74].
SaP iterates toward a learned safety polytope [75], and BarrierSteer projects onto a first-order condition of a nonlinear safety score [40].
Other projections account for the norm of the hidden state.
These include Householder pseudo-rotation [41], angular steering in a fixed plane [42], spherical steering with a per-token confidence gate [43], selective steering [76], and Riemannian steps [77].
One method solves a minimum-displacement problem under a unit-norm constraint [78], and an angle–norm analysis finds that a modest norm increase lowers perplexity at a fixed angle [79].
Affine minimal interventions on distributions have closed-form solutions [80], and FishBack derives edits that minimize the off-target KL divergence under a pullback Fisher metric [81].
DIAL’s projection builds on these methods, but generates its half-space and norm weight from the instruction and level at every token, and learns both from the guided distributions.
Text-Conditioned Control.
HyperSteer maps a steering prompt to a single vector that is added at every position, and its best variant cross-attends to the base model’s residual stream [20].
FLAS learns a velocity field at one layer of Gemma-2 that is conditioned on the description and integrated over several steps, and cross-attends to the description encoded by the frozen model’s first layers.
It has about 255M trainable parameters and learns from concept-aligned completions, and on Gemma-2 2B its scaling curve crosses the prompting baseline only at 1,900–5,500 training concepts [21].
Persona Dosing fine-tunes FLAS on persona-labelled data and then calibrates its flow time against measured trait expression [44].
DIAL instead defines every level by the CFG teacher for any instruction.
UniSteer trains a text-conditioned flow with an external text encoder [22].
Text-to-LoRA, HyperDAS, and Doc-to-LoRA amortize other interventions over text [82, 83, 84].
DIAL is also text-conditioned, but outputs a half-space and a norm weight rather than a vector, velocity, or adapter, and learns from the frozen model’s guided distributions rather than completions.
Appendix B Implementation Details
B.1 Steering Adapter Architecture
Frozen and Trainable Components.
We apply DIAL at one layer $\ell$ of the frozen LLM.
The adapter reuses the layer’s input normalization and query, key, and value projections, but not its output projection.
We train the 512-dimensional feature layer, LayerNorm, prediction heads, and read-out paths.
The adapter’s feature computation at each token is given in Eq. 3.
Adherence-Level Conditioning.
We condition the threshold and norm weight on $\alpha$ through a learned shift to each base head:
 $\tau_{t}(\alpha)=\tau_{0}(f_{t})+\Delta_{t}(\alpha),\qquad\lambda_{t}(\alpha)=1-\sigma\bigl(r(f_{t})+\Delta^{R}_{t}(\alpha)+b_{R}\bigr).$ 
Here, $\tau_{0}$ and $r$ are linear maps, and
 $\Delta_{t}(\alpha)=\phi\bigl(\operatorname{stopgrad}(f_{t}),\log\alpha\bigr)-\phi\bigl(\operatorname{stopgrad}(f_{t}),0\bigr).$ 
The MLP $\phi$ has one 128-unit GELU hidden layer and a zero-initialized output layer.
A separate MLP defines $\Delta^{R}_{t}$ in the same way.
Both shifts are zero at $\alpha=1$ .
The bias $b_{R}=\operatorname{logit}(0.05)$ initializes the norm weight near 0.95, making the initial edit close to the projection geometry.
Attention and Memory.
The current state $h_{t}$ provides the attention query.
Instruction and memory states provide the keys and values.
We do not apply positional rotation to instruction keys.
Memory keys use the frozen layer’s rotary embedding relative to the current token position.
During inference, we cache instruction keys and values once per request.
A 16-entry ring buffer stores the memory keys and values of edited states.
During training, the memory instead uses the unedited states $h_{t-16},\dots,h_{t-1}$ from the student’s rollout.
These states are available from one LLM inference pass, allowing us to compute the adapter’s edits at all positions in parallel (Figure 7).
Training on edited memory states, which matches inference exactly, does not improve results (Section D.2).
Read-Out Paths and the Lighter Variant.
We add two read-out paths to DIAL and DIAL-IF.
A linear skip $Sz_{t}$ passes the attention context to the direction head.
The second path adds $W_{r}\operatorname{RMSNorm}(h_{t})$ to the feature layer before LayerNorm.
The resulting computations are
 $\displaystyle f_{t}=\operatorname{GELU}\!\left(\operatorname{LayerNorm}\!\left(W_{z}z_{t}+b_{z}+W_{r}\operatorname{RMSNorm}(h_{t})\right)\right),$ 
 $\displaystyle\widetilde{u}_{t}=W_{u}f_{t}+b_{u}+Sz_{t},\qquad u_{t}=\frac{\widetilde{u}_{t}}{\lVert\widetilde{u}_{t}\rVert}.$ 
Both paths are zero-initialized and use a $0.1\times$ learning-rate multiplier.
DIAL-IF-Lite omits them.
On Gemma-2-9B, DIAL and DIAL-IF have 20.6M trainable parameters, while DIAL-IF-Lite has 4.08M.
All variants keep the base LLM frozen throughout training and inference.
B.2 Geometry of the Steering Edit
Minimum-Norm Projection.
The adapter predicts $u_{t}$ , $\tau_{t}(\alpha)$ , and $\lambda_{t}(\alpha)$ from the current state and context, then holds them fixed while editing the state.
The classifier score is $s_{t}(v)=\langle u_{t},v\rangle-\tau_{t}(\alpha)$ .
The projection in Eq. 4 solves
 $\delta_{t}=\arg\min_{\delta}\ \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert\delta\rVert^{2}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\langle u_{t},h_{t}+\delta\rangle\geq\tau_{t}(\alpha),\qquad h^{P}_{t}=h_{t}+\delta_{t}.$ 
Because $u_{t}$ has unit norm, a state outside the half-space takes a minimum-norm step along $u_{t}$ with magnitude $\tau_{t}(\alpha)-\langle u_{t},h_{t}\rangle$ .
A state inside the half-space remains unchanged.
Norm Adjustment.
For nonzero $h_{t}$ and $h^{P}_{t}$ , the state with the projected direction and original norm is
 $h^{R}_{t}=\lVert h_{t}\rVert\,\frac{h^{P}_{t}}{\lVert h^{P}_{t}\rVert}.$ 
(5)
This preserves the original norm and rotates the state within $\operatorname{span}\{h_{t},u_{t}\}$ without a rotation matrix.
The edited state is the interpolation $h^{\prime}_{t}=(1-\lambda_{t})h^{R}_{t}+\lambda_{t}h^{P}_{t}$ , with norm $(1-\lambda_{t})\lVert h_{t}\rVert+\lambda_{t}\lVert h^{P}_{t}\rVert$ (Figure 5).
At $\lambda_{t}=1$ , the state lies in the classifier’s half-space.
Norm adjustment at $\lambda_{t}<1$ need not preserve that membership.
Neither property guarantees instruction adherence in the response.
The adapter generates a new classifier at each token and handles degenerate normalization cases numerically.
Figure 5: Geometry of the Steering Edit. The norm weight $\lambda_{t}$ interpolates between norm-preserving rotation ( $\lambda_{t}=0$ ) and minimum-norm projection ( $\lambda_{t}=1$ ).
B.3 Training Data
We use separate training runs for DIAL on Concept16k and DIAL-IF on SteerIF.
DIAL uses concept instructions, while DIAL-IF covers concept steering, persona steering, and verifiable instruction following.
Concept Instructions.
The concept-steering pool contains 14,880 descriptions from Concept16k.
We remove all exact-match and near-duplicate descriptions of Concept500 and FLAS’s 100 held-out concepts.
SteerIF Construction.
SteerIF contains 98,625 instruction texts in five categories (Table 8).
The dataset includes the same 14,880 concept descriptions and 584 one-sentence persona instructions.
Of these, 57 are author-generated, kept after filtering out the evaluation personas of [57] and related ones.
The other 527 are derived from persona adjectives: 510 occur only in TDA, 14 only in MWE, and 3 in both sources [85, 86].
We count shared adjectives once.
We convert each adjective into a one-sentence persona description using the generation prompt in [57].
We remove matches to held-out persona groups using WordNet relations [87], embedding similarity with all-mpnet-base-v2 [88], and a pairwise comparison with Phi-4-14B.
Single instructions specify verifiable or descriptive response requirements.
We collect 23,480 from IF_multi [10], 25,248 from VerInstruct [89], and 44 from the IFEvalG instruction registry [90].
VerInstruct covers 13 categories, including literary devices, morphology, item listing, grammatical structure, and multilingual output.
We also generate 2,942 instructions by writing alternative phrasings and wrappers for types represented by only one template in the original sources.
These author-generated variants retain the original response requirement.
We construct 31,109 composite instructions by combining two (85%) or three (15%) compatible single instructions.
Each combination contains at most one requirement on length, case, language, structure, or punctuation.
We also include 338 IFEval-type instructions.
SteerIF therefore contains only instruction text, without responses or LLM outputs in any category.
Request Pairing and Verification.
We pair verifiable and descriptive instructions with 16–32 requests drawn from 376 instruction-free IFEval questions [9, 36].
Concept and persona instructions use AxBench’s shared request corpus.
Code can verify 36,014 of the 82,823 verifiable and descriptive instructions.
The other 46,809, including all VerInstruct texts, receive supervision from the CFG teacher without a response checker.
All 25 IFEval instruction types appear in training, with at least 340 training instructions for every type in the dataset.

The analysis excludes concept and persona instructions.
Positive instructions account for 42.4% of this subset, negative instructions for 29.9%, and the remaining 27.7% specify lower, upper, range, or exact requirements.

B.4 Training Configuration
Concept16k Training.
For the AxBench experiments, we train DIAL on 14,880 Concept16k descriptions.
We train the Gemma-2-9B adapter from scratch for one epoch on H200 GPUs, using 4.06 GPU-hours.
The adapter edits layer 20, counting from 0 as in AxBench, and uses the architecture and read-out paths in Section B.1.
SteerIF Training.
In separate runs, we train DIAL-IF and DIAL-IF-Lite for one epoch on SteerIF.
Each adapter is shared across concept, persona, and verifiable instructions, and is also evaluated on AxBench.
Each adapter edits one layer near half the LLM’s depth, counting from 0: layer 20 of 42 for Gemma-2-9B, 13 of 28 for Qwen2.5-7B, 15 of 32 for Llama-3.1-8B, 19 of 40 for Phi-4-14B, 7 of 16 for LFM2.5-1.2B, and 15 of 36 for Ministral-8B.
We train both variants for each of these LLMs except Ministral-8B, for which we train only DIAL-IF, and both variants use the same layer.
On Gemma-2-9B, DIAL-IF has 20.6M parameters and DIAL-IF-Lite has 4.08M.
The variants differ in the read-out paths described in Section B.1 while sharing all other components.
On Gemma-2-9B, we train both variants from scratch with the same data, steps, and seed.

Adherence Levels and Rollouts.
We sample 24 adherence levels per instruction–request pair from $\operatorname{LogUniform}[0.5,2]$ .
Student rollouts use temperature 1 and generate at most 128–1,024 new tokens, depending on the instruction type.
Loss and Optimization.
We use JSD with $\beta=0.5$ over the union of the base, prompted, and student top-64 tokens.
We add the regularization term $0.1\,\mathbb{E}\,[\lVert h^{\prime}_{t}-h_{t}\rVert^{2}/\lVert h_{t}\rVert^{2}]$ .
This term is approximately 3% of the JSD at the end of training.
The optimizer uses a learning rate of $4\times 10^{-3}$ for every adapter.
Choice of Adherence Range.
The CFG teacher sweep on FLAS’s held-out concepts (Figures 3 and 10) supports this range.
At $\alpha=0.5$ , the teacher’s concept score is 0.23, close to the unprompted LLM’s 0.14.
Its fluency falls from 1.58 to 1.27 as $\alpha$ increases from 1 to 2.
Beyond $\alpha=2$ , its overall score falls from 1.32 to 1.23 at $\alpha=2.5$ .
We therefore use $\alpha=2$ as the upper bound when sampling adherence levels.
B.5 Pseudocode
We provide PyTorch-style pseudocode for the adapter, training, and inference.
We omit batching, padding, grouped-query attention, and numerical precision.
The modules norm, W_q, W_k, W_v, and attn reuse the frozen layer’s input normalization, projections, and attention scoring, including the attention scale and logit soft-cap at the selected LLM layer.
class DIAL(nn.Module):
 def __init__(self, layer, d_model, d_attn, d_feat=512, readout=True):
 self.layer = layer
 self.proj = nn.Linear(d_attn, d_feat)
 self.ln = nn.LayerNorm(d_feat)
 self.dir = nn.Linear(d_feat, d_model)
 self.dir_bias = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(d_model))
 self.thr = nn.Linear(d_feat, 1)
 self.nrm = nn.Linear(d_feat, 1)
 self.thr_lvl = MLP(d_feat + 1, 128, 1)
 self.nrm_lvl = MLP(d_feat + 1, 128, 1)
 self.readout = readout
 if readout:
 self.skip = nn.Linear(d_attn, d_model, bias=False)
 self.res = nn.Linear(d_model, d_feat, bias=False)
 def kv(self, s, pos=None):
 x = self.layer.norm(s)
 k, v = self.layer.W_k(x), self.layer.W_v(x)
 return (k if pos is None else rope(k, pos)), v
 def heads(self, h, ctx, alpha):
 z = self.proj(ctx)
 if self.readout:
 z = z + self.res(self.layer.norm(h))
 f = F.gelu(self.ln(z))
 d = self.dir(f) + self.dir_bias
 if self.readout:
 d = d + self.skip(ctx)
 u = d / d.norm(dim=-1, keepdim=True)
 fs = f.detach()
 shift = lambda m: m(cat([fs, log(alpha)])) - m(cat([fs, 0.0]))
 tau = self.thr(f) + shift(self.thr_lvl)
 lam = 1 - torch.sigmoid(self.nrm(f) + shift(self.nrm_lvl) + logit(0.05))
 return u, tau, lam
 def forward(self, h, Ik, Iv, Mk, Mv, alpha, pos):
 q = self.layer.W_q(self.layer.norm(h))
 ctx = self.layer.attn(q, keys=[Ik, Mk], values=[Iv, Mv],
 rope_q=pos)
 u, tau, lam = self.heads(h, ctx, alpha)
 return edit(h, u, tau, lam)
def edit(h, u, tau, lam):
 gap = tau - (u * h).sum(-1, keepdim=True)
 hP = h + gap.clamp(min=0) * u
 s = (lam + (1 - lam) * h.norm(dim=-1, keepdim=True)
 / hP.norm(dim=-1, keepdim=True))
 return s * hP

C.1 Controlling Concept Expression Levels
Evaluation Setup.
Following AxBench, we score the steered LLM based on how it expresses the concept, follows the original request, and preserves fluency from $0$ to $2$ [19].
We take the overall score as the harmonic mean of the three scores.
The adherence-level curves in Figure 3 use the median rating from Phi-4-14B, Qwen3.8-27B [95], and gpt-4o-mini for each criterion.
All methods generate ten $128$ -token responses per concept from AlpacaEval requests at temperature $1$ .
On Concept500, we score requests 5–9 after the published per-concept methods select a steering level on requests 0–4 [53].
The Held-In column of Table 1 is Concept500, and the Held-Out column is FLAS’s released $100$ -concept held-out split [21], on which we score all ten requests.
FLAS trains on 480 of the 500 Concept500 concepts; the other 20 are in its held-out split, so the two columns share 20 concepts, whereas DIAL trains on none of its concepts, and neither method trains on a held-out concept.
HyperSteer’s published held-out score uses its own unreleased $500$ -concept set [20], on which prompting scores $1.091$ .
Using $256$ -token responses and a different request draw, FLAS reports $1.185$ and $1.113$ on its held-in and held-out splits.
DIAL and FLAS use one fixed level across concepts.
We use $\alpha\in\{1,2\}$ for DIAL alone, $\alpha\in\{0.5,1\}$ with prompting, and $T=2$ for FLAS.
The published per-concept results select one of AxBench’s 14 steering factors (0.2–5.0) for each concept on requests 0–4.
LoReFT and LoRA steer four layers (12, 20, 31, and 39), while the other methods steer layer $20$ .
Method differences are paired by concept.
Main-table comparisons use $\alpha\leq 2$ , while the adherence-level curves show figure-only extrapolation above $2$ to examine behavior beyond the training range.

Baselines.
The prompting baseline uses AxBench’s concept-specific prompts written by gpt-4o-mini.
Per-concept methods train one steering method and select one level for each concept.
They include LoReFT, LoRA, ReFT-r1, DiffMean, and SAE from AxBench [19], SV + RePS [52], and S-PSR, the single-layer variant of PSR [53].
Text-conditioned methods use one network across concepts.
HyperSteer maps a concept description to a steering vector [20], while FLAS integrates a description-conditioned flow [21].
We use published results for the per-concept methods and HyperSteer.
We run FLAS with its released Gemma-2-9B checkpoint, original code, and three flow steps.
CFG applies the teacher in Eq. 1 at inference, requiring two inference passes per token as a reference.
We do not compare with Persona Dosing [44] or UniSteer [22], whose code and checkpoints are not released.
We also omit simpler level-conditioned edits, such as an additive steering vector or a difference-of-means vector between prompted and unprompted activations scaled by $\alpha$ .
Such single-vector edits underperform text-conditioned methods such as FLAS and reduce fluency at high strengths [19, 21]: DiffMean, a difference-of-means vector, scores 0.322 on Concept500, against 0.997 for FLAS (Table 1).

Levels out of 10 and verbal levels from “very slightly” to “extremely” behave the same way: from the lowest to the highest level, the concept score changes by only 0.10–0.21, compared with 1.18–1.40 for DIAL (Table 11).

Concept Steering Performance.
On Concept500 with requests 5–9, DIAL scores 1.059 at $\alpha{=}2$ , above the shown single-layer per-concept methods (S-PSR, 0.900), despite training on none of the 500 concepts.
Prompt+ DIAL is best on every split.
It raises AxBench’s prompt from 1.049 to 1.198 ( $+0.149$ ) on Concept500 and exceeds prompting by $+0.158$ on held-out concepts.
The gain reflects higher concept incorporation with nearly unchanged instruction following.
The level remains effective with prompting.
At $\alpha{=}0.5$ , prompt+DIAL lies between prompting and prompt+DIAL at $\alpha{=}1$ on every split.
On held-out concepts, it is $+0.051$ above prompting and $0.107$ below $\alpha{=}1$ .
Comparison with FLAS.
DIAL alone scores above FLAS’s released checkpoint by $+0.062$ on Concept500, on which FLAS was trained, and by $+0.049$ on held-out concepts, and it matches prompting on both.
DIAL requires no labelled responses, whereas FLAS is trained on completions written by gpt-4o-mini.
DIAL-IF, trained on 6.6 $\times$ more instructions of every kind, retains most of this performance.
It scores above FLAS on Concept500 ( $+0.042$ ), matches it on held-out concepts, and lies 0.02–0.04 below DIAL.
Prompt+DIAL-IF is within 0.03 of prompt+DIAL.
Fine-Grained Control.
On held-out concepts, the concept score of DIAL increases monotonically with the level, from 0.20 at $\alpha=0.5$ , close to the unsteered LLM’s 0.14, to 1.42 at $\alpha=2$ and 1.47 at $\alpha=2.5$ , beyond the training range.
At $\alpha=1.5$ , its concept score of 1.29 matches that of prompting (1.29).
Over levels 0.5–2, the concept score spans 1.21 points, compared with 0.66 for FLAS, whose weakest setting already reaches 0.60 and which remains at 1.18–1.26 from $T=1$ onward.
DIAL exceeds FLAS from $\alpha=1.375$ and is clearly higher on both metrics from $\alpha=1.625$ to 2, with a best overall score of 1.24 against 1.16.
At a matched concept score near 1.05, it preserves more of the request, with instruction scores of 1.96 against 1.88 and fluency scores of 1.46 against 1.33.
Prompting alone (1.31) achieves a higher overall score than DIAL alone, but the level remains effective on top of the prompt.
Prompt+DIAL spans 1.35–1.69 in concept score and 1.35–1.52 in overall score, above prompting and FLAS at every level.
CFG, the two-pass teacher, serves as an upper reference, but its overall score falls past $\alpha=1.625$ , reaching 1.23 at $\alpha=2.5$ , while DIAL remains at 1.24 at that same out-of-range adherence level (Figures 3 and 10).
Adherence Levels of Prompt + DIAL.
DIAL is trained with the request alone as input, and Prompt + DIAL composes it with prompting without further training.
In Prompt + DIAL, $\alpha$ sets the strength of the edit added to the prompted LLM rather than an absolute adherence level.
If the edit acts as the CFG shift $\alpha(\log\pi_{\mathrm{prompted}}-\log\pi_{\mathrm{base}})$ , adding it to the prompted LLM, which is at level 1, gives level $1+\alpha$ .
Prompt + DIAL at $\alpha=0.5$ , 1, and 1.5 reaches concept scores of 1.35, 1.58, and 1.67, close to CFG at levels 1.5, 2, and 2.5 (1.46, 1.58, and 1.64), with higher fluency from $\alpha=1$ (1.43 and 1.37 against 1.28 and 1.13).
On personas, Prompt + DIAL-IF increases with $\alpha$ but stays below CFG at level $1+\alpha$ (Figure 11).
DIAL alone thus covers levels below and above prompting, while Prompt + DIAL extends control above prompting.
Training DIAL with the prompt in its input would calibrate these levels, which we leave to future work.
Distillation Fidelity.
Figure 9 plots the score of DIAL against that of CFG, which provides its training signal, at the same adherence level.
The score of DIAL increases with $\alpha$ wherever that of CFG does, so the order of levels is preserved, but it stays below CFG at a matched level.
On concepts, DIAL reaches 82–90% of CFG’s concept score from $\alpha=1$ to 2 (0.93 against 1.13 at $\alpha=1$ ) and reaches CFG’s concept score at $\alpha=1$ at about $\alpha=1.25$ .
On personas, the gap at $\alpha=1$ ranges from 13.7 points on Qwen2.5-7B to 53.7 on Llama-3.1-8B, whose sampling we discuss in Section C.2.
The adherence level of DIAL is thus monotone but compressed relative to that of CFG.
A likely cause is capacity: CFG conditions every layer on the instruction, while DIAL edits one layer.
Editing four layers instead of one adds 0.057 on Gemma-2-2B (Section D.1), and closing the gap with larger adapters is future work.

C.2 Controlling Persona Expression Levels
Evaluation Setup.
We follow the protocol in [57] for seven released personas: evil, sycophantic, hallucinating, apathetic, humorous, impolite, and optimistic.
Neither these personas nor their near-synonyms appears in SteerIF.
Each persona has 20 questions.
Figure 4 uses four samples per question for every curve and baseline, and the tables use ten, except for Llama-3.1-8B, which uses four.
The figure’s four samples are the first four of the tables’ ten, so values at the same level differ slightly between them, e.g., 76.4 in Figure 4 and 75.8 in Table 16 for Gemma-2-9B with DIAL-IF at $\alpha{=}1$ .
We sample each response at temperature 1 for up to 1,000 new tokens.
Phi-4-14B, Qwen3.8-27B, and Llama-3.3-70B [48] score persona expression and coherence from 0 to 100 using the rubrics in [57].
Each score is the median of the three probability-weighted ratings.
We skip a judge’s refusal or off-format answer.
User prompting places the persona description before the question in the user message.
System prompting places it in the system message and is reported for Qwen2.5-7B, Phi-4-14B, and LFM2.5-1.2B.
Gemma-2 has no system role, and Ministral-8B’s chat template places a system prompt before the question in the user message, so system and user prompting coincide.
For DIAL-IF alone, the prompt contains only the question and the adapter receives the persona description.
Llama-3.1-8B uses 1,024-token persona rollouts during training, covering the 1,000-token evaluation horizon.

Sampling on Llama-3.1-8B.
Under full-vocabulary sampling at temperature 1, DIAL-IF produces low-coherence responses on Llama-3.1-8B (Table 15).
One possible cause is limited supervision of the low-probability tail.
The training loss uses the union of the top-64 token sets from the base, prompted, and student distributions, rather than the full vocabulary.
This may leave the tail poorly supervised and reduce coherence when inference samples from the full distribution.
At $\alpha=1$ , 41.6% of DIAL-IF-only responses are degenerate and 36.1% reach the 1,000-token limit, compared with 5.2% and 2.5% for prompting.
Restricting sampling to the 64 most likely tokens removes the observed degeneration.
We therefore use top-64 sampling for every Llama-3.1-8B persona method, including prompting, CFG, and no instruction.
The other five LLMs have degeneration rates of at most 0.1% under full-vocabulary sampling.

C.3 Verifiable Instruction Following
Evaluation Setup.
IFEval uses all 541 prompts with the official checker [9].
For DIAL-IF alone, the instruction is removed from the prompt [36] and passed to the adapter.
For prompt+DIAL-IF, the instruction remains in the prompt and is also passed to the adapter.
Table 23 reports instruction-level strict accuracy, pooled over all instructions in a benchmark or within a Global or Local block.
The instruction-free versions of 376 IFEval questions occur in the training request pool.
The other 165 requests contain one instruction each and are easier, with prompting at 75–96%.
On them, prompt+DIAL-IF at $\alpha=1$ changes accuracy over prompting by +5.5, $-1.2$ , +5.5, +3.0, $-1.8$ , and +11.5 points on Gemma-2-9B, Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Phi-4-14B, LFM2.5-1.2B, and Ministral-8B, similar to the gains on all prompts.
IFBench uses 219 of its 300 prompts after removing prompts whose verifiable instructions cannot be located and prompts whose instruction types duplicate or closely match a training type.
We use its strict checker [10].
Coherence is evaluated separately from checker-based accuracy.
We do not split the prompts into validation and held-out sets, because no adherence level is selected on the test prompts: $\alpha$ is configurable at test time, every result uses a fixed $\alpha$ , and the tables report each level.
Table 4 in the main text gives prompt-level IFEval accuracy for every base LLM with DIAL-IF, and Table 22 gives it for DIAL-IF-Lite with the no-instruction baseline.

DIAL-IF-Lite with Prompting.
On IFEval, prompt+DIAL-IF-Lite raises prompt-level strict accuracy over prompting on all five LLMs: from 72.8 to 77.6 on Gemma and from 61.7 to 69.7 on Phi-4-14B, and from 73.6 to 77.1 on Llama, from 72.3 to 73.6 on Qwen, and from 83.4 to 84.3 on LFM2.5.
DIAL-IF-Lite without Prompting.
Without the instruction in the prompt, DIAL-IF-Lite at $\alpha=2$ raises IFEval accuracy from about 10 to 43–55 for every LLM, more than half of the gain obtained by prompting.
Verifiable Instruction Categories.
Without the instruction in the prompt, DIAL-IF raises Gemma’s accuracy on Global verifiable instructions from 17.3 to 73.1, close to prompting’s 81.1, and exceeds prompting on letter-case instructions.
On Local verifiable instructions, it reaches 48.5 against prompting’s 78.6, and it fails when an exact word must appear at an exact position (10.5).
On top of the prompt at $\alpha{=}0.5$ , DIAL-IF raises accuracy over all 834 IFEval instructions from 80.2 to 83.7, Global accuracy from 81.1 to 84.6, and Local accuracy from 78.6 to 82.0.
It improves every category except language, where it ties at 93.5, and word position, where accuracy falls from 89.5 to 86.8 (Table 23).
The level determines which kind of verifiable instruction is favored.
From $\alpha{=}0.5$ to 2, Global accuracy rises from 84.6 to 88.3 while Local accuracy falls from 82.0 to 77.6, so the total remains near 84.
On Qwen, prompt+DIAL-IF scores slightly above prompting only at $\alpha{=}1$ (80.2 against 79.6) and below it at $\alpha{=}0.5$ and 2 (79.4 and 78.1), and steering alone reaches 71.1 on Global verifiable instructions against prompting’s 82.0.
On Llama, prompt+DIAL-IF raises accuracy from 81.1 to 84.9 at $\alpha{=}0.5$ , on Phi-4-14B from 71.8 to 76.3 at $\alpha{=}1$ , and on LFM2.5 from 88.1 to 89.7 at both levels, and on Ministral-8B from 63.2 to 73.4 at $\alpha{=}1$ (Tables 23 and 25), showing that the best adherence level depends on the LLM.
CFG on IFEval.
Table 24 reports CFG, which provides the training signal of DIAL-IF.
At $\alpha=1$ , CFG reduces to prompting; the remaining differences of at most 2.4 points come from the two inference implementations.
At $\alpha=1.5$ and 2, CFG improves over prompting on Gemma-2-9B, Phi-4-14B, and Ministral-8B (up to 78.0, 72.5, and 67.1 against 72.8, 61.7, and 53.4), by 3.5 points on Llama-3.1-8B, and not on Qwen2.5-7B or LFM2.5-1.2B.
Verifiable instructions are checked as pass or fail, so strengthening an instruction beyond prompting brings limited gains, which also bounds what distilling CFG can add.
At matched levels, where prompt+DIAL-IF at $\alpha$ corresponds to CFG at $1+\alpha$ (Section C.1), prompt+DIAL-IF at $\alpha=1$ is within 0.4 points of CFG at $\alpha=2$ or above it on four LLMs (76.9, 71.9, 75.6, and 85.6 against 76.2, 72.3, 75.4, and 81.1), and below it on Phi-4-14B (66.7 against 72.5) and Ministral-8B (65.1 against 67.1), while CFG requires two LLM passes per token (Table 2).

C.4 Training and Inference Cost
Evaluation Setup.
All timings use Gemma-2-9B on an H200 GPU, 128 new tokens per request with end-of-sequence ignored, sampling at temperature 1, and prompts from FLAS’s evaluation set with one concept per row.
Training cost is the GPU time of each recipe (Table 3).
We measure HyperSteer and FLAS by retraining each with its released recipe.
vLLM serving runs every method in one session with continuous batching.
The steering methods run inside the engine’s CUDA graphs.
CFG evaluates the two contexts of a request as a pair of sequences in the same batch.
FLAS is our port of its released checkpoint (three flow steps at layer 20, flow time 2), with each concept encoded once before serving.
For a fair comparison we optimized this port as far as DIAL’s own path: its flow runs on vLLM’s attention kernels inside the same CUDA graphs.
Batched Hugging Face inference runs every method in one process.
In both settings, we interleave methods, run untimed warm-ups, and take every ratio against the base LLM measured in the same session.
CFG evaluates both contexts of each request in one batch of twice the rows, and FLAS uses its released checkpoint and generation loop.
DIAL runs at $\alpha{=}2$ throughout, the fixed adherence level used in the main comparison.
Inference Efficiency.
In vLLM, the per-token inference time of DIAL matches that of the base LLM at every batch size, and DIAL retains 96–99% of the base throughput, compared with 41–86% for CFG and 84–86% for FLAS.
The per-token inference time of FLAS is 12–19% slower even though we also run it inside vLLM’s CUDA graphs.
The first token of DIAL arrives later than that of the base LLM ( $\times$ 1.17–1.45 from batch size 32), because it edits every prompt position, but earlier than those of FLAS ( $\times$ 1.31–1.51) and CFG ( $\times$ 2.34–2.62).
In batched Hugging Face inference, DIAL retains 95–99% of the base throughput, compared with 77–91% for FLAS, and CFG runs out of memory at batch size 256 (Tables 3, 2 and 30).
Training Scalability.
Training DIAL requires $4.65\times$ fewer GPU-hours than FLAS and $25\times$ fewer than HyperSteer, with $12.4\times$ fewer parameters than FLAS and without its 72 gpt-4o-mini completions per concept.
A second and third epoch do not improve performance (Table 43).
On SteerIF, which has 6.6 $\times$ more instructions than Concept16k and longer responses, training DIAL-IF takes 99.5, 58.7, 72.1, 150.3, 30.4, and 65.8 GPU-hours on Gemma-2-9B, Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Phi-4-14B, LFM2.5-1.2B, and Ministral-8B, and DIAL-IF-Lite takes 93.5 on Gemma-2-9B.
Tables 30 and 31 detail the costs summarized in Tables 3 and 2: vLLM and batched Hugging Face serving with absolute values, separate prefill and per-token inference measurements, and time to first token.

Appendix D Ablations
D.1 Steering Layers
Ablating Steering Layers.
Table 34 reports DIAL trained separately at several layers of Gemma-2-9B and Gemma-2-2B and scored at $\alpha{=}2$ with Phi-4-14B.
Both score best at their default half-depth layer (layer 20 of 42 and layer 12 of 26).
On 9B, layers 16 and 20 tie on every split, layer 12 loses 0.069 on Concept500, and layer 30 loses 0.499.
On 2B, every deeper layer loses (0.091 at layer 16, 0.269 at layer 20).
On 2B, the training JSD follows the same order.
On 9B, layers 12–20 fit the teacher similarly (JSD 0.055–0.059), and only layer 30 fits clearly worse (0.096).
On 2B, FLAS’s released 2B checkpoint scores above DIAL at every layer under this judge (1.090 against 0.907 on Concept500).
Steering Multiple Layers.
Editing four consecutive 2B layers (12–15) instead of one adds 0.057 on Concept500, whereas eight layers (12–19) add nothing over four, and neither reaches FLAS-2B except on the held-out concepts, where four layers tie it ( $-0.031$ ).

Design Choices.
We use a memory of the 16 most recent states, which performs well while keeping attention cost low.
Future work can use richer aggregations over the generated states, such as the maximum of rolling attention-weighted means over fixed-width windows [96], and test whether longer contexts need a longer memory.
We also tried pooling the instruction states into a single embedding and found that it performed worse than attention over all instruction tokens.
Attending over every token preserves word-level detail that a single vector compresses away, which matters for local instructions that name specific words and for long instructions.
The half-space parameterization edits a state only when it lies outside the half-space and scales the edit with the gap, so low adherence levels edit fewer tokens and by smaller amounts: at $\alpha{=}0.5$ , DIAL-IF edits 12–67% of tokens, against 77–99% at $\alpha{=}2$ (Table 42).
A directly predicted edit would move every token.
We test this below.
The projection onto the half-space boundary also gives the norm weight a target, between a minimal-norm projection and a norm-preserving rotation.
Training on Edited Memory States.
During training, the memory holds the unedited states of the student’s rollout, while at inference it holds the adapter’s edited states (Section B.1).
To match inference, we also train with a second pass in which the memory holds the edits of the first pass.
This doubles the training passes and does not improve results (Table 39): the overall score falls from 1.134 to 0.939 for DIAL at $\alpha{=}1$ and changes by at most 0.03 for the other rows.
A second training seed shows the same pattern.
Replacing Classifier-Based Steering with Direct Edits.
This variant changes only what the adapter outputs.
It keeps the attention over the instruction and memory states, the read-out paths, the adherence-level conditioning, the objective, the data, and the training seed.
Instead of a direction, threshold, and norm weight for the closed-form edit, its head predicts the edit vector $\Delta_{t}$ itself and applies $h^{\prime}_{t}=h_{t}+\Delta_{t}$ at every token.
The direct edit scores lower on all four rows (Table 40), most for prompt+DIAL at $\alpha{=}0.5$ (1.409 against 1.558), and the loss comes from concept expression.
Its edit size grows little with the adherence level ( $\|\Delta_{t}\|/\|h_{t}\|$ from 0.090 at $\alpha{=}0.5$ to 0.137 at $\alpha{=}2$ , against 0.034 to 0.128 for DIAL), so at low levels it moves every token further without expressing the concept more strongly.

D.3 Steering Geometry
Learned Norm Weight.
Figure 12 shows that the learned norm weight differs across LLMs.
We divide the single training epoch into equal intervals and average $\lambda_{t}$ over the positions in each interval at the sampled adherence levels.
In the last interval, 54% of Gemma-2-9B positions, 61% of Llama-3.1-8B positions, 44% of Qwen2.5-7B positions, 60% of Phi-4-14B positions, 57% of LFM2.5-1.2B positions, and 70% of Ministral-8B positions lie outside the half-space and are edited.
On all six LLMs, $\lambda_{t}$ moves from its initial value of 0.95 within the first interval.
It reaches 0.02 for Gemma-2-9B at layer 20, 0.22 for Llama-3.1-8B at layer 15, 0.33 for Phi-4-14B at layer 19, 0.44 for Qwen2.5-7B at layer 13, 0.51 for LFM2.5-1.2B at layer 7, and 0.25 for Ministral-8B at layer 15.
The Gemma-2-9B edit is close to norm-preserving, while the other five retain more of the projection’s norm change.
Figure 12: Learned Norm Weight across LLMs. $\lambda_{t}$ converges to a different value for each LLM during training.
The dotted line shows its initial value of 0.95.
Fixed Steering Geometry.
We train LFM2.5-1.2B adapters that differ only in the norm weight: learned, fixed to rotation ( $\lambda{=}0$ ), or fixed to projection ( $\lambda{=}1$ ), and evaluate persona steering with the same three-judge jury (Table 41).
Relative to the learned norm weight, fixed rotation lowers the persona score at every adherence level, and at a matched persona score its coherence is up to 10 points lower.
Fixed projection is more coherent at the same $\alpha$ but expresses the persona less, scoring 3–5 points lower without prompting.
At a matched persona score, projection and the learned norm weight reach similar coherence.
The learned norm weight therefore covers the widest range of persona expression levels without reducing coherence.

Edit Analysis.
To see how the adapter realizes each adherence level, we record every edited token during persona steering without prompting on all six LLMs, decoding greedily for up to 256 tokens (Table 42).
From $\alpha{=}0.5$ to 2, the total intervention grows 5–13 times through both a larger share of edited tokens and larger edits, with the balance depending on the LLM: LFM2.5-1.2B and Qwen2.5-7B mainly edit more tokens, Llama-3.1-8B, Phi-4-14B, and Ministral-8B mainly edit more strongly, and Gemma-2-9B does both.
On five of the six LLMs, the learned norm weight moves from projection at $\alpha{=}0.5$ (mean $\lambda$ 0.59–0.96) to rotation at $\alpha{=}2$ (mean $\lambda$ at most 0.01).
Gemma-2-9B rotates at every level, consistent with its learned norm weight of 0.02 (Figure 12).

D.4 Training
Training Ablations.
Table 43 varies the training signal, the number of epochs, and the size of the training pool.
Supervising only $\alpha{=}1$ gives the adherence-level shift no training signal, so the adapter ignores $\alpha$ : its concept score on the held-out concepts stays at 0.95 for every $\alpha$ from 0.5 to 2, whereas DIAL’s rises from 0.25 to 1.58, and its overall score at $\alpha{=}2$ falls from 1.408 to 1.039.
A second and third epoch change the overall score by at most 0.06 without a consistent gain.
At equal compute, 100 concepts trained for 149 epochs score 1.137 at $\alpha{=}2$ on Concept500, against 1.408–1.463 for 1,000 or more concepts, so the number of distinct instructions matters more than repeated passes.
The equal-compute run on all 14,880 concepts matches the main run within 0.033.

D.5 Multi-Turn Conversations
Multi-Turn Conversations.
An instruction given once in the prompt drifts over a conversation, as attention to it decays with the length of the exchange [56, 97].
DIAL instead steers the hidden state at every token.
We test whether steering persists over a conversation.
For each held-out concept, the 10 AxBench requests form one 10-turn conversation, and every response stays in the context.
The concept is specified once: prompting places the steering prompt in the first user turn, and DIAL steers every token at $\alpha{=}2$ .
We also compare with DIAL-LoRA, a rank-8 LoRA [98] trained with the same CFG distillation signal as DIAL, which reads the adherence level from an attribute line “{concept}:{ $\alpha$ }” at the start of its first response [7].
DIAL-LoRA is also an instance of our training pipeline: it applies the same on-policy distillation across adherence levels to a LoRA student instead of a steering adapter.
The two students are complementary, and a LoRA and DIAL’s steering adapter could be combined in one LLM, which we leave to future work.
Scores are the mean of the Phi-4-14B and Qwen3.8-27B judges.
In the first turn, all three methods express the concept, and DIAL-LoRA scores highest (Figure 13): its concept score is 1.62, against 1.55 for DIAL and 1.44 for prompting.
In later turns, DIAL-LoRA has no attribute line before its response and loses the concept from turn 2 (0.46), while prompting falls to 0.64.
By turn 10, both reach 0.14 and 0.20, close to no steering (0.16).
By steering every token, DIAL keeps 1.13 at turn 2 and 0.69 at turn 10, with an overall score of 0.75 at turn 10 against 0.25 for prompting and 0.16 for DIAL-LoRA.
DIAL still weakens over the conversation: over turns 2–10, it scores 0.65 below the same requests asked alone.
Neither DIAL nor DIAL-LoRA is trained on multi-turn conversations or long contexts, as both learn only from single-turn responses, yet DIAL generalizes better to later turns.
Training on long contexts reduces this drift.
We prepend 1–4 earlier, unrelated conversation turns of 200–700 tokens to half of the training requests and keep the architecture, objective, and data otherwise unchanged.
This variant keeps an overall score between 1.33 and 1.49 over the ten turns, against 0.75 for DIAL at turn 10 (Figure 13).
Its training takes 12.0 GPU-hours, against 4.06 for DIAL.
DIAL-LoRA with the same long-context training still loses the concept from turn 2 when its attribute line is given only in the first turn, with an overall score of 0.12 at turn 10.
We also report the setting in which every method restates its condition in every turn: prompting places the steering prompt in every user turn, Prompt+DIAL adds DIAL to it, and DIAL-LoRA places its attribute line at the start of every response.
Prompt+DIAL scores highest at every turn (Figure 13b): at turn 10, its concept score is 1.62, against 1.35 for DIAL-LoRA and 1.27 for prompting, and its overall score is 1.47, against 1.36 and 1.34.
With the condition given once, DIAL keeps the most concept; when the condition is restated in every turn, prompt+DIAL keeps the most.
In both settings, steering the hidden state at every token keeps the concept better than relying on the concept in the prompt alone.
We hypothesize that this effect is even stronger in longer contexts and conversations with more turns.

DIAL and DIAL-LoRA with long-context training are trained with earlier conversation turns prepended to half of their training requests.

Appendix E Ethics Statement
Dual Use.
The ability to strengthen instruction adherence can support beneficial applications but can also make harmful behavior easier to elicit.
Our persona evaluation includes evil, sycophantic, and hallucinating personas, following the evaluation protocol in [57].
DIAL-IF increases their persona scores as it does for other personas (Table 16).
At $\alpha>1$ , steering can strengthen an instruction beyond prompting, as also observed with amplified system prompts [13].
Steering can partly bypass refusal behavior: Phi-4-14B refuses the evil persona under prompting (persona score 0.0) and keeps refusing when the persona is also in the prompt (at most 0.4 with prompt+DIAL-IF), but DIAL-IF alone reaches 24.4 at $\alpha{=}2$ (Table 19).
We have not evaluated harmful-request benchmarks such as StrongREJECT [99] or HarmBench [100] across adherence levels.
In steering-only use, the instruction is not in the prompt, so prompt-only filters would not inspect it.
Deployments should therefore validate the instruction text, control access to steering settings, and retain output filtering.
Reliability.
DIAL is not a verified safety mechanism.
Instruction adherence is learned rather than guaranteed, monotonicity across levels is not enforced, and steering fails on some verifiable instruction types (Section 4.3).
Our evaluation covers six LLMs with 1.2–14B parameters, while deployment requires broader evaluation and adversarial testing on larger LLMs.
Our benchmarks use single-turn prompts with short responses, apart from the multi-turn analysis in Section D.5, and do not cover long documents or real user interactions.
